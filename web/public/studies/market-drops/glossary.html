Glossary

The terms used on these pages, in plain words.

90% day
A day on which 90% or more of volume was in rising stocks (up day) or falling stocks (down day).
Advance / decline
The number of stocks that rose on a day against the number that fell. “Share of advancers” here = rising stocks ÷ (rising + falling), in this study's universe of about 660 large stocks.
Base rate
How often something happened across all past cases, before looking at the details of the present one.
Bear market
By convention a fall of 20% or more in an index from its high. On these pages, “the long bears” means 1973–74, 2000–02 and 2007–09.
Breadth
How many stocks take part in a move. Measured here by the share of stocks above their 50-day or 200-day average, at 52-week lows, or advancing on the day.
Breadth thrust
A sudden, broad surge in the share of rising stocks after a decline. Several named definitions exist (Zweig, Whaley, Deemer); this study uses its own stand-ins computed on its stock universe.
CAPE (Shiller P/E)
Index price divided by the average of the last ten years of inflation-adjusted earnings. A slow valuation gauge.
Capitulation
A burst of forced or panicked selling, usually on very high volume with nearly all stocks falling.
Close location
Where the close sits in the day's range: 0 at the low, 1 at the high.
Correction
By convention a fall of 10% to 20% from a high.
Credit spread
The extra yield investors demand to hold corporate bonds rather than Treasuries. It widens when lenders fear defaults. The Baa spread used here is Moody's Baa corporate yield minus the 10-year Treasury yield.
Death cross / golden cross
The 50-day average crossing below / above the 200-day average.
Drop
On these pages: the index closes at least 10% below its highest close of the past 252 sessions. It runs from that high (the peak) to the lowest close before a new 52-week high (the low).
EMA / SMA
Exponential and simple moving averages. The charts show the 9- and 20-day EMA and the 50- and 200-day SMA; weekly charts show 10- and 40-week SMAs.
Equal-weight index
An index in which every stock counts the same, so it shows what the average stock is doing. RSP is the equal-weight S&P 500 fund.
Failed rally
A rise of 5% or more from a closing low inside a drop, whose starting low is later broken on a closing basis.
Fed pivot
A change in the Federal Reserve's direction or stated intention, from tightening to pausing or easing.
Follow-through day
From the fourth to about the fifteenth session of an attempted rally, a gain of 1.25% or more in the index on higher volume than the day before, without the low having been broken. A term from William O'Neil and Investor's Business Daily.
Forward P/E
Index price divided by analysts' expected earnings for the next twelve months.
Gap
The difference between today's open and yesterday's close. A gap up is “sold” if the day closes below its open.
Margin debt
Money borrowed from brokers against securities. Reported monthly by FINRA and quarterly in the Fed's accounts.
Momentum crash
The pattern, documented by Daniel and Moskowitz, that at market lows the stocks that fell most rebound far more than the stocks that held up.
Overnight / session
Overnight = previous close to today's open. Session = today's open to today's close.
Policy rate
The Federal Reserve's target for the overnight federal funds rate (the middle of the target range since 2008).
Relative strength line
A stock's or sector's price divided by the index's. Rising = outperforming. Its low inside a drop is the day the group stopped underperforming.
Retest
After a low and a first rally, a fall back to near the low. Here: a close within 3% of the low between 5 and 60 sessions after it.
Session
One trading day. About 21 sessions make a month and 252 a year.
Survivorship bias
The distortion from studying only companies that still exist: the ones that failed are missing, so the past looks better than it was.
The turn
The rally that starts at the final low of a drop.
V-bottom / W-bottom
A low followed by an uninterrupted recovery (V), or by a rally, a retest near the low and then a recovery (W).
VIX
The options market's estimate of S&P 500 volatility over the next 30 days, as an annualised percentage. The 3-month VIX is the same for the next three months; when VIX is above it, the curve is “inverted”.
Yield curve
The difference between long-term and short-term Treasury yields (here 10-year minus 2-year, or minus 3-month). Negative = inverted.
