What the score says
  • By this score the backdrop at the October 2026 high was closest to Sep 2018, then Jan 2018 and Mar 2000. The chart-watchers' comparison with 2018 has a basis in the macro numbers: a Fed raising rates, low unemployment, calm credit, strong earnings, an index well above its 200-day average.
  • The same score puts March 2000 at rank 3. What 2026 shares with 2000 and not with 2018: a valuation near the top of its history (CAPE 41 against 33 in 2018 and 43 in 2000), margin loans growing +31% in a year, and oil and inflation rising rather than falling.
  • The largest single difference from 2018 is inflation's direction: 3.7% and rising at the 2026 high, 2.6% and about to fall in 2018. That bears on whether the Fed can stop.

Earnings are strong and still being revised up; the fall so far is all valuation

S&P 500 profits grew about 33% in the third quarter of 2026 and analysts are still raising their estimates. Since 30 September the index price has fallen 6.2% while the earnings estimate for the next twelve months has risen 4.4%. What changed is the price investors pay for those earnings.

Working from FactSet’s figures, the forward P/E was about 19.4 at the 6 October record close and about 16.5 at the 20 November close. That is a fall of roughly 15% in the multiple in 33 sessions with no fall in estimates.

Three details behind the headline

  • The growth is concentrated. Energy earnings rose 151% because oil averaged $85.68 in the quarter against $64.97 a year earlier. Technology earnings rose 69%, and 133% in semiconductors. Without semiconductors, technology grew 30%. Financials grew 6.4%.
  • The largest companies now grow more slowly than the rest. The “Magnificent 7” grew earnings 27.0% in the quarter against about 33–35% for the other 493 companies, the first such gap since Q4 2022. FactSet expects the same in Q4 (22.6% against 30.8%). Meta booked a $9.9bn legal charge and Alphabet an $8.7bn loss on investments.
  • The estimates flatten in mid-2027. Expected growth drops to 3.6% in the second quarter of 2027, when this year’s energy and chip profits become the comparison.

A slower-moving measure tells a less comfortable story. The CAPE (price divided by the ten-year average of inflation-adjusted earnings) was 40.6 at the October peak and is 37.9 now, against 32.6 at the September 2018 peak and 43.2 at the 2000 peak, by the study’s macro sheet. The Fed’s November report says equity valuations “remained elevated” and that the extra return investors receive for holding stocks over bonds is near the low end of its historical range Fed Financial Stability Report, 13 Nov 2026.

What to take from it
  • Nothing in reported earnings or in analyst estimates had turned down by 20 November.
  • On next-year earnings the index is cheaper than its ten-year average. On ten-year earnings it was, at the October peak, more expensive than at any past peak in the study except 1999 and 2000.

Earnings were growing 25% when the index fell 20%

The decline was a fall in the price paid for earnings, not in earnings. Profit growth peaked in the quarter reported during the October slide. Forecasts for 2019 were cut by an ordinary amount. Earnings did shrink later, in the first half of 2019, while the index was rising.

How forecasts were cut

  • Expected earnings growth for calendar 2019: 10.3% on 21 Sep 2018, 9.4% on 2 Nov, 7.9% on 21 Dec, 7.4% on 4 Jan 2019, 2.7% on 28 Jun 2019.
  • In dollars, the 2019 estimate fell from $178.05 to $173.94 a share during the fourth quarter, −2.3%. The five-year average cut in a fourth quarter is −1.8% and the ten-year average −3.6% FactSet, 4 Jan 2019.
  • From 30 September to 21 December the index price fell 15.3% while the estimate for the next twelve months’ earnings rose 0.3% FactSet, 21 Dec 2018.

What to take from it
  • The multiple fell from 16.9 to 14.2 in three months and recovered to 16.2 in two more. Earnings estimates hardly moved over the same five months.
  • The price low came about two quarters before the low in earnings growth, and while analysts were still cutting.

The Fed tightened into the decline, then reversed within seven sessions of the low

The Fed raised rates four times in 2018 and was shrinking its balance sheet at the full planned pace from October. A handful of phrases from its chair marked the stages of the decline and the turn.

What the Fed did

  • Rate: 1.25–1.50% at the start of 2018; rises in March, June, 26 September and 19 December, to 2.25–2.50% Fed minutes, Dec 2018.
  • Forecast (“dot plot”, the officials’ own rate projections) for 2019: three rises in September 2018, two in December, none in March 2019 CNBC, 26 Sep 2018 Yahoo Finance, 20 Mar 2019.
  • Balance sheet: the runoff (maturing bonds not replaced) stepped up each quarter from October 2017 and reached $50bn a month, $30bn of Treasuries and $20bn of mortgage bonds, in October 2018. On 20 March 2019 the Fed said it would halve the Treasury part in May and stop in September Federal Reserve, 20 Mar 2019.

The December decision was not unanimous in spirit: no one dissented, but a few officials favoured no change Fed minutes, Dec 2018. In January the Fed’s staff and officials gave little weight to the runoff as a cause of the selloff, while noting that some investors believed it was one Fed minutes, Jan 2019. Inflation made the reversal possible: the Fed cited muted inflation pressures, and its preferred core measure was running near 2% not verified for the exact monthly prints.

What to take from it
  • From the hawkish peak (3 Oct) to the first softer words (28 Nov) took eight weeks; from the low to “patient” took seven sessions.
  • On 19 December the market got fewer forecast rate rises and sold anyway. What it had wanted was flexibility on the runoff.

Yields, oil and credit all turned within days of the stock low

Yields peaked in early November and fell hard into January. Oil lost 44% and bottomed on the same day as stocks. Credit spreads peaked six sessions after the stock low. The factory slowdown was real and lasted most of 2019.

Oil, dollar, mortgage rates

  • Crude oil (WTI) settled at $76.41 on 3 October and $42.53 on 24 December, −44%. It was in a bear market by 8 November and rose 9% on 26 December CNN, 8 Nov 2018 CNBC, 24 Dec 2018.
  • The broad dollar index rose from 113.50 (20 Sep) to 116.22 (24 Dec) and was back at 113.55 on 31 January FRED.
  • The 30-year mortgage rate rose from 3.95% in January 2018 to 4.94% in mid-November and fell to 4.06% by late March 2019 FRED. Existing-home sales fell 6.4% in December to the lowest in three years AP, Jan 2019.

Activity

  • ISM manufacturing index (a monthly factory survey; above 50 means growth): 61.3 in August 2018, 54.1 in December, 49.1 in August 2019 and 47.8 in September 2019 ISM, Dec 2018 ISM, Sep 2019.
  • Jobs stayed strong: +250,000 in October, +155,000 in November, +312,000 in December, unemployment 3.9% BLS, 4 Jan 2019.
  • China: stocks in a bear market from June, a four-year low in mid-October, retail sales growth in November the weakest since 2003; bank reserve requirements cut in October and January Bloomberg, 15 Oct 2018 CNBC, 14 Dec 2018.

Credit

The high-yield spread (the extra yield on junk bonds over Treasuries) was 5.12% on 21 December, the widest since July 2016, and peaked near 5.36% on 3 January Hedgeye, 21 Dec 2018 Reuters, early 2019. The study’s own series, the Baa spread (yield of Baa-rated corporate bonds over Treasuries), peaked at 2.48 points six sessions after the stock low. No junk bond was sold in the US between 30 November and 10 January CNN, 11 Jan 2019. The BIS noted that high-yield spreads only briefly exceeded their pre-crisis average BIS, Mar 2019.

What to take from it
  • Bond yields had been falling for six weeks before stocks bottomed; the bond market priced a Fed stop before the Fed announced one.
  • Credit confirmed late: the spread peak came on the 3 January higher low, not at the stock low.
  • The slowdown in factories continued for nine months after the stock low.
Limits The daily high-yield spread series could not be pulled again for this study (the public source now serves three years only), so the 3 January peak level rests on one press report.

Earnings fell by half in one bear and by nine-tenths in the other, and turned at different times against price

Reported earnings of S&P 500 companies fell 54% in 2000–01 and 92% in 2007–09. In 2000 earnings peaked six months after the share-price peak, so they gave no warning. In 2007 they peaked four months before it, but the fall was confined to financial companies. Valuation flagged the 2000 peak and not the 2007 one.

When the forecasts turned

  • 2000. Forecasts were still being raised in July 2000, four months after the peak. Warnings from large companies began on 21 September 2000. Analysts cut their year-ahead forecasts sharply only in December 2000 and January 2001, nine months after the peak. Their three-to-five-year growth forecasts barely moved even then. Fed Monetary Policy Report, Feb 2001
  • 2007. Forecasts for the year ahead were already being cut through mid-November 2007, but were unchanged once financial companies were left out. Fourth-quarter 2007 earnings were projected about 20% lower than a year before, with non-financial companies about 12% higher. Fed Greenbook, 23 Jan 2008
  • The exact months in which the forward-earnings series peaked and bottomed are not verified.

What to take from it
  • Rising forecasts were no protection in 2000: they kept rising for four months after the peak.
  • In 2007 the earnings decline was real at the peak but looked like one sector’s problem.
  • In 2002 shares kept falling for seven to ten months after earnings had bottomed. In 2009 the two turned together.
  • In November 2026 forecasts are still rising and the forward P/E is far below 2000, while CAPE is close to it.
