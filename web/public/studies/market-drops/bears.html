2000–02 and 2007–09

The S&P 500 fell 49% over 637 sessions from March 2000 and −57% over 355 sessions from October 2007. Neither looked like a bear market in its first two months. This page sets out how each unfolded, how many rallies failed on the way, and what the first weeks looked like at the time.

Six weeks in, the two great bears looked like ordinary corrections

Thirty sessions after its peak the S&P 500 was down 6.8% in 2000 and 8.0% in 2007. Corrections that ended within months were as deep or deeper at the same point. What differed was under the index: which groups had already broken, the Federal Reserve’s direction, and credit.

2000: a correction that healed, then a second peak

The S&P 500 fell 11.1% in the three weeks to 14 April 2000 and then recovered. On 1 September 2000 it closed at 1,520.77, 0.4% below the March record. The Nasdaq Composite was a different picture: 37.3% down at its 23 May low, and still 16% below its peak on the day the S&P retested. AP market report, 1 Sep 2000

Market reports in March and April 2000 carried strategists describing the Nasdaq’s fall as a healthy correction, and one called a bottom on 4 April, the day of a 13.6% intraday plunge. AP market report, 4 Apr 2000 The Fed’s report to Congress in July 2000 said company earnings had mostly beaten expectations and that forecasts were still being revised higher. Fed Monetary Policy Report, Jul 2000 Wall Street strategists’ year-end targets for 2000 could not be retrieved: not verified.

2007: a 10% dip, a rally on Fed speeches, an official forecast of no recession

The S&P 500 was 10.1% below its peak on 26 November 2007. Two Fed speeches on 28 and 29 November signalled more rate cuts, and the index rose 7.7% in two weeks. The November low broke on 15 January 2008.

On 11 December 2007 the Fed’s staff told the rate-setting committee that their forecast showed slow growth and no recession. The National Bureau of Economic Research later dated the start of the recession to that same month. FOMC transcript, 11 Dec 2007 NBER, 1 Dec 2008

Despite all the financial turmoil, the economy avoids recession. — Fed staff director, FOMC meeting, 11 Dec 2007

The same measures in November 2026

  • S&P 500 −11.6% in 33 sessions from the 6 October 2026 record. Nasdaq 100 −19.8%. The semiconductor index is 42.5% below its 22 June 2026 high.
  • The average stock has barely moved. The equal-weight S&P 500 is −2.3% since 6 October, the median of about 635 large stocks is −1.3%, and 47% of them are higher than on 6 October.
  • Consumer staples +10.6% and health care +6.1% since the peak; technology −26.2%. The strongest fifth of stocks by prior 12-month return is down 22% at the median.
What to take from it
  • Depth after six weeks carried no information: bears and corrections overlapped completely.
  • In both bears, officials and earnings forecasts gave no warning at this stage.
  • The differences were in leadership, the Fed’s direction, the yield curve, credit and new supply. Each of these was visible at the time, and each had also given false signals in other years.
Limits Two bears are a small sample. The 1998 and 2015 corrections also began with small companies and transports lagging. Press quotations from the time could not be retrieved.

2000: a top in two stages, with the Fed still raising rates

The S&P 500 fell 49.1% in 637 sessions, from 24 March 2000 to 9 October 2002. The Nasdaq Composite fell 77.9%. The first decline in spring 2000 was repaired by September. The decline that did not recover began only after a second peak on 1 September, when large technology companies started to warn about sales.

Three peaks in ten weeks

The Dow peaked first, on 14 January 2000. The Nasdaq Composite peaked on 10 March at 5,048.62; that day the Dow was already 15.3% below its own high. The S&P 500 peaked last, on 24 March at 1,527.46. AP market report, 10 Mar 2000 Money was leaving the old leaders for the Dow stocks and back again within days: on 15 and 16 March the Dow gained 819 points while the Nasdaq corrected.

The Federal Reserve was tightening throughout. It raised its policy rate on 2 February and 21 March 2000, and by 0.50 point to 6.50% on 16 May, eight weeks after the S&P peak. FOMC statement, 16 May 2000 The yield on 10-year Treasuries had been below the 2-year yield since 2 February (an inverted yield curve, which has often come before recessions).

The April 2000 crash

  • 3 April. Settlement talks in the Microsoft antitrust case collapsed. The Nasdaq fell 349 points (7.6%) while the Dow rose 300. AP, 3 Apr 2000
  • 4 April. The Nasdaq was down 13.6% during the day and closed down 1.8%.
  • 14 April. A strong consumer-price report raised fear of more rate rises, and forced selling by investors who had borrowed to buy shares (margin calls) added to it. The Nasdaq lost 9.7% on the day and about 25% on the week. The S&P 500 lost 5.8% and closed 11.1% below its peak, its first close 10% down. AP, 14 Apr 2000
  • The next two sessions brought the Nasdaq’s two largest point gains to that date (+6.6% and +7.2%).

Two well-known hedge funds were casualties from opposite sides. Tiger Management, which had bet against the bubble, closed to outside investors on 30 March. The managers of Soros’s Quantum fund, which had owned technology shares, left on 28 April. not verified beyond Wikipedia’s citations of the press.

The summer rally and the 1 September retest

The Nasdaq made its spring low on 23 May 2000 at 3,164.55, down 37.3%. Then it rose 35.1% in eight weeks, to 4,274.67 on 17 July. A soft jobs report on 2 June had raised hope that the Fed was finished. On 1 September the S&P 500 closed at 1,520.77, within 0.4% of its record. The Nasdaq that day was 16% below its own.

To this point company earnings had given no warning. The Fed’s July report said forecasts were still being revised up. Fed Monetary Policy Report, Jul 2000

Autumn 2000: the warnings

The second decline was led by news from the companies themselves. Intel warned on 21 September and fell 22% the next day on record volume. Apple, Dell, Home Depot and IBM followed within four weeks, and Nortel fell 29% on 25 October after a shortfall in sales of optical equipment. AP, 22 Sep 2000 AP, 25 Oct 2000 The disputed presidential election ran from 7 November to 12 December; the court ruling that ended it brought no relief rally.

The Fed held its rate at 6.50% and kept warning about inflation until 19 December, when it switched to warning about weakness without cutting. The Nasdaq fell 4.3% that day and 7.1% the next. It ended 2000 down 39%, its worst year.

What to take from it
  • The first 11% fall in the S&P 500 was fully repaired within five months. The top was a process, not a day.
  • The leading index broke first and made lower highs in July and September while the broad index returned to its record.
  • The lasting decline began with company warnings, six months after the price peak.

2001: eleven rate cuts, three large rallies, lower lows

The Fed cut its rate from 6.50% to 1.75% during 2001. Each burst of cuts produced a rally of about 20% in the S&P 500 and 40% in the Nasdaq. Each rally failed, because profits and business investment were still falling.

The surprise cut of 3 January

A factory survey on 2 January 2001 came in at its lowest since 1991 and the Nasdaq fell 7.2%. The next day, between scheduled meetings, the Fed cut by 0.50 point. The Nasdaq rose 14.2%, its largest one-day gain, and the S&P 500 rose 5.0% on record volume. FOMC statement, 3 Jan 2001 AP, 3 Jan 2001

The rally lasted three weeks. The Nasdaq peaked on 24 January, 24.8% above its 2 January close, and a second cut on 31 January was sold. Then the news that had started the decline resumed. Cisco missed its earnings forecast on 6 February for the first time in over six years. Nortel, Dell and Hewlett-Packard warned on 15 February. By 4 April the Nasdaq had fallen 42.7% from the January high and the S&P 500 19.1%.

The reason the cut did not hold was stated by the Fed itself in April: a persistent fall in current and expected company profits, and investment plans being postponed or cancelled. FOMC statement, 18 Apr 2001 Profits of non-financial companies were falling at an annual rate of about 45% in the last quarter of 2000. Fed Monetary Policy Report, Feb 2002

Spring rally, summer slide

Cisco warned again on 16 April and cut 8,500 jobs. Two days later the Fed made a second surprise cut. The S&P 500 rose 19.0% from 4 April to 21 May and the Nasdaq 41.2% to 22 May. Over the summer the rally faded as the expected recovery kept being pushed back. JDS Uniphase reported the largest write-down of acquired assets to that date on 26 July.

11 September

The attacks closed US markets for four trading days. They reopened on 17 September with a 0.50-point cut before the bell. The Dow lost 7.1% that day and 14.3% on the week, its largest weekly loss since 1940. The low came on 21 September: S&P 500 965.80. The VIX (an index of expected S&P 500 volatility taken from option prices) reached its highest intraday level of the whole bear market that day, 49.35.

From there the S&P 500 rose 21.4% to 4 January 2002 and the Nasdaq 44.7%. In the study’s count this is the largest failed rally inside any S&P 500 decline since 1950. The recession had started in March 2001 and ended in November 2001, though that was announced only later. NBER, 26 Nov 2001

What to take from it
  • Rate cuts produced three rallies of about 20% in the S&P 500 inside twelve months. All three ended at lower highs.
  • The rallies began on policy news. The declines resumed on company news.
  • The Fed’s cuts worked on housing and consumers; the recession lasted eight months. They did not restore technology spending or profits.

2002–03: a last leg with no recession, a low on earnings news, and a retest

The S&P 500 fell another 33.8% from 4 January to 9 October 2002 with the economy growing and the Fed’s rate unchanged. The cause was a collapse of trust in reported profits, at valuations that were still high. The low held on a run of earnings reports, and was tested once more in March 2003 before the advance began.

The accounting scandals

  • Enron filed for bankruptcy on 2 December 2001, the largest US filing to that date. Global Crossing followed in January 2002.
  • Tyco’s chief executive resigned on 3 June 2002; the stock fell 27% and the S&P 500 2.5%.
  • WorldCom disclosed $3.8bn of disguised expenses on 25 June 2002 and filed for bankruptcy on 21 July. Wikipedia, WorldCom scandal

July 2002: the panic

On 23 July 2002 the S&P 500 closed at 797.70; the Dow had fallen on eleven of the last twelve sessions. The next day brought arrests at Adelphia and agreement in Congress on an accounting-reform law; the S&P 500 rose 5.7% on record New York Stock Exchange volume. It rose another 5.4% on 29 July. The Sarbanes-Oxley Act was signed on 30 July. The rally reached 962.70 on 22 August, a gain of 20.7%, and then failed. AP, 24 Jul 2002

Volume and fear peaked in this stretch, not at the final low. The VIX made its highest close of the bear market, 45.08, on 5 August 2002.

9 October 2002: the low

The S&P 500 closed at 776.76 and the Nasdaq at 1,114.11. The VIX closed at 42.13, lower than in August. The turn came on earnings: Yahoo on 10 October, General Electric on 11 October, then Citigroup, General Motors and Johnson & Johnson on 15 October. The Dow gained 13.3% in four sessions. AP, 15 Oct 2002 By the study’s rule the follow-through day (a strong gain on higher volume four or more sessions after a low) came on the fourth session, and the index was back above its 50-day average with a gain of 13.4%.

The Fed cut by 0.50 point to 1.25% on 6 November, four weeks after the low. The first advance ended on 27 November at 938.87, up 20.9%.

March 2003: the retest and the turn

Through the winter the approach of war in Iraq weighed on prices. The S&P 500 closed at 800.73 on 11 March 2003, 3.1% above the October low. The Nasdaq was 14% above its October low and the semiconductor index 30% above. The index rose 3.5% on 13 March and 3.5% on 17 March, hours before the ultimatum to Iraq; the invasion began on 20 March. The S&P 500 closed above its 200-day average on 21 March 2003, 112 sessions after the low. The Fed’s last cut, to 1.00%, came on 25 June 2003.

What to take from it
  • The final leg had no macro cause: the recession had ended eleven months before the low.
  • Fear and volume peaked in July and August 2002. The October low came on less of both.
  • The low turned on company earnings, not on the Fed. On the way down the index had closed back above its 20-day average 37 times and above its 50-day average 19 times without the decline ending.
Limits Index levels for single days come from afternoon-edition wire reports and can differ by a point from final closes. The exact days of the Global Crossing filing and of some indictments are not verified.

2007: credit broke in the summer, the index peaked in October

The S&P 500 fell 56.8% in 355 sessions, from 9 October 2007 to 9 March 2009. The trouble started in mortgage credit eight months before the index peak. Two ordinary-looking corrections came and went in 2007, and the Federal Reserve was already cutting rates when the index made its high.

The tremors: February and the summer

  • February 2007. HSBC warned on 7–8 February of losses on US subprime mortgages (loans to weaker borrowers); the market ignored it. On 27 February the S&P 500 fell 3.5% after a plunge in Shanghai. It lost 5.9% in all and was at a new high by mid-April.
  • March–May. The Fed chairman told Congress on 28 March that the subprime problem seemed likely to be contained. Bernanke testimony, 28 Mar 2007
  • June–July. Two Bear Stearns hedge funds holding mortgage securities failed: redemptions frozen on 7 June, a letter on 17 July saying almost nothing was left, bankruptcy on 31 July. Lenders had demanded more collateral and nobody would buy the assets. Financial Crisis Inquiry Commission report, p. 240
  • 19 July. The Dow closed above 14,000 for the first time. The Russell 2000 index of small companies had peaked on 13 July and the Dow Transports peaked that day; neither made a new high in October.

August 2007: the first credit seizure

On 9 August the French bank BNP Paribas froze three funds because their mortgage securities could not be priced. The European Central Bank lent about €95bn overnight. In the same week, funds that held shares long and short by computer models (quant funds) suffered sudden losses as they all sold the same positions: a simulated strategy of this kind lost 6.85% in three days before leverage. Khandani and Lo, 2007 The S&P 500 fell 9.4% from 19 July to 15 August on closing prices.

The Fed cut its discount rate (the rate on its direct loans to banks) on 17 August and its main policy rate by 0.50 point on 18 September. The index rose 2.9% that day and went on to a record close of 1,565.15 on 9 October 2007.

October–December 2007: the peak and the first 10%

The peak came with the Fed easing and with credit unrepaired. Merrill Lynch raised its third-quarter loss to $8.4bn on 24 October; the chief executives of Merrill and Citigroup left within a week of each other. The S&P 500 was 10.1% below its peak on 26 November. Speeches by two Fed officials on 28 and 29 November promised flexibility, and the index rose 7.7% in two weeks. A rate cut of only 0.25 point on 11 December disappointed: −2.5% that day. FOMC minutes, 11 Dec 2007

Stress between banks was worse in late November than in August. The TED spread (the gap between the rate banks charge each other and the Treasury-bill rate) was 1.29 points at the October peak and 1.99 on 26 November. Short-term company IOUs backed by assets (asset-backed commercial paper) shrank from $1,226bn on 8 August to $775bn at year-end.

What to take from it
  • Two corrections in 2007 (−5.9% and −9.4%) recovered to new highs in seven to eight weeks. Depth did not mark the one that mattered.
  • Financials, homebuilders, small companies and transports peaked months before the index.
  • The index peaked three weeks after the first rate cut.

2008: rescues, rallies, then the autumn crash

The Fed cut its rate from 4.25% to 2.00% between January and April 2008 and rescued Bear Stearns in March. The index rallied 12% and then fell through the summer as oil reached $147. After Lehman Brothers failed on 15 September, the S&P 500 lost 32% in six weeks (12 September to 27 October).

January to May: emergency cut, Bear Stearns, the spring rally

  • 22 January. After a worldwide sell-off on a US holiday, made worse by a French bank unwinding a rogue trader’s positions, the Fed cut by 0.75 point between meetings. The index made an intraday low on 23 January, 18.9% below the peak, and reversed. FOMC minutes, 30 Jan 2008
  • 11 March. The Fed offered to lend $200bn of Treasuries against mortgage bonds; S&P 500 +3.7%.
  • 14–17 March. Bear Stearns ran out of cash in four days as lenders and hedge-fund clients withdrew. JPMorgan agreed to buy it with Fed support, first at $2 a share, later $10. The Fed cut by another 0.75 point on 18 March; S&P 500 +4.2%.
  • 19 May. The rally peaked at 1,426.63, up 12.0% from the 10 March close and 8.9% below the record. It ended just under a falling 200-day average.

Summer: oil, the mortgage agencies, a first 20% loss

Oil peaked at $147.27 on 11 July 2008. Energy and materials shares had led the market until 20 May and then fell hard. The S&P 500 first closed 20% below its peak on 9 July. Worry moved to Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac, the two government-sponsored mortgage companies; the government took control of both on Sunday 7 September. St. Louis Fed crisis timeline

September–October: Lehman and the crash

  • 15 September. Lehman Brothers filed for bankruptcy; Merrill Lynch was sold to Bank of America. S&P 500 −4.7%.
  • 16–17 September. The Fed lent $85bn to the insurer AIG. A large money-market fund fell below $1 a share, and prime money-market funds lost $349bn in a week. Hedge funds asked to withdraw $32bn from Morgan Stanley in one day.
  • 18–19 September. A ban on short selling of financial shares and the first outline of a bank rescue plan: +4.3% and +4.0%. SEC, 19 Sep 2008
  • 29 September. The House of Representatives rejected the rescue plan (TARP). S&P 500 −8.8%, Dow −777.68.
  • 3 October. TARP passed; the index fell 1.4% that day.
  • 6–10 October. The index lost 18.2% in five sessions. The intraday low on 10 October was 839.80, on record New York Stock Exchange volume. The TED spread reached a record 4.58.
  • 13 October. Governments announced they would put capital directly into banks: +11.6%, the largest one-day gain since at least 1950. Two days later −9.0%.

November: the fear peak

After the election rally (+18.5% from 27 October to 4 November) the index fell 25.2% in twelve sessions. On 20 November 2008 it closed at 752.44 and the VIX closed at 80.86, its highest close until March 2020. Citigroup shares had fallen 23% and 26% on the last two days. Over the weekend the government guaranteed $306bn of Citigroup’s assets, and on 25 November the Fed announced it would buy up to $600bn of mortgage-agency debt and mortgage bonds, the start of what became known as quantitative easing. Fed, 25 Nov 2008 The rate on 30-year mortgages fell from 6.04% to 5.10% by year-end: the first time in the crisis that policy reached borrowers.

What to take from it
  • Rescues and rate cuts set off rallies of 7% to 19%; each failed while bank losses kept growing.
  • Approval of a rescue was sold more than once (3 October 2008; 10 and 17 February 2009).
  • Fear, volume and bank-to-bank stress peaked in October and November 2008, months before the final low.

2009: a last leg in banks, and a low with no retest

The index rose 24.2% from 20 November 2008 to 6 January 2009 and then fell 27.6% to 9 March. The last leg was concentrated in banks, industrial companies and the defensive groups that had held up before. Technology, emerging markets, commodities and company bonds did not make new lows.

January and February: the rally fails

The Fed cut its rate to a range of 0–0.25% on 16 December 2008. The rally that followed never closed above the 200-day average. Bank of America needed a second rescue on 16 January. The index fell 5.3% on 20 January, inauguration day, 4.9% on 10 February when the new Treasury Secretary presented a bank plan without detail, and 4.6% on 17 February when the fiscal stimulus was signed. AIG reported a loss of $99.3bn for 2008 on 2 March.

6–9 March 2009: the low

The intraday low was 666.79 on 6 March and the closing low 676.53 on 9 March. The VIX closed at 49.68, far below November’s 80.86. A survey of individual investors on 5 March showed 70.3% expecting lower prices, the highest reading in its history. AAII sentiment survey The Nasdaq 100 closed on 9 March slightly above its 20 November close.

What changed in the next two weeks

  • 10 March. Citigroup’s chief executive told staff the bank had been profitable in January and February. S&P 500 +6.4%. The Fed chairman spoke the same day about reviewing accounting rules that forced banks to mark assets to distressed prices. Bernanke, 10 Mar 2009
  • 12 March. Bank of America said the same about its profits. +4.1%.
  • 18 March. The Fed enlarged its purchases: $750bn more mortgage bonds and $300bn of Treasuries. +2.1%. Fed, 18 Mar 2009
  • 23 March. A Treasury programme to buy troubled assets with private partners. +7.1%.
  • 2 April. Accounting rules for hard-to-price assets were eased.
  • 7 May. Results of the bank stress tests: ten of nineteen banks had to raise $74.6bn in total. Fed, 7 May 2009

The index was 23.1% above its low after thirteen sessions and never came back. By the study’s rule the follow-through day came on the seventh session; the 50-day average was regained on the tenth with a gain of 21.6%, and the 200-day average after 58 sessions, on 1 June 2009, with a gain of 39.4%. The recession ended in June 2009. NBER, 20 Sep 2010

What to take from it
  • The final low came on lower fear and lower volume than October and November 2008, with technology and emerging markets holding above their earlier lows.
  • What ended the decline was bank capital, guarantees, asset purchases and accounting relief, plus two banks saying they were making money. Rate cuts had run out three months earlier.
  • On the way down the index had closed back above its 20-day average 31 times and above its 50-day average 15 times.

Inside the two bears: frequent, violent rallies that began on policy and ended on earnings

The study counts 15 failed rallies of 5% or more in the S&P 500 in each bear, and 40 in the Nasdaq Composite in 2000–02. The largest gained 21% in the S&P 500 and 45% in the Nasdaq. Most began on a Federal Reserve move or a rescue. Each failed while company profits were still falling or bank losses still growing.

What they looked like

  • Sharper than anything in a rising market. Ten of the Nasdaq Composite’s twenty largest one-day gains since 1971 fall inside the 2000–02 bear, including +14.2% on 3 January 2001. Seven of the S&P 500’s twenty largest since 1950 fall inside the 2007–09 bear, including +11.6% on 13 October 2008.
  • Big up days were a mark of the bear itself. The S&P 500 gained 3% or more on 17 days in 2000–02 and on 23 days in 2007–09. In the whole advance from March 2003 to October 2007 it did so twice.
  • Short in a crisis. The ten S&P 500 rallies of 7% or more in 2007–09 lasted a median of six sessions. One lasted a single day.
  • Led by what had fallen most. This was true of the failures and also of the real low: in the three months after 9 March 2009 the financial sector fund doubled while consumer staples gained 23%.

The 200-day average as a ceiling

Once the 200-day average had turned down, every failed rally ended below it or no more than 2% above it. In May 2001, August 2002, May 2008, August 2008 and January 2009 the index never closed above it at all. But the first rally off the true low of October 2002 also stayed below it throughout. Both real advances were confirmed late by this measure: after 112 sessions in 2003 and 58 in 2009.

What published research adds

Goldman Sachs counted 19 bear-market rallies in world shares since the 1980s, lasting 44 days and gaining 10–15% on average, and explained them by thin positioning. CoinDesk report of the Goldman note, 10 Apr 2025

What to take from it
  • A rally of 20% did not end either bear. Four rallies of 19% to 24% failed in the S&P 500 between 2001 and 2009.
  • The triggers were policy actions. The real lows turned on earnings (October 2002) and on banks reporting profits (March 2009).
  • Size, speed and leadership by beaten-down shares were the same in the failures and in the real turn.
Limits Two bears. Goldman’s original notes could not be opened; only a press report was read.

The Fed cut by hundreds of basis points in both bears, and shares kept falling

In 2001 the Federal Reserve cut its policy rate by 475 basis points (4.75 percentage points) and the S&P 500 still fell 42% from the first cut to the low. In 2007–08 it cut by 325 basis points in seven months and the index fell 55% from the first cut. The first problem was profits and overbuilt capacity; the second was the solvency of lenders.

2001–02: cheap money could not restore profits

  • The downturn was not caused by tight money, so easy money did not reverse it. It was led by a fall in profits, then by cuts in stocks of goods and in business investment. BIS Annual Report 2002
  • Too much had been built. Telephone companies were investing at a rate of about 33% of their capital in 2000 against a long-run 18%. Spending on communications equipment fell more than 30% in 2001. New York Fed, 2004
  • Profits of non-financial companies fell at an annual rate of about 45% in late 2000 and about 25% a year through 2001. Fed Monetary Policy Report, Feb 2002
  • The cuts did work where borrowing responds to rates: housing, cars and consumer spending. The recession lasted eight months.
  • The 2002 leg happened with the rate flat at 1.75%. It was about trust in accounts after Enron and WorldCom.

One bear had many places to hide, the other had almost none

In 2000–02 the damage was in technology and telecoms; the median large stock that survived was roughly flat and half of them rose. In 2007–09 almost everything fell by half. In both, the groups that had resisted cracked in the last leg, several groups bottomed before the index, and most leaders of the next advance had made their own lows months earlier.

What defied each bear, and when it cracked

  • 2000–02. Homebuilders, health insurers, defence contractors, tobacco and discount retailers made their lows between October 1999 and the week the Nasdaq peaked, then rose for two years. From 24 March 2000 to 9 October 2002: NVR +410%, Lockheed Martin +241%, UnitedHealth +217%, AutoZone +203%, Lennar +139% (study data). Berkshire Hathaway made its low on 10 March 2000, the day of the Nasdaq peak.
  • They broke late. Most of these winners topped between March and July 2002 and lost 20–45% into October: NVR −29% from 2 May, Toll Brothers −43% from 14 May, Philip Morris −37% from 4 June.
  • 2007–09. Energy, materials, steel, fertiliser and solar shares led until May and June 2008, eight months into the bear, then fell 70–90% by November. After Lehman only Treasuries (+25% for the long-bond fund over the whole bear), gold (+24%), the dollar and a few discount retailers held: AutoZone +22%, Ross Stores +10%, Wal-Mart +7%.

Leaders of the next advance

From 2003. Fifteen of the later big winners reached a 52-week high within seven weeks of 15 October 2002, and every one had made its own low 12 to 31 months before the index: eBay (low December 2000), Sohu and NetEase (April and July 2001), Amazon (28 September 2001), Cognizant, Coach, UnitedHealth, Career Education, Teva, Gilead, Garmin. A second wave broke out in the thirteen weeks after 17 March 2003: Netflix, Starbucks, Amgen, the homebuilders, Research In Motion, Freeport-McMoRan, Marvell. Apple made its low late, on 17 April 2003, five weeks after the index retest.

From 2009. The growth leaders made their lows between 27 October and 10 December 2008 and stood well above them when the index bottomed on 9 March 2009: Netflix +115%, Freeport-McMoRan +92%, Amazon +73%, MercadoLibre +70%, Priceline +66%, Baidu +48%, Goldman Sachs +42%, Salesforce +41%, Chipotle +27%. What rebounded hardest

In both cases the fifth of stocks that had fallen most gained most in the next year: +70.5% in 2002–03 and +150.5% in 2009–10 at the median, against +25.6% and +40.2% for the fifth that had fallen least. The winners of each bear lagged afterwards: in 2009 Wal-Mart lost 4.7% and McDonald’s gained 0.4%.

What to take from it
  • 2000–02 punished what had risen most; 2007–09 punished everything, and financials most.
  • Resisting a bear for two years did not protect a group in its last three months.
  • In 2008–09 most growth groups stopped falling on 20 November 2008, 72 sessions before the index low.
  • Two kinds of next-cycle winner existed: stocks holding far above their own lows as the index bottomed, and late casualties that bottomed with it.

What to take from it
  • Neither bear announced itself. After 33 sessions both were shallower than the present drop, and both later rallied (2000 to within 1% of its high, 2007 to within 9%) before the real decline.
  • What distinguished them was not the first leg but persistence: 15 and 15 failed rallies, a 200-day average that turned down and stayed down, earnings that fell by more than half, and (in 2007–09) credit spreads that kept widening.
  • In both, the Fed was cutting rates for most of the way down. Rate cuts did not end them.
  • Their final lows were lows of exhaustion: VIX and new-low counts had peaked months earlier.
Limits Two episodes, both with recessions and both with a broken financing channel (telecom and dot-com equity in 2000; mortgages and banks in 2007). They show what a bear can look like, not what one must look like. The 1973–74 and 2022 bears had other mechanics (inflation and rates).
