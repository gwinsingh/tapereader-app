The stocks that led

After each low, which stocks led, and what had they looked like during the drop? Three questions are put to about 250 to 630 large stocks in each drop since 2000: did the hardest-hit bounce most, did the pre-drop leaders lead again, and did the first stocks back at new highs stay in front.

In seven of eight drops the first month belonged to the wreckage: the fifth that had fallen most gained two to four times as much as the fifth that had fallen least. This is the pattern the research literature calls a momentum crash: at a market low the losers snap back hardest. The exception is 2022, a slow grind in which the low was not a washout.

  • After the crash-type lows (2009, 2020, 2025) the hardest-hit kept leading for a year: what had been sold in panic recovered furthest.
  • After the December 2018 low the advantage was gone within three months: by six and twelve months the five groups were level (rank correlation −0.12 and −0.15). The same goes for 2022.
  • The fifth that fell least was at or near the bottom in every drop over the following year. The shelters of the decline were the laggards of the advance, among stocks as among sectors.

Median returns. Green = the pre-drop leaders beat the pre-drop laggards by more than five points.

  • In 2018, 2020 and 2025 the strongest stocks going in fell a little more than the rest and then led clearly coming out (after December 2018: +40% against +25% over six months). The theme that led the market up had not been broken by the drop.
  • In 2007–09 and 2022 having been a leader before told nothing about afterwards.
  • In 2000–02 the strongest fifth going in fell 47% at the median among the companies that survived, against a gain for the weakest fifth. The survivors among them then rebounded well from the 2002 low, but from far below; the theme did not lead the next bull market.
  • Today, by the same sort, the strongest fifth going into 6 October has fallen 22% at the median while the other four fifths are between −4% and +4%. In 2018 all five fifths fell about the same (−19% to −24%).

Returns are measured from the index low, so they include the move that carried the stock to its new high.

The rule held after the 2018 lows and not after the deep ones. The reason is visible in who makes early new highs: after a 20% correction it is the strongest growth stocks; after a 35% to 55% collapse it is mostly the stocks that barely fell (utilities, staples, gold miners), and those lag the rebound. The rule works better as a description of leadership in a correction than as a general law.

What the 2019 leaders had in common
  • They were not the stocks that held up. Their median fall was −24%, against the typical stock's −20%; 5 of 30 were above their 200-day average at the low.
  • They were the stocks that had been strong before: median gain of +65% in the 12 months before the peak, against +17% for the typical stock.
  • They were back at new 52-week highs fast: a median of 41.5 sessions after the low, while only 4.5% of all stocks made a new high within 20 sessions.
  • Software, payments and internet names dominate the list; semiconductors (AMD, Lam, KLA, Teradyne) appear in the 12-month list. The names match the leaders the research file identifies from contemporary sources.

Off a low the hardest-hit stocks rose most; the lasting leaders showed themselves later

Two views conflict. Growth-stock practice says the first stocks to reach new highs after a low are the new leaders. Academic work on momentum says the opposite happens in the first weeks: the stocks that fell most rebound hardest. The study’s stock data side with the second view for the first month and give the first view only mixed support.

The practitioner view

William O’Neil’s rule is that the first stocks to break out of sound price bases to new highs in the weeks after a follow-through day (a strong gain on higher volume, four or more sessions after a low) tend to lead the next advance, and that their relative-strength lines (the stock’s price divided by the index) often reach new highs before the price does. The source is his book How to Make Money in Stocks; no independent test was found. not verified One related test was negative: the share of NYSE stocks at 52-week highs on the signal day was the same before rallies that worked (2.1%) and rallies that failed (2.0%). Quantifiable Edges, 7 Feb 2008

The momentum-crash evidence

A momentum strategy buys the past year’s winners and sells its losers. Daniel and Moskowitz showed that its worst losses come after market declines, when volatility is high, as the market rebounds. Daniel & Moskowitz, 2014

+163%past losers, March–May 2009
+8%past winners, March–May 2009
+232%past losers, July–August 1932
+32%past winners, July–August 1932

The market itself rose 26% in the 2009 window and 82% in the 1932 one. In March 2009 the loser group was down 84% from its peak on average and held banks and carmakers; the winner group held defensive companies. The authors’ description: after a bear market, beaten-down and indebted stocks behave like options on a recovery.

  • The hardest-hit fifth led over the first 21 sessions at seven of the eight lows, by a wide margin. The exception was 2022, the slow bear market without a panic.
  • Stocks that made a new high within 30 sessions of the low did better than the rest over the next year at three of eight lows (February 2018, December 2018, 2022) and worse at five (2002, 2011, 2016, 2020, 2025).
  • Stocks that had been strongest in the year before the drop beat the weakest over the next year at five of nine lows. Prior leaders were neither reliably finished nor reliably back.
  • In the multi-leg declines 38–51% of stocks made their own low ten or more sessions before the index (2002, 2009, 2011, 2016, 2022). In the fast shocks almost none did: 0.2% in 2020, 6.5% in 2025.

First weeks against first months

  • First weeks. The rebound was led by what had fallen most: energy and miners in 2016, energy and homebuilders in 2020, banks in 2009. A screen for relative strength run in the first month mostly found defensive stocks.
  • First months. Groups making new highs two to three months after the low were often the durable leaders: banks and semiconductors in 2016, semiconductors and large technology in 2023. This is O’Neil’s observation on a slower clock.
  • After a short shock inside a rising market (1998, 2025) the earlier leaders simply resumed: internet stocks in 1998, semiconductors in 2025.
What to take from it
  • “Strongest stocks” and “most beaten-down stocks” were different lists at a low, and the second list won the first month in seven of eight cases.
  • The first-new-highs rule was not confirmed as a general rule in this data; it may hold for the narrower set of growth stocks it was written for.
  • Whether old leaders returned depended on the decline: yes after outside shocks, no for energy after 2022.

What to take from it
  • In the first weeks off a low, the most beaten-down stocks bounced most, almost every time. That edge lasted a year after crash-type lows and about a quarter after the 2018 low.
  • After the 2018, 2020 and 2025 lows the stocks that had led before the drop led again, because their theme was intact. After 2000–02 the old leaders did not lead the next advance.
  • Which of those two the present belongs to cannot be read from the price action of a 33-session-old drop. What can be read is that the damage so far is concentrated in the former leaders, which is how 2000 began and not how 2018 looked.
  • The stocks that held up best in a drop were not the next leaders; they were usually the next laggards.
