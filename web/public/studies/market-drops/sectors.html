Sectors and themes

Which sectors and industry groups fell most in each drop, which held up, which turned up before the index did, and which led afterwards. Measured with sector and industry ETFs, the same way for every drop since 2000.

How to read the tables

Four numbers per group, per drop.

  • Fall: total return from the day of the S&P 500's peak to the day of its low. vs S&P: the same minus the S&P 500's (SPY) return.
  • Own low: how many sessions before the index low the group made its own low. 0 = the same day.
  • Relative low: how many sessions before the index low the group's price relative to the S&P 500 made its low. A group whose relative line bottoms weeks early is already outperforming while the index is still falling: it has “turned up before the market”.
  • Next 6 months vs S&P: return over the 126 sessions after the index low, minus the S&P 500's.

What repeated across the drops

Eight drops of 10% or more since 2000 (leaving aside the nine-day one of February 2018).

−0.8%median 6-month excess return after the low for groups whose relative line bottomed 15+ sessions before the index (155 cases)
+3.9%the same for groups whose relative line bottomed with the index or later (172 cases)
−11.7%average 6-month excess return after the low for staples, utilities and health care
−0.68typical rank correlation across sectors between the fall and the next 6 months' excess return (negative = what fell most rebounded most)
What to take from it
  • The shelters of the decline lagged the recovery. Staples, utilities and health care were among the smallest fallers in nearly every drop and trailed the index over the following six months in almost every one.
  • Groups that stopped underperforming early tended to lead. In 2018 the relative lines of semiconductors, homebuilders and software bottomed 23 to 43 sessions before the 24 December low; in 2008–09 miners, homebuilders, retailers and emerging markets bottomed in relative terms in November 2008, about 70 to 90 sessions before the March 2009 low.
  • The group at the centre of the damage was different. Financials in 2008–09 and technology in 2000–02 kept making relative lows to the very end; financials then rebounded hardest, technology (after 2002) did not regain leadership for years.
  • Energy and commodities followed their own cycle: they fell most in 2015, 2018 and 2020, and rose 45% in 2022 while everything else fell.

“Relative low: still falling” means the group made a new low against the S&P 500 on the latest day. For the groups that have beaten the index since the peak, the relative low is simply the first day.

So far 6 of 11 sectors are higher than on the day of the index high (consumer discretionary, financials, real estate, utilities, health care and consumer staples), while technology is down 26% and semiconductors −37%. In the 2018 drop no sector rose and the gap between the best and worst sector was about 25 points; here it is 37 points after 33 sessions. A few earlier drops had a sector or two rise over the whole decline (staples in 2000–02, utilities in 2011 and 2015–16, energy in 2022), but after the same number of sessions only 2000 shows a gap this wide. Now against 2018 takes this up.

Limits ETFs change their holdings over time; a sector fund in 2000 is not the same basket as today (communication services was created in 2018; real estate in 2015). Six-month excess returns are measured from the index low, which is known only afterwards. “Relative low” is sensitive to single days. Eight drops is a small sample.
