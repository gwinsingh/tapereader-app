Signs at the low

What was visible around past lows, sign by sign: which came before the low, which came with it, and which only confirmed it afterwards. The closing table sets today's readings beside them.

The method is the same for every sign: take each drop since 1987, find the sign's extreme reading, and count how many sessions before or after the final price low it came. A sign that peaks before the low in some drops and after it in others cannot time a low; it can only say whether conditions resemble past lows.

VIX history starts in 1990 (for 1987 the older VXO is used elsewhere on the site). The 3-month VIX starts in 2006. “Inverted” = VIX above 3-month VIX: the market pays more for protection now than for protection later, which is the mark of acute stress.

  • In the fast drops (1998, 2018 December, 2020, 2025) the VIX peaked within a week of the price low.
  • In the slow ones the VIX peaked far earlier: 72 sessions before the March 2009 low, 46 before the October 2002 low, 156 before the October 2022 low, 118 before the February 2016 low. The final low came with less fear than an earlier low.
  • The curve inverted in every drop of 15% or more since 2006 and was back to normal within days of the low in the fast drops.
  • After a low the VIX stayed above 20 for weeks: a median of 69 sessions. A falling but still high VIX was normal in the first leg of a recovery.

The VIX usually peaked before price did, and its level did not say what kind of decline it was

The VIX is the index of expected S&P 500 volatility over the next 30 days, taken from option prices. In multi-leg declines its highest close came one to seven months before the final price low. In single-shock declines the two coincided. The level reached did not separate corrections from bear markets.

Peak before the low

Of the 15 completed drops of 10% or more that began in 1990 or later in the study’s table, the VIX made its highest close 30 or more sessions before the final price low in seven: 1990 (34 sessions), 2000–02 (46), 2007–09 (72), 2010 (30), 2011 (39), 2015–16 (118) and 2022 (about 155). In each the final low came at a lower VIX: 49.7 on 9 March 2009 against 80.9 in November 2008, and 28.1 on 11 February 2016 against 40.7 in August 2015.

In seven others the peak fell within five sessions of the low: 1997, 1999, February 2018, December 2018, 2020, 2023 and 2025. In 1998 the highest reading came at the retest, after the closing low.

The usual reading of a lower VIX at a lower price is that forced selling is fading. The study’s rally table adds a caution. Among lows that undercut an earlier low, the VIX peak was lower than at the earlier low in 6 of 12 turns and also in 12 of 29 failed rallies. It described the type of decline. It did not identify the last low.

The VIX measures the speed of a decline more than its size. The 2022 bear market never closed above 36.5; a trader waiting for 45 as a sign of capitulation did not get it.

The curve: VIX above three-month VIX

Normally the three-month measure is higher than the 30-day one. When the 30-day VIX closes above it, the curve is inverted: more fear is priced now than later.

  • Inversion began before the low: 5 inverted sessions before it in February 2018, 15 to 27 before the lows of 2010, 2015–16, 2018, 2020 and 2025, 42 in 2011 and 153 in 2007–09.
  • The ratio’s highest reading came before the price low in most cases: 1.43 on 24 October 2008, 1.26 on 8 August 2011, 1.34 on 28 February 2020.
  • After the low the curve returned to normal within 0 to 9 sessions in seven of nine cases. It took 12 sessions in 2025 and 23 in 2020.
  • In 2022 the ratio never exceeded 1.02. In the 10.3% fall of 2023 it never inverted.

A separate calculation in the research file, on Cboe closes from September 2009 to November 2026: the curve was inverted on 7.6% of sessions. The S&P 500’s average gain over the following 21 sessions was 3.0% (72% of cases positive), against 1.1% (67%) for all sessions. Over 63 sessions it was 7.3% against 3.1%. Those inverted days come from about eight episodes, so the effective sample is small.

What published work says

  • From 1989 to 1999, high readings of the VIX, the put/call ratio and the Arms index were followed by above-average S&P futures returns over 10 to 30 days. Simon & Wiggins, 2001
  • Expected returns do not rise in proportion to volatility, so portfolios that take less risk when volatility is high earned better risk-adjusted returns. Moreira & Muir, 2017
  • The slope of the VIX curve predicts returns on volatility products themselves. Johnson, 2017 That is not evidence about the date of a stock-market low.

Rules of the form “VIX spikes, then closes back below a level”, and the volatility of the VIX itself, could not be checked against a tested study. not verified

What to take from it
  • In a decline with several legs, the highest fear came early. The final low was quieter.
  • An inverted curve marked the zone in which lows formed, and in which the largest down days also happened. It appeared weeks before the low.
  • Slow, policy-driven bear markets (2022) gave no volatility signal at all.
Limits Fifteen declines since 1990; the three-month series starts in 2007. The forward-return figures use overlapping days.

Capitulation gauges mark a zone, not a day

Heavy volume, 90% down days, extreme put buying, surges in new lows and bearish surveys all cluster near lows. Each has also appeared months early. The final low of a multi-leg decline usually came on ordinary volume, after these readings had peaked.

90% days

A 90% down day has nine-tenths of volume and of points in falling stocks. Paul Desmond of Lowry Research held that one such day means nothing, that declines with two or more tend to produce a series, and that the low is confirmed by a 90% up day or two 80% up days. One outside audit since 1960 counts 9 bottoms identified, 3 missed, 16 extra signals and 3 false ones. The Dow Theory report In the study’s data a 90% up day within ten sessions followed 10 of 13 turns and also 40 of 51 failed rallies.

Put/call ratios and the Arms index

The put/call ratio divides the volume of put options (bets on a fall) by call options. From 1989 to 1999, extremes in the put/call ratio, the Arms index (which compares the volume in falling stocks with their number) and the VIX were followed by above-average index returns over 10 to 30 days. Simon & Wiggins, 2001 A later study found the predictive part of option volume is the non-public part, who is opening which position; the published ratio adds little. Pan & Poteshman, 2006 In the study’s data the highest total put/call reading of the 2007–09 decline came on 6 October 2008, five months before the low; in 2018 it came two sessions before.

New lows

The common claim is that the number of stocks at new lows peaks before the index does. In the study’s data that held in the multi-leg declines: 69.0% of stocks at new lows in autumn 2008 against 31.4% on 9 March 2009; 34.8% in June 2022 against 15.5% on 12 October; 23.7% in January 2016 against 17.7% on 11 February. The Reformed Broker, 12 Feb 2016 In single-leg declines (1999, 2011, both 2018 drops, 2023, 2025) new lows peaked on the day of the low. Measured against the immediately preceding swing low, only 1 of 9 turns had fewer new lows, so the comparison depends on which earlier low is used.

Surveys

The share of bears in the weekly AAII survey of individual investors reached 70.3% on 5 March 2009, 67.0% on 19 October 1990, 61.9% on 3 April 2025 and 60.9% on 22 September 2022. It also reached 59% in January and March 2008, a year before the low, and only 52.1% at the March 2020 low. AAII’s own study of 1987–2013 found that unusually high bearishness (above 50.8%) was followed by a gain of only 2.8% over six months, because of the run of early signals in 2008. AAII The managers’ exposure survey (NAAIM) is described by its publisher as a record of positioning, not a forecast. NAAIM

Insider buying and fund flows

Surges in buying by company insiders are often cited at lows (November 2008, March 2009, August 2011, March 2020). not verified The academic test usually cited poses the question of whether aggregate insider trading predicts the market; its summary does not state the answer. Seyhun, 1988 For fund flows the evidence is that money follows prices: inflows go with gains in the same month, and 85% of that gain reverses within four months. Ben-Rephael, Kandel & Wohl, 2012 Record outflows mark heavy selling, not its end.

What to take from it
  • “No capitulation yet” was not a reason the low was not in: 2009, 2011, 2016 and 2022 all bottomed on ordinary volume.
  • Sentiment extremes opened a zone that lasted days in 2018 and 2025 and a year in 2008.
  • The combination seen at final lows of multi-leg declines was a lower price on a lower VIX and normal volume, well after the panic readings.
Limits No tested success rate was found for new-low divergences, insider buying or survey extremes as entry signals. The study’s breadth data cover today’s index members from 1998, which leaves out stocks that failed.

Breadth thrusts confirm a low; they do not time it

A breadth thrust is a burst in which nearly all stocks rise together for several days. The published record after such bursts is very good over six to twelve months. But they fire 10 to 63 sessions after the low, several real lows had none, and the known failures came while the Fed was still tightening.

What followed

  • Zweig, NYSE. Since 1962, no loss “of any magnitude” at any horizon, and no pullback over 9.2% within a year, per SentimenTrader. Other counts give 16 or 17 signals since the 1940s, all higher six and twelve months later. not verified
  • NYSE ten-day ratio. In SentimenTrader’s version (46 signals since 1932) the index was higher a year later after every signal since 1942.
  • S&P 1500 version. 35 signals in 52 years; the S&P 500 was higher a year later 97% of the time; all 8 signals that came after a 20% decline were higher at six and twelve months.
  • The study’s own test on the stocks it tracks since 1998: a ten-day advancing share above 61.5% appeared after 10 of 13 turns and 15 of 51 failed rallies. More than 55% of stocks at 20-day highs appeared after 4 of 13 turns and 1 of 51 failures. A 90% up day within ten sessions appeared after 10 of 13 turns and 40 of 51 failures, so it did not separate them.

The documented failures

  • 4 January 2022. The S&P 1500 version fired one session after the bull-market high. The index fell 25% over the next nine months.
  • December 2008 and 2 January 2009. The same version fired during a bear-market rally. The index fell another 27% from 2 January to 9 March 2009.
  • 28 July 2022. It fired in the summer rally. Price made a new low in October, though it was higher a year later.
  • Lowry’s sequence. One audit since 1960 counts 9 bottoms identified, 3 missed, 16 extra signals and 3 false ones.
  • Absent at real lows. No NYSE signal by Deemer’s rule in 1998, 2002–03 or 2011.

SentimenTrader’s comment on the January 2022 failure names the common factor: no indicator is infallible, “especially when Federal Reserve policy is unfriendly”.

What to take from it
  • A thrust said the advance was broad, after 10–25% of it had already happened. It was evidence about the next six to twelve months, not about the next week.
  • No rally inside the 2000–02 or 2007–09 bear markets produced an NYSE signal by Deemer’s rule. Narrow rallies were the ones that failed.
  • The failures came with the Fed tightening or a credit problem unresolved.

Credit spreads peaked with the stock market low more often than before it

A credit spread is the extra yield a corporate bond pays over a Treasury. Spreads widening means lenders are more afraid. In the declines studied, spreads usually peaked on or near the day stocks bottomed. They confirmed the low; they rarely announced it.

Why the difference

  • 1998 and 2018. The problem was liquidity and positioning. Profits were flat to rising and no recession followed. Easier policy removed the cause.
  • 2001 and 2007. The cut answered a collapse in profits and credit that had only begun. Profits fell for two years. The cut did not remove the cause; it was a sign of it.
  • 2020. Rate cuts did nothing for three weeks. The low came when the Fed backed corporate credit directly.
  • 1994, 2004, 2006, 2022. Stocks bottomed while increases were still under way, before any pivot.

This reading is an inference from nine cases. A statement that can be tested: a pivot was followed by a low within weeks when earnings estimates were not falling and credit spreads had stopped widening.

Lows against the business cycle

Six of seven recession lows came before the recession ended: May 1970 (six months before), October 1974 (five), August 1982 (three), October 1990 (five), March 2009 (three), March 2020 (one). The exception was October 2002, eleven months after the recession ended. Earnings troughed after the price low in 1990–91, 2009, 2020 and 2022–23. News on jobs and profits was still getting worse when stocks turned.

What to take from it
  • The first cut was a reliable marker only in declines without a recession.
  • In recessions the low came about three to six months before the economy turned, with spreads at or just past their peak.
  • Spreads that were still widening argued against a low holding.
