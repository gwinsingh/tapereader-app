Train the eye

Thirty unlabeled charts. Each stops five sessions after a closing low inside a real S&P 500 drop: a pop is under way. Decide whether the low will hold, then step forward a day, a week or a month at a time, or reveal the rest.

The charts are daily candles of the S&P 500 with volume, the 9- and 20-day EMA and the 50- and 200-day SMA, as on the other pages. Dates are hidden and prices are rebased so that the last visible close is 100; the axis counts sessions from that day. About half of the thirty lows held and half failed, in random order. Nothing is scored: the point is to notice what the eye is drawn to and how often it is right.

Before revealing, it helps to say out loud: how far below the 200-day average is this? Is this the first low of the drop or a later one? What did volume do? Would a close above the 50-day change the view? Then compare with the base rates on Failed pops and the real turn: across 76 years the first five days of the rallies that held and of those that failed were nearly indistinguishable.

Limits Thirty cases drawn from drops since 1962; the same thirty every time the page is built from the same data. Knowing that half held is itself a clue a trader never has. The drill trains recognition of what a low looks like on the fifth day; it cannot supply the context (the Fed, earnings, breadth) that the other pages show mattered more.
