Do look-alike charts mean anything?

A chart of today laid over a chart of 2018 looks convincing. This page tests how much such a match has been worth: across 95 past declines, the paths that looked most alike said nothing about what came next.

The claim: “this looks like 2018”

Both charts start from an early-autumn record high in a midterm-election year with the Fed raising rates. Laid over each other from their highs, they do look alike. The first question is how alike, measured rather than judged by eye.

Measured from the peak, the first 33 sessions of the two declines have a correlation of 0.54 (1.00 would be identical shape). That puts 2018 at rank 53 of 95 among all past declines of 5% or more: an ordinary match, not a close one. The eye is more generous than the arithmetic because both lines go down and both wiggle.

If the 2018 chart is allowed to slide in time until it fits best, the best-fitting 33-session stretch of 2018 runs from 5 Nov 2018 to 24 Dec 2018 (correlation 0.86). That is how most overlay charts are made: the old chart is shifted, and often stretched, until it fits. A fit found that way is certain to look good.

The test: did look-alikes forecast anything?

For each past decline, pretend it is the present: find the five other declines whose first sessions match it best, average what those five did over the next 60 sessions, and compare that “forecast” with what really happened.

A chart that matches the past closely says almost nothing about what comes next

Overlaying today’s price path on a famous crash is the most shared kind of market chart and the least reliable. A close match can always be found, because there are thousands of past windows to choose from. In a test on the S&P 500 since 1970, the best historical match predicted the next move worse than simply assuming prices rise.

The famous failures

  • 1929 over 2013–14. A chart laying the Dow of 1928–29 over 2012–14 spread widely in early 2014, promoted among others by the market timer Tom DeMark. No crash followed. The Reformed Broker, 13 Feb 2014
  • How it was made to fit. The two lines were drawn on different vertical scales. On a common percentage scale the Dow had risen over 230% from 1925 to 1929 and 156% from 2009 to 2013; the shapes no longer matched. Krinsky, via The Reformed Broker, 29 Jan 2014
  • 1987. A 1920s overlay ran in a national newspaper on the morning of the 1987 crash, according to Robert Shiller, as cited by The Reformed Broker. It is the one case usually offered in the method’s favour.
  • 2008 over later declines. Overlays of 2008 circulated in 2011, 2016, 2018 and 2022. Who published which chart was not verified. The outcomes are known: those declines ended at −19.4%, −14.2%, −19.8% and −25.4%, against −56.8% in 2007–09.

Why a 0.9 correlation is weak evidence

  1. Search. With about 19,000 past windows to try, a good fit exists for any path. The fit measures how hard one looked.
  2. Trending series. Two unrelated series that each wander will often show a high correlation in their levels. Economists call this spurious correlation.
  3. Free choices. Start date, length, scale and stretching of the time axis are all chosen after seeing the data.
  4. Different causes. Two paths can look alike while the Fed, profits and credit are in opposite states.

A test

From the research file. For about 1,430 dates between 1970 and 2026 (every tenth session), the last 40 to 120 sessions of the S&P 500 were compared with every earlier window of the same length, and the best match was kept. Then the match’s next move was compared with what the index did.

  • A match correlating about 0.9 was available nearly every time.
  • Its next move agreed in direction with the outcome 53–57% of the time, less often than always saying “up”.
  • Matches above 0.95 did no better (54–59%). Averaging the ten best matches did not help.
  • The size of the match’s next move had no relation to the size of the outcome (correlations between −0.02 and +0.07).

The study’s own test, on a different design, agrees. Each past decline’s first 33 sessions were matched with the five most similar declines, and their average next 60 sessions were used as a forecast. The forecast’s correlation with the outcome was −0.04, and 0.07 using the single closest match. The direction was right in 65 of 95 cases, about the 67% of all 60-session windows that rose anyway.

One academic result sits beside this. Chart patterns defined by a fixed algorithm, on US stocks from 1962 to 1996, did carry some information: returns after a pattern were distributed differently from returns in general. Lo, Mamaysky & Wang, 2000 That concerns the odds after a defined pattern across thousands of cases. It does not support forecasting a path from one hand-picked resemblance.

Where analog thinking is useful

  • As a library of sequences. The profiles in this study show what has happened and in what order: where fear peaked, whether the low was retested, how long the base lasted.
  • As base rates conditioned on causes, not shapes. Declines during rate increases with rising profits (1994, 2004, 2006) ended differently from declines with a credit event (1998, 2011, 2020) or falling profits (2000, 2007, 2022).
  • As a list of what would have to be true. For a decline to follow 1998, a policy reversal has to be available. For it to follow 2022, the Fed has to keep tightening while profit forecasts fall.
  • As a range. The spread of outcomes across similar cases is information; the path of the single closest case is not.
What to take from it
  • A striking overlay is evidence that someone searched, not that history is repeating.
  • The questions that sorted past declines were about the Fed, profits and credit, not about the shape of the chart.
Limits The test uses one simple matching method on one index. It does not test matching on volume, breadth or economic conditions.
What to take from it
  • Measured from the peak, 2018 is an ordinary match for the present chart (rank 53 of 95). It becomes a striking match only when it is shifted to fit.
  • Across 95 past declines, the paths that looked most alike had no forecasting value for the following 60 sessions (correlation -0.04).
  • The useful part of an analog is not the line drawn to the right. It is the list of things that happened in that year: what the Fed did, what broke, what turned the market. Those are covered on 2018, day by day, Now against 2018 and 2000–02 and 2007–09.
  • A look-alike is a scenario to prepare for, not a forecast. The same present chart also resembles the first weeks of 1961, 1973, 2007, 1968.
Limits The test uses closing prices of one index and one window length (33 sessions, the age of the present decline). Other lengths give the same answer in kind (correlations near zero) but the exact numbers move. The test says nothing about analogs built on fundamentals; Now against 2018 looks at those.
