Reaction to news

Was the market's reaction to news a reliable guide, in 2018 and now? The evidence is of two kinds: counted (how stocks were treated after earnings beats and misses) and anecdotal (days when good news was sold or bad news bought). Both are set out here with their limits.

The idea being examined is old: in a falling market good news is sold, and near the end bad news stops making new lows. If true and visible in real time it would be useful. The difficulty is that every day has both good and bad news, so examples can always be found for either case after the fact. The counted evidence is therefore worth more than the anecdotes.

The reaction to earnings flipped between October and February, and it can be measured

In the reporting season of October 2018, companies that beat estimates fell. In the season of January and February 2019, companies that missed barely fell. The low on 24 December sits between the two. The reaction to news confirmed the turn; it did not time it.

The October reading was the worst reaction to beats since the second quarter of 2011. The results themselves were strong: 78% of companies beat, and earnings grew 25.9%, the most since 2010. FactSet checked the obvious explanations and rejected them: guidance was no worse than usual and analysts were cutting estimates less than usual. In the next season the results were weaker (69% beat) and the reaction was twice as kind as average.

What to take from it
  • The price reaction to beats and misses is published weekly during each reporting season, so the reading was available at the time.
  • It turned negative two months before the low and positive only after it.

From October to 24 December, good news was sold

Each piece of good news produced at most a few days of gains. The pattern held for data, for earnings, for the trade truce and for the Fed’s first softer words.

The Fed’s own record explains 19 December. Market contacts told the Fed that its December message was read as not appreciating how much financial conditions had tightened, and as inflexible on the balance sheet Fed minutes, Jan 2019. The good part of the news, fewer rate rises, was not the part the market cared about.

From 26 December, bad news was bought

The test came on 2 and 3 January 2019, six sessions after the low, with two pieces of plainly bad news. The index fell 2.5% and held 4.1% above the low. One day later it was higher than before the news.

Verdict: reliable as confirmation, unreliable as a signal for the low

The reaction to news told the truth about the trend while the trend lasted. It did not say when the trend would end, and it gave at least three false positives on the way down.

Where it worked

  • Good news being sold was visible from 11 October and measurable from 26 October. It stayed that way for two months.
  • After the low, the 3 January response to the Apple warning and the ISM report was the first time in the episode that clearly bad news failed to make a new low.
  • The earnings-reaction numbers of the next season agreed.

Where it misled

  • 23 Oct: the Dow recovered from −549 to −126 after the Caterpillar and 3M warnings. The index fell 3.1% the next day.
  • 6 Dec: the Dow recovered from −785 to −79 after the Huawei arrest. It lost 2.3% the next day.
  • 10 Dec: the index broke its October low and closed higher. The reversal failed four sessions later and the index went on to the 24 December low.

What set 3 January apart was not the reversal itself. It came after a 5.0% day on 26 December, at a higher low, with the VIX ten points lower, and it was followed within one session by a second broad advance and by a change in the Fed’s language. No dated practitioner quotation saying “the market has stopped going down on bad news” for that day was found not verified; the behaviour is documented, the phrase is not.

Limits One bad-news reversal did not mark the low in October or in early December. The evidence here is a single episode, read with hindsight.

Since the high, good news has been sold in the leaders

From 6 October to 20 November 2026, strong reports from the largest and most-owned companies were met with selling. Weak reports from ordinary companies were punished less than usual. The pattern says more about who owned what than about the news itself.

“Second day worse”

Twice a large fall came the day after a session that looked like relief. The largest fall of all came the day after a 2% loss.

  • 4 Nov: S&P 500 +1.58% on the election result. 5 Nov: −4.36%.
  • 18 Nov: −0.23%, with the Nasdaq up and Nvidia’s results out of the way. 19 Nov: −2.02%, after opening 1.3% higher.
  • 19 Nov: −2.02%, the sixth loss in a row. 20 Nov: −3.70%.

Gap up, then fade

Three of the last six sessions opened higher and closed lower: 13, 17 and 19 November. The study’s price data show the wider pattern. Over the 33 sessions from the top, the sum of overnight moves (prior close to open) was 0.0% and the sum of moves during regular hours was −11.4%. All of the fall happened while the market was open. In the 64 sessions before the top it was the other way round: +5.2% overnight and −1.0% during the day.

What to take from it
  • The selling of good news was concentrated in the stocks that had risen most. Outside them, reactions to earnings were normal or better until 18 November.
  • Relief days were followed by large falls twice in three weeks, and a 2% fall was followed by a larger one.
  • Strength appeared overnight and at the open; the selling came during the session.
  • Bonds began to act as a shelter only on the worst days. Gold and bitcoin gave none.
Limits Reads of this kind deserve modest weight. The tables hold a dozen hand-picked cases from six weeks, chosen after the fact; cases that did not fit were easy to leave out. The reason given for a move is a journalist’s summary written that afternoon, and the same news is often cited for opposite moves. “Good news sold” describes most declines once they are under way, including ones that ended days later, and the 9–13 November rally showed how quickly the pattern can pause. What the record does support is narrower: the FactSet reaction figures and the overnight-versus-session split are measured across all companies and all days, not selected.

“It stopped falling on bad news”: often true at lows, never tested as a rule

Traders have long read the market’s reaction to news as a sign of exhaustion. The declines in this study give clear examples on both sides. No study was found that defines the signal and counts how often it worked, so it remains an observation made with hindsight.

The one measured version: reaction to earnings

FactSet measures the average price change from two days before a company’s earnings report to two days after, separately for results above and below forecasts, and compares it with a five-year average. In the reporting season of autumn 2026 (91% of S&P 500 companies reported by 13 November), results above forecasts were followed by +1.1% against a five-year average of +0.9%, and results below forecasts by −1.1% against −3.1%. The seven largest companies were the exception: −0.2% after beats and −7.8% after misses. FactSet, 13 Nov 2026

No test was found of this asymmetry as a signal for the index. not verified as a timing tool.

In three of the four, good news was sold within two weeks before the final low. Selling of good news appeared near the end of a decline as often as in its middle.

How reliable

  • Each example was chosen knowing where the low was. Reversal days also occurred inside every long decline and failed.
  • The study’s rally table gives a rough check. The median low-day close was in the bottom fifth of the day’s range at turns (0.20) and at failed rallies alike (0.15). The low day itself did not usually close strongly.
  • A version that could be counted: an intraday 20-day low, a close in the top quarter of the day’s range and above the prior close, on a day with scheduled bad news. No such count was found.
What to take from it
  • At four of the lows studied the index closed higher on bad news. That was visible on the day; what it meant was visible only later.
  • The reaction to policy help was a poor guide: the first Fed action was sold in 1998 and 2011, days before the low.
  • The evidence is anecdote. It works as a tie-breaker when other gauges already say “zone”, not as a signal.
Limits Eight hand-picked days. No base rate.

How much weight this deserves

A summary across the two episodes and the research notes.

  • The counted evidence is real and it flipped at the 2018 low: companies that beat estimates were sold on average during the autumn 2018 reporting season and rewarded in the season after the low. That statistic is published weekly and was available at the time.
  • It is a slow gauge. It moves with reporting seasons, four times a year, and the flip in early 2019 was visible only in late January, a month after the low.
  • Single days are weak evidence. The best-known “bad news bought” day of the 2018 low (the Apple warning, 3 January 2019) was a 2.5% fall that held above the low; it looked like confirmation only after the next day's rally.
  • In the present drop the pattern is split: megacap and AI news has been sold, while the average company's results were treated kindly until mid-November. That fits the sector and breadth picture on the other pages.
  • As a filter for longs, reaction to news is best treated as one input among several, with the breadth and price evidence carrying more weight because they can be counted the same way every time.
Limits The day-by-day lists are selected by hindsight and by what the press chose to explain. The index move on a “news day” reflects everything that happened that day. Nothing on this page was tested as a signal.
