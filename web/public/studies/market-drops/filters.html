Moving averages as a filter

Would staying out of longs while the index was under a moving average have helped? What it cost and what it saved, in drops since 1950, shown rather than back-tested: no entries, exits or sizing are simulated here.

“Inside a drop (peak to low)” is defined with hindsight (the low is only known later) and is shown to make one point: while a drop was still running, a close back above the 50-day average did not improve the following month.

What the table shows
  • Below the 200-day average the average month was not much worse, but the spread was far wider: the worst 5% of months and the average dip inside the month were roughly twice as deep. The average hides this because the biggest up months also happen there.
  • The moving average did not predict direction. It marked the zone where both the largest falls and the largest rallies happened.
  • Under the 200-day, being back above the 50-day or the 20-day made the next month calmer but did not make it clearly better.

“Sessions after” counts from the closing low. The gain is the index's rise from the low to that close.

  • Median across all 35 drops since 1950: back above the 20-day EMA after 4 sessions (+5.0% off the low), above the 50-day after 12 sessions (+7.1%), above the 200-day after 22 sessions (+9.8%).
  • The cost was highest after the deepest and fastest falls, because the averages had the furthest to come down: +16.7% in early 2019, +35.7% in 2020, +39.4% in 2009.
  • Set against the first-year gain in the last column, waiting for the 200-day still left most of the first year in every case, and waiting for the 50-day left almost all of it.

Short, sharp drops gave almost no false reclaims (there was no time). The long ones gave many: the 50-day average was regained and lost 19 times in 2000–02 and 15 times in 2007–09.

Put together: in a fast drop the averages are late but not misleading; in a slow bear they are misleading again and again at the shorter lengths, and only the 200-day stays mostly clean. That fits what the Failed pops and the real turn page found from the other side: 67% of failed rallies got back above the 50-day, 41% above the 200-day.
