Leverage and positioning

How much borrowed money was in the market at each peak, how fast it came out during the drop, and where it stands now. Hard numbers exist for margin debt; hedge-fund and systematic leverage is known only from regulators' and dealers' reports, which the research notes below summarise.

Quarter-end figures, so the timing is coarse: the reading “at the peak” is the last quarter-end on or before the index high, published about ten weeks later.

What the numbers say
  • Margin loans stood at 2.28% of GDP in the latest quarter (Q2 2026), after growing +47% in a year. At the September 2018 high they were 1.45% of GDP and had grown +16%; at the March 2000 high 2.64% and +65%; at the October 2007 high 1.78%; at the end of 2021 2.06% and +10%.
  • On this measure the build-up before the present drop sits between 2018 and 2000: faster and from a much higher level than in 2018, slower than the last months before March 2000.
  • In every drop the borrowed money came out as prices fell; the flush was a symptom of the decline and an amplifier of it, and its end coincided with lows only loosely.

Leverage was at or near records going into the fall

Borrowing against shares reached a record in June 2026 and was still 22% higher than a year earlier in October. The Fed reported the highest hedge-fund leverage since its data began in 2013. How much of this has been unwound in November is not yet known: the data arrive with a lag.

Leveraged funds, retail and flows: thin evidence, no sign of a washout

The few numbers available show leveraged funds still holding tens of billions, individual investors selling on the worst day, and surveys less fearful on 18 November than in September.

Leveraged funds

  • TQQQ, a fund that aims for three times the Nasdaq-100’s daily move, held $28.0bn on 19 November. It lost 20.5% in October ProShares, 19 Nov 2026.
  • On 20 November the Nasdaq-100 fell 5.61% and the chip index 10.73%. Three times those moves is about −17% and −32% in one day, before costs.
  • SOXL, the three-times chip fund, fell 11% on 13 November alone The Glass Desk, 13 Nov 2026. Its assets and flows were not found.

Individual investors and fund flows

  • 5 November: individuals were net sellers of about $358m by mid-afternoon, the most since March 2020, by Vanda Research’s count The Next Web, 6 Nov 2026.
  • 19 November: individuals traded both sides of the Anthropic listing and sold other stocks, AMD most of all, to pay for it Yahoo Finance, 19 Nov 2026.
  • Week to 11 November: US equity funds took in $6.6bn and money-market funds $37.5bn Investomine, 13 Nov 2026.
  • 16 November, one day: $1.73bn into the chip fund SMH and $3.16bn out of the chip fund SOXX Capp Notes, 19 Nov 2026.

Credit

The extra yield on high-yield company bonds over Treasuries was 3.47% on 18 November, close to its one-year high of 3.48% and up from 3.20% on 13 November. The high-yield bond fund HYG fell only 0.7% from 6 October to 20 November. Stress showed in single names, not in the market as a whole: CoreWeave, Intel and Meta each saw the cost of insuring their debt reach a record between 22 October and 5 November.

What could not be found

  • Prime-broker figures (Goldman Sachs, Morgan Stanley) for hedge-fund gross and net leverage in October and November.
  • Estimates for trend-following funds, volatility-targeting funds and risk-parity funds.
  • Any named fund failure or forced closure.
  • CNN Fear & Greed, put/call ratios, Investors Intelligence, and the NAAIM manager survey (published with a three-month delay).
  • The share of option volume in same-day contracts.
  • Flows into and out of leveraged funds; company buyback activity and blackout dates.
  • Fund flows after 11 November, and any margin-debt figure after October.
What to take from it
  • Margin debt peaked four months before the index did, at a level well above 2018’s relative to the economy.
  • Desks described overall positioning as light and chip positioning as crowded. The price data fit that: chips −36.7%, the equal-weight index −2.3%.
  • Credit markets had barely reacted by 18 November.
Limits Most of the evidence here is either months old (hedge-fund leverage as of March) or a one-line summary of a bank note. The gaps listed above are the measures that normally show whether forced selling has run its course.

Hedge funds were at post-crisis highs in leverage and crowded into the same stocks

The Federal Reserve flagged hedge-fund leverage in November 2018 and reported in May 2019 that it had come down late in the year. The most popular hedge-fund stocks fell more than the market. Exact leverage figures from prime brokers (the bank units that lend to hedge funds) were not found.

  • Fed, November 2018: some indicators put hedge-fund leverage at post-crisis highs; a broad measure rose by about one-third over 2016 and 2017 Financial Stability Report, Nov 2018.
  • Fed, May 2019: gross leverage rose through early 2018 and fell at the end of the year as volatility rose, most clearly at equity funds Financial Stability Report, May 2019.
  • Crowding: Goldman Sachs’s basket of the most popular hedge-fund holdings lagged the S&P 500 by more than seven percentage points from mid-June to November 2018, and the 50 most popular stocks made up 32% of hedge-fund long positions. These figures come from press summaries of bank notes whose pages could not be opened not verified.
  • Goldman Sachs and Morgan Stanley prime-brokerage net and gross leverage for October–December 2018: not verified, no figures found.
  • Results: the HFRI composite index of hedge funds lost 4.7% in 2018, its worst year since 2011 CMC Markets. On Preqin’s count 59% of funds lost money, and 746 funds closed against 609 opened Chief Investment Officer. Clients withdrew $22.5bn in the fourth quarter Bloomberg, 18 Jan 2019.

The crowded positions were the 2017–18 leaders. Nvidia lost 54% in the fourth quarter, the worst stock in the S&P 500 CNBC, 21 Dec 2018. In the study’s universe, the fifth of stocks that had risen most in the year before the high (median +62%) fell most in the decline (median −23.6%).

Rule-driven selling and thin order books made each leg faster

Several kinds of funds sell automatically when volatility rises or trends break. Their selling met an order book with a third of its usual depth. Bank estimates of the amounts differ widely and should be treated as rough.

Systematic strategies

  • 10 Oct: JPMorgan’s Kolanovic named option hedging by dealers as the largest forced seller of the day, said trend-following funds had largely finished, and expected volatility-targeting funds (which cut holdings as volatility rises) to sell for several more days. He judged systematic selling about 70% complete on 12 October Bloomberg, 12 Oct 2018.
  • 4 Dec: a Nomura strategist estimated that trend-following funds added about $42bn of exposure on the 3 December rally and cut it to a fifth the next day. A Nomura colleague and other banks disputed this AFR excerpt, 6 Dec 2018.
  • S&P 500 option open interest reached a record above 21 million contracts in December S&P Dow Jones Indices, 27 Dec 2018.

Liquidity

  • Goldman Sachs, 18 Dec: the depth of the S&P 500 futures order book was down 70% in a year, to a ten-year low Fortune, 30 Dec 2018.
  • JPMorgan, March 2019: depth in the fourth quarter was less than a third of the average of other selloffs of the decade, so the same order moved the price about three times as far, in both directions Bloomberg, 21 Mar 2019.
  • The Fed’s minutes: the late-December selling was amplified by poor liquidity and thin year-end trading Fed minutes, Jan 2019.

Year-end flows: record fund outflows, tax selling, then pension buying

December’s selling had a calendar component. Investors sold mutual funds and losing stocks before year-end, partly for tax reasons, and pension funds had to buy stocks in the last days of the quarter.

  • Week to 12 Dec: a record $46.2bn left US stock mutual funds and exchange-traded funds (ETFs) together CNBC, 14 Dec 2018.
  • Week to 19 Dec: long-term mutual funds lost $56.2bn, the most since October 2008 InvestmentNews.
  • December in full: stock and mixed mutual funds −$115.4bn, the most since at least January 2008; money-market funds +$63.1bn; but ETFs +$50.1bn Lipper, Jan 2019. Part of the mutual-fund outflow was a switch into ETFs, which is how a tax loss is booked without leaving the market.
  • Tax-loss selling: DataTrek estimated about $41bn in December against about $25bn of offsetting ETF purchases, accelerating from 14 December once a down year looked likely Yahoo Finance, 19 Jan 2019.
  • Pension rebalancing (funds restoring their stock share after stocks fell): Wells Fargo estimated about $60bn of stock buying into year-end, $35bn of it large companies. The late-day reversal of 27 December was attributed to it Fortune, 28 Dec 2018.
  • Buybacks: S&P 500 companies bought back a record $223bn of their shares in the fourth quarter S&P Dow Jones Indices, 25 Mar 2019. The dates of the pre-earnings blackout windows, often cited for early October, are not verified.
  • Small option traders bought nearly 3 million puts in the week before 24 December, the second-highest on record, and insiders bought their own shares at the highest rate relative to sales since August 2011 SentimenTrader, 24 Dec 2018 Bloomberg, 26 Dec 2018.

What to take from it
  • Leverage had been cut hard by the low: margin debt −17% from its peak, record fund outflows, hedge-fund leverage reduced.
  • Three flows reversed at the calendar turn: tax selling ended on 31 December, pension buying arrived in the last week, and forced selling by rule-driven funds stops when volatility stops rising.
  • The recovery was not built on re-borrowing. Prices recovered first; leverage lagged by many months.
Limits Positioning data are estimates. Single-bank figures for systematic selling were disputed at the time, and the prime-broker leverage numbers are missing here.

Borrowed money peaked with or before the index, and forced selling marked the worst weeks

Margin debt (money investors borrow from brokers against shares) rose 54% in the five months to March 2000 and peaked in the month of the index peak. In 2007 it peaked in July, three months before the index. The 2007–09 bear added leverage of a different order: investment banks with up to 40 dollars of assets per dollar of capital, funded overnight. Each of the worst stretches was a forced unwinding.

2007: leverage in the lenders themselves

  • The five large investment banks ran assets of up to 40 times capital in 2007. Bear Stearns had $11.8bn of equity against $383.6bn of liabilities and borrowed up to $70bn overnight. Goldman Sachs went from 17:1 in 2000 to 32:1 in 2007. The two mortgage agencies, Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac, stood at 75:1. Financial Crisis Inquiry Commission report
  • The funding was short-term. Overnight loans against securities (repo) cleared through two banks grew from $800bn in 2002 to $2.8trn in early 2008.
  • Asset-backed commercial paper (short-term IOUs issued by off-balance-sheet vehicles) peaked at $1,226bn on 8 August 2007 and fell 37% by year-end.
  • A background case: the hedge fund Long-Term Capital Management had $4.7bn of equity against more than $124bn of borrowing at the start of 1998 and lost $4.6bn that year. Wikipedia, LTCM

What to take from it
  • Margin debt rose fastest just before each peak and turned down with or before the index.
  • In 2007–09 the decisive leverage was inside banks and funds, and it was unwound by lenders, not by choice.
  • A 2× fund lost 85% in a 55% bear. Its 49% gain in the largest rally was gone within two months.
  • In 2026 the Fed’s margin-loan series stood at 2.28% of GDP in the second quarter, up 47% in a year; the comparable reading was 2.64% in early 2000.
Limits Margin statistics are published weeks late and also fell in corrections (−14% from July to October 1998). No three-times fund existed for the first thirteen months of the 2007–09 bear, so its full path cannot be observed.

  • Growth above 50% a year preceded the tops of 2000, 2007 and 2021, by months and not by a fixed interval. In 2021 growth peaked seven months before the level did.
  • The bear-market lows came after margin debt had fallen 35–55%. The lows of shock declines (2020, 2025) came after falls of 9–17%.
  • The trough month in margin debt fell close to the stock low: the month-end just before it in 2002 and 2009, the month-end after it in 2020 and 2025, and two months after it in 2022. Published three weeks late, it could only confirm the unwinding.

The others

  • Hedge funds. Leverage of equity-strategy funds was 1.65 at its low in mid-2016, 3.30 at its high in mid-2025 and 2.73 in mid-2026. The Fed’s November 2026 report described hedge-fund leverage as the highest since the data began in 2013. No evidence was found that the series times turns.
  • AAII. Bears above 60% marked the lows of 1990, 2009, 2022 and 2025, and also October 2008 and February 2025, which were early. See the page on capitulation.
  • Fund flows. Inflows go with gains in the same month, and most of that gain reverses within four months. Ben-Rephael, Kandel & Wohl, 2012
  • NAAIM. Its publisher describes it as a record of positioning, not a forecast. Readings at the lows of 2020, 2022 and 2025 were not verified.
What to take from it
  • These series measure how much forced selling is possible, months ahead, and how much has happened, weeks behind.
  • A decline that ended with margin debt down under 10% had been a shock inside a bull market. The bear markets took a third to a half of it away.
  • In October 2026 margin debt was 3.6% below a record, after growth above 50%.
Limits Five cycles of margin data since 1997. The hedge-fund series covers one full cycle.
Limits The Fed's margin series is quarterly and arrives late; FINRA's monthly figures (in the research notes) are timelier but also lag by three to four weeks. Neither captures leverage taken through options, futures, swaps, leveraged ETFs or private credit, which is where much of today's leverage sits. Hedge-fund leverage is reported with a lag of months and in aggregate. A high reading says the system is fragile, not when it breaks.
