Hand-kept breadth gauges, tested

Many traders keep a daily tally of breadth: how many stocks are above their 10-day, 20-day and 50-day averages, and how many moved 4% up or down. Tested on every drop since 1999, the washout readings of these gauges turned out to be a real mark of where lows form, with no timing; the surges and the 4% up-days were as common after failed rallies as after real lows; a washout followed by a broad surge was the best combination found.

What was tested

Seven tallies, computed every day from March 1999 on this study's universe of about 660 large stocks.

  • Above the 10-day EMA, the 20-day EMA, the 50-day average: the share of stocks closing above each of their own averages.
  • Up 4% / down 4%: the share of stocks that closed 4% or more above, or below, the previous close. Also the 10-day ratio: the sum of the last ten days' 4%-up shares divided by the sum of the 4%-down shares.
  • Down 25% in a quarter: the share of stocks 25% or more below their close of 65 sessions earlier.
  • Each gauge is asked the same three questions: what followed its washout readings, what followed its surges out of a washout, and whether it looked different at lows that held and at lows that later broke.
Before comparing with a hand-kept tally The universe is large stocks only (today's S&P 500 and Nasdaq-100 members and other widely traded names). A tally kept on all US stocks contains thousands of small ones that move 4% far more often, so its counts and thresholds are not the same as the percentages here; the shape of the readings is what carries over. Companies that later failed are missing, which flatters 2000–02 and 2008.

What to take from it
  • The deep washouts were followed by better-than-usual returns. After the first day with fewer than 10% of stocks above their 50-day average (16 times), the S&P 500 was higher 60 sessions later in 88% of cases, median +9.9%, against 67% and +3.0% for any day. Fewer than 5% above the 20-day EMA gave 88% and +7.3%.
  • They did not time the low. Of those 16 readings, 6 came within ten sessions of a final low and 10 came inside a drop that still had further to go. The worst case lost another 32% within 60 sessions (October 2008).
  • The slower the average, the cleaner the reading. The 50-day and 20-day versions gave the best odds. The 10-day version fires often (95 times under 15%) and mostly outside drops, where it means little; its extreme (under 5%) was right 60 sessions later only 59% of the time.
  • A day with a third of stocks down 4% (28 times) behaved like the other washouts on average (+8.0% over 60 sessions) with the widest spread of all: worst further fall 39%.
  • None of these readings occurred at a market top or in a calm market: the extreme ones happened only inside drops. As a statement of “this is the zone where lows form”, they held up.

What to take from it
  • By themselves the surges were ordinary. After the 10-day EMA share went from under 15% to over 80% (55 times) the S&P 500's median change over the next 60 sessions was +3.1%, the same as after any day, and the prior low held 29 times of 55.
  • A big 4%-up day was no proof of a low. A day with 35% or more of stocks up 4% happened 29 times; 15 of them were inside a drop still running, and the prior low held only 13 times of 29. The largest such days sit in bear-market rallies: 3 January 2001, 24 July 2002, 16 July and 18 September 2008.
  • The 10-day ratio turning from under 0.5 to over 2 was the steadiest of the group (low held 47 of 73; median +4.3% over 60 sessions), still close to the any-day figure.
  • Over a year the picture is better (higher in 85% to 83% of cases against 76% for any day), which mostly reflects that surges happen after falls and markets recover.

What to take from it
  • The plain versions barely moved the odds. A washout at the low, a 4%-down climax before it, a 4%-up surge after it, the ratio back over 2: each was present at about half or more of the lows that later broke.
  • Strength mattered more than occurrence. Raising the bar (20-day EMA share to 80%, 50-day share to 75%, ratio to 3) roughly doubled the share that were final, to 36–40%.
  • Washout plus surge was the best reading found: a 50-day share at or under 15% at the low and at or over 75% within 30 sessions appeared at 8 of 12 final lows and 6 of 51 that broke, so 57% of its appearances were final lows against a base rate of 19%. It was still wrong 6 times, and it takes up to six weeks to complete.
  • The ratio reaching 2 was close to a necessary condition (it followed 12 of 12 final lows) and far from a sufficient one.

Only the counts of large moves have an author and exact rules

The counts of 4% days and 25% quarters on a hand-kept breadth sheet (breadth: how many stocks take part in a move) come from one published monitor with exact scan rules. The share of stocks above a moving average is a vendor series. Its 10-day EMA (exponential moving average) form has no author and no published levels.

The published record is good at six to twelve months and thin at the low itself

The claim is that a washout (an extreme low reading) marks a bounce or a bottom, and that a ratio above 2 confirms the new advance. Published tests support the long horizon. None was found for the ratio, the count bands or the quarter count.

  • The author’s evidence is dated examples. In mid-2010 the count of stocks up 25% in a quarter fell to 130; a rally began in September. On 8 August 2011 he wrote that such readings led to a bounce within one to six weeks. The study dates that final low to 3 October 2011. Stockbee, 8 Aug 2011
  • Share above the 50-day below 20%, 2002–2023. 56 periods; the S&P 500 rose during 42. Median length 3.5 sessions. Seven lasted ten sessions or more and one of those showed a gain; the worst ran 48 sessions to 4 December 2008 (index −20.8%). Dr. Duru
  • Ten-day average of that share below 10%. The index was higher two weeks later in 50% of cases, six months later in 83% and a year later in all. The number of cases is not verified. SentimenTrader, 11 Jun 2021
  • Share above the 10-day average from under 2% to over 88% in four sessions. Higher two months later after 36 of 40 signals since 1947. The article marked a signal on 24 June 2022; the index made its low on 12 October. SentimenTrader, 27 Jun 2022
  • The one test of a 4% count. On 14 November 2023 about 25% of 6,300 stocks rose 4% or more. After earlier surges that large the index was higher three, six and twelve months later every time; the 2011 case first lost up to 9.8%. The count of cases is not given. SentimenTrader, 15 Nov 2023
Limits Small samples, overlapping signals, no test on later data. Most results come from firms that sell the data. One academic paper on breadth measures could not be opened: not verified

A count from 6,300 stocks and a percentage of 660 do not share levels

The study measures about 660 large stocks. Traders who keep such a monitor count all US common stocks above a low trading minimum. Small stocks move 4% more often, so the same percentage is a rarer event in the study’s data.

  • Scale. Against 6,300 stocks, 300 is 4.8%, 500 is 7.9% and 1,000 is 15.9%. The universe’s size in 2011, when the bands were written, is not verified. In the study, 16.2% of stocks down 4% is the worst 5% of days.
  • Volume. The hand count needs volume above the prior day’s. A plain share of stocks down 4% does not.
  • Quarter. The hand count measures from the 65-session low or high. Its washout rule watches the count of stocks up 25%; the study’s rows use the share down 25%.
  • Membership. The study applies today’s index members to the past, which softens old washouts.
What to take from it
  • A level from one universe says nothing in the other.
  • A reading’s rank within its own history is comparable. So is the order of events: washout first, surge later.
