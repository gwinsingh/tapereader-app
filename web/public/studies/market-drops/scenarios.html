Correction or bear: two paths

Two kinds of path lead on from a 10% fall: a correction that ends within a few months, and a bear market that takes a year or more. This page describes what each kind has looked like from this point, so that either can be recognised as it unfolds. It ranks no outcome as likely.

The November 2026 readings, grouped

  • Like the start of a bear: other indices below their highs at the record; a Federal Reserve that is raising rates; margin debt that rose fast and peaked before the index; heavy new supply of shares and bonds; first cuts to capex plans in the leading sector; a fall concentrated in the former leaders while the average stock holds, as in spring 2000.
  • Like a correction: the Baa spread has barely moved; banks are not tightening loan standards; jobless claims are at their low.
  • Neutral or uninformative: the yield curve (inverted in 2024–25, positive now); earnings forecasts (rising, as in spring 2000); valuation (far below 2000 on forward earnings, near it on CAPE); depth and speed; the 200-day average; volatility; consensus forecasts.

The same mix of readings (tightening Fed, high CAPE, divergent leadership, calm credit and labour data, rising forecasts) was present in spring 2000 and in January 2022. In 2000 the credit and labour confirmation arrived six to nine months later. In 2022 the labour confirmation never came and the decline ended at −25.4%. In 1998 the Fed cut within ten weeks and the decline ended at −19.3%.

What to take from it
  • The gauges that separated the two great bears from corrections were credit, bank lending, jobless claims and the behaviour of former leaders on rallies. All of them confirmed after the peak, not before.
  • The gauges visible before the peak (leadership divergence, yield curve, valuation, margin debt) were present in the bears but also gave false signals.
  • Depth, speed and the first break of the 200-day average said nothing.
Limits Eight episodes. The corrections were not researched for events here, only measured. The 1973 yield curve is not in the data used. The page describes readings and does not say which case 2026 is.

Midterm years had deeper falls and strong rebounds; part of the rebound figure is hindsight

The second year of a presidential term (the midterm-election year: 2018, 2022, 2026) has seen larger declines than other years, and the twelve months after the year’s low have been strong. Both statements check out on S&P 500 closes since 1950. The size of the rebound is overstated by the way it is measured.

The mild ones: 1954, 1958, 1986, 1994, 2006, 2014, all under 10%.

The caveat

  • The low is known only afterwards. Measured from the lowest close of any year, the next twelve months look good: +21.5% on average in non-midterm years too. The midterm difference is about ten points, not thirty.
  • Nineteen cases. Six of them had a fall under 10%.
  • The month varied. The midterm-year low came in October five times (1966, 1974, 1990, 2002, 2022), in January five times, and at least once in every month from February to August, plus December 2018.

The month of major lows

October appears often among the lows in this study: 1957, 1966, 1974, 1987 (the crash low), 1990, 1998 (intraday), 2002, 2011, 2022. Others came in December (1987 close, 2018), March (2009, 2020), February (2016), April (2025), May (1970), June (1962) and August (1982). A trading almanac’s label for October, the month that ends bear markets, fits about half the list. Its own count was not verified.

2026 so far

To 20 November 2026 the year’s lowest close was 6343.72 on 30 March. The record close was 7818.93 on 6 October, and the 20 November close of 6910.39 is 11.6% below it. That is the year’s largest fall so far, smaller than the midterm-year average.

What to take from it
  • A fall of 15% or more within a midterm year was ordinary: it happened in 11 of 19.
  • The calendar gave a tendency for the year, not a date for the low.
Limits Calendar patterns have no agreed cause, and nineteen observations cannot separate a real effect from chance.

What would tell the two apart from here

The markers below are the ones that differed most between the two kinds of path in the record. They are things to observe, in rough order of when they appeared.

  • Whether the first real rally holds. In corrections the first or second rally of 5%+ was the low. Of the drops with two or more failed rallies, 10 of 17 went to 20% or more, against 11 of 35 overall (count).
  • Whether a washout is followed by a broad surge. The lows of every drop of 18% or more since 1999 came with fewer than 21% of stocks above their 200-day average, and most real turns were followed within three weeks by a breadth thrust (breadth).
  • Whether the former leaders stop making relative lows. In 2018 semiconductors and software stopped underperforming one to two months before the index low and led afterwards; in 2000–02 technology made relative lows to the end (Sectors and themes).
  • Whether the average stock joins the fall. So far it has not. In 2000–02 it never fully did; in 2008 it did, completely.
  • Whether credit spreads widen past about one point. They widened 1.8 to 4.3 points in 2000–02, 2008–09 and 2020 and 1.1 or less in every other drop since 1987, the 2022 bear included (credit).
  • Whether earnings estimates start to fall. They are rising now. In 2000 they peaked about six months after the index.
  • Whether inflation gives the Fed room. The 1998 and 2018 drops ended with a Fed that could stop. In 2022 it could not, and the drop lasted until inflation turned.
  • What the 200-day average's slope does. It was still rising at every correction low and had been falling for months at every bear low.
Limits The two columns are built from a handful of episodes each, sorted by an outcome known only afterwards. Real drops mix features of both: 2022 had a correction's breadth and a bear's length; 1987 had a crash's depth and a correction's economy. The tally of today's readings is a description, not a score to be added up.
