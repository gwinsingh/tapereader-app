Bill Ackman: ten positions and capital that cannot leave

Ackman's audited record is about 16% a year since 2004 from roughly ten large positions and a few cheap hedges, but it includes four losing years in a row, and the listed fund has trailed the S&P 500 since its own launch in 2012.

Bill Ackman runs Pershing Square, which puts almost all of its money into eight to twelve large companies and sometimes presses their managements to change course. His main fund is a listed company with audited accounts, so the record can be checked: about 16% a year after fees from 2004 to 2025, against about 11% for the S&P 500. Inside that record are a 34% fall over four years, one stock that lost about $4bn, and two hedges that turned $446m into about $5.3bn. The one thing worth taking from him is that with ten positions the size of each position is the risk control, and that he survived his worst years because most of his capital could not be withdrawn.

The vocabulary used here is explained in the hedge fund equity primer. The profile of Michael Burry covers the other name in this group, and the method post explains how records are graded.

How he got started#

Ackman was born on 11 May 1966 in Chappaqua, New York. He took a degree at Harvard College in 1988 and an MBA at Harvard Business School in 1992 (Wikipedia). He then started a hedge fund (a private fund for wealthy investors and institutions that charges a share of profits) with a classmate, David Berkowitz.

Gotham Partners, 1993–2003#

Most accounts skip this firm. It explains much of what came later.

A regulatory filing from 1998 states that Ackman had co-managed the Gotham funds since 1 January 1993 (First Union proxy material, SEC). The seed capital is reported as $3m, from investors who included the publisher Marty Peretz (MarketFolly, July 2009, a secondary source; not verified). In 1995 Gotham bid for Rockefeller Center together with Leucadia National, an investment company. The bid lost, but the attention brought in money, and assets reached about $500m within three years (Wikipedia, above).

Gotham then did three things that recur in his career. It ran a proxy fight (a vote in which shareholders choose between the company’s board candidates and an outsider’s) at First Union Real Estate, a property trust, and Ackman became its chairman in June 1998. It published research on companies it was betting against: a bet against the mortgage agency Farmer Mac is said to have earned $75m (Commercial Observer, April 2011). And it put a large part of the fund into assets that could not be sold quickly, above all a golf-course operator.

That last choice ended the firm. Investors asked for their money, the golf business could not be turned into cash, and at the turn of 2002–2003 Gotham announced it would wind down its funds (Commercial Observer, above; the detail that a court blocked a merger of the golf company is widely repeated but was not verified here). The same profile says he resolved never to buy private companies again.

While Gotham was closing, Ackman published a long report titled “Is MBIA Triple-A?”. MBIA insured bonds and carried the highest credit rating, while holding about $1 of capital for every $140 of debt it guaranteed (Wiley, 26 Apr 2010). In 2003 the New York attorney general, Eliot Spitzer, and the Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC, the US markets regulator) investigated Gotham’s trading for about six months. No charges were brought. Spitzer said later: “we put him through the wringer” (Commercial Observer, above).

Pershing Square, 2004#

Pershing Square Capital Management began on 1 January 2004 with $54m, from Ackman and from Leucadia (Wikipedia, above; the annual report refers to a single outside investor in that first year). The first fund was a private partnership, Pershing Square, L.P.

The lesson of Gotham was about the match between assets and money. Assets that take years to sell had been financed with money that could leave in months. He met the same problem again in 2015.

Ken Griffin spreads risk over hundreds of hedged books. Ackman has nine investment professionals and about ten positions that all rise and fall with the stock market. The nearest relative in this series is Warren Buffett, whom he cites as the model.

The process#

The checklist#

What qualifies

The prospectus describes the target as “simple, predictable, free-cash-flow generative businesses” with strong finances and good management. The fund’s reports add: high barriers to entry, little debt, little need to raise money from markets, limited exposure to outside factors such as commodity prices or regulation, and a price below the firm’s estimate of what the business is worth. Free cash flow is the cash a business produces after paying for its operations and investment.

The positions that broke the list are the ones that lost the most: Valeant depended on acquisitions and on drug pricing rules, and J.C. Penney needed a turnaround that had not started.

Finding and timing#

Ideas come from the bottom up, company by company. The half-year report of August 2026 says the firm has built a library of hundreds of qualifying businesses over two decades and waits for them to become cheap (Interim Report, above).

No evidence was found that he uses price charts. Entries follow a fall caused by a specific event. Alphabet was bought in early 2023 after the launch of ChatGPT raised doubts about search. Amazon was bought in the days after the tariff announcement of April 2025. Meta was bought in November 2025 after its shares fell 20% on a spending plan (2025 annual report, above).

Building the stake#

Stakes are often built with derivatives (contracts whose value follows a share).

Total return swap

A contract with a bank that pays the holder the gain or loss on a share, as if the holder owned it, in exchange for a financing charge. It gives the economic position without owning the shares, so it can be built quietly and with less cash up front. Losses are as real as with shares.

Examples: 4.9% of McDonald’s was held through options in 2005 (Fortune, 15 Nov 2005). The General Growth stake was 19.9% including derivatives (Institutional Investor, 28 May 2010). Once a holder passes 5% of a US company it must file a public form, Schedule 13D, which is how most of his campaigns became known.

Sizing, adding and trimming#

With eight to twelve holdings, an average position is about a tenth of the fund. Two rules can be read from the reports.

Winners are trimmed when they grow too large. In February 2020 Chipotle had risen to more than 20% of the portfolio, and a third of it was sold to reduce the weight (2019 Annual Report).

Existing holdings are added to after market-wide falls. In March 2020 the fund raised its stake in Lowe’s by 46%, in Berkshire Hathaway by 39%, in Hilton by 34% and in Howard Hughes by 158% (same report).

Adding to a single falling stock is a different matter, and here the record is poor. He added to Platform Specialty Products at $25 and later called it a mistake. He stayed in Valeant, joined its board after the collapse, and wrote afterwards that the position had been too large (Reuters via Fortune, 27 Jan 2016).

There are no price stops. The 2022 report says positions are resized only to manage concentration, and that the firm does not sell a core holding because it expects a short-term fall (2022 annual report, above).

Selling#

Three kinds of exit appear.

  • Price reaches value. Hilton was bought in 2018 at under 23 times earnings and sold in February 2026 at 32 times (2025 annual report).
  • The business stops being predictable. Netflix was sold three months after purchase. Nike was sold after about 18 months.
  • Forced. Canadian Pacific was sold in 2016 partly to repay investors who were leaving. He later called it one of the firm’s “greatest investment regrets” (Reuters via Yahoo, 7 Mar 2022).

From loud to quieter#

Until 2017 the method was public. He ran proxy fights at Target, Canadian Pacific and ADP, gave presentations lasting several hours, and ran one famous short campaign. Short selling means selling borrowed shares in order to buy them back cheaper later.

In March 2022 he wrote of activist short selling: “we have permanently retired from this line of work” (CNBC, 29 Mar 2022). The 2025 report repeats that the firm has no intention of starting a public short position.

Whether going public helps is not settled. At Canadian Pacific about 90% of votes went to his candidates. At Herbalife a public short brought well-funded opponents to the other side. He posts at length on X, to more than 2 million followers. No study that isolates the effect on his returns was found.

Risk and drawdowns#

The stated rules#

  • Avoid permanent loss of capital, as distinct from a temporary fall in price.
  • No margin loans (money borrowed from a broker against the shares). His reason, given in January 2016, is that shares can “trade at any price in the short term” (Reuters via Fortune, above).
  • Hedge only when the hedge is cheap and the payoff large.

The listed fund does borrow, through bonds. It had about $3.6bn outstanding at the end of 2025, at an average cost of 3.6%, with the last one due in 2039. The bonds have no clause that forces repayment when prices fall, so a market drop cannot trigger a forced sale the way a margin loan can (2025 annual report).

2015 to 2018#

The fund rose about 21% a year until July 2015 (2022 annual report). Then came four losing calendar years: −20.5%, −13.5%, −4.0% and −0.7%. The S&P 500 gained in three of them.

Losses were only part of the damage. Assets fell from above $20bn in 2015 to $8.2bn in April 2018, because investors in the private funds withdrew as soon as their terms allowed. Most outside investors left (CNBC, 5 Apr 2018). The prospectus says that when the trouble began, the two funds that allowed withdrawals held about two-thirds of the firm’s assets. The money in the listed fund could not leave. Its shareholders could only sell their shares to someone else.

What he changed#

His own account is in the 2022 letter: “Beginning in late 2017, we returned to our roots as an investment-centric operation”. In practice:

  • The firm stopped raising money for the funds that allow withdrawals.
  • Staff were cut. Press reports give 56 employees at the start of 2018 and 38 at the end (not verified).
  • Activist short selling ended.
  • The portfolio moved to large, simple businesses that pass the checklist.
  • The investment team stopped doing marketing (2019 annual report).

The fund then made 58.1%, 70.2% and 26.9% in 2019, 2020 and 2021.

The asymmetric hedges#

Asymmetric hedge

A position that costs a small, known amount if nothing happens and pays many times that amount if a feared event occurs. The cost is called premium, as with insurance. While nothing happens the premium drains away, which is called negative carry.

Ackman does not hold hedges all the time. The 2022 letter says he makes such a bet only when the payoff is many times the cost and he is confident of the outcome, and that he made none between 2008 and February 2020.

March 2020. In late February 2020 the funds bought credit default swaps (contracts that pay out when the cost of insuring corporate debt against default rises) on indexes of US and European company debt. Insurance was then close to its cheapest ever, so little could be lost. He wrote that he had first considered selling the whole portfolio, and chose a hedge because it fitted long-term ownership better (2019 annual report).

Interest rates, 2020 to 2023. From late 2020 he bought swaptions (options that gain value when interest rates rise), expecting inflation. They cost $419m in total and returned $2.8bn by March 2023. In 2022 they added 14.3 percentage points to the fund’s return, which is why it lost 8.8% when the index lost 18.1% (2022 annual report).

Treasuries, 2023. In August 2023 he announced on X a bet against 30-year US government bonds, made with options. He closed it on 23 October 2023 for a reported gain of about $200m (Wikipedia; not verified in a primary source). The fund held no interest-rate contracts at the end of 2024 or 2025.

The record#

Year by year#

The listed fund publishes one table in every report. For 2004 to 2012 it shows the private partnership, Pershing Square, L.P. From 2013 it shows Pershing Square Holdings. All figures are after fees.

In 2026 the fund lost 12.6% in the first half, stood at −4.3% on 11 August against +13.7% for the index, and at −6.0% on 17 November. An index figure for the last date was not found.

What $100 became#

The full period hides three very different stretches.

The uncomfortable number is the second pair of bars: the listed fund, taken alone from its launch on 31 December 2012, is behind the index. The fund’s own interim report carries the comparison to 11 August 2026 and prints 12.5% a year for the fund against 15.2% for the S&P 500.

The listed fund as a vehicle#

Pershing Square Holdings is a closed-end fund (a company whose shares trade on an exchange and whose capital stays inside; a shareholder who wants out sells to another investor). Its shares began trading in Amsterdam on 13 October 2014 after an offering of about $2.7bn to $3bn, depending on the source. It joined the London Stock Exchange in 2017 and left Amsterdam in January 2025 (2025 annual report).

The price of a closed-end fund’s shares can differ from the value of what it owns, the net asset value (NAV). Pershing Square Holdings has traded below its NAV for years.

A shareholder earns the share price return, which can be far from the NAV return in any one year.

The fund has spent $1.9bn since 2017 buying back its own shares at an average discount of 29.2%. Ackman wrote in 2023 that even aggressive buybacks would not close the gap. In August 2026 the board called the discount “of real concern” (interim report).

Fees are 1.5% of assets a year plus 16% of gains above the previous peak, the high-water mark. In 2025 the fund paid its manager $208.0m in management fees and $489.2m in performance fees. Two changes reduced the fee slightly in 2024 and 2025: the manager gave up $36m of old offering costs it had been entitled to recover, and it now deducts fees it earns from Howard Hughes on the fund’s own Howard Hughes shares (2025 annual report).

Record confidence
  • Grade A for 2013 to 2025. Pershing Square Holdings is a listed company. Its net asset value comes from accounts audited by Ernst & Young and is published weekly. The same record is repeated in a prospectus filed with the SEC.
  • Grade B for 2004 to 2012. These are the private partnership’s figures. They are printed in the listed fund’s reports, year after year without change, but the table is outside the audited accounts and the partnership’s own accounts are not public.
  • Grade C to D for Gotham Partners. A return of 40% a year appears in secondary sources only.
  • The combined 16% a year assumes an investor who moved from one fund to the other in 2012. The report says no single fund achieved it.

Signature trades#

Wins#

General Growth Properties, 2008–2014. General Growth owned shopping malls and had $27bn of debt it could not refinance. Ackman began buying in late 2008 at 35 to 50 cents a share and held 19.9%, counting derivatives, when the company filed for bankruptcy on 16 April 2009. His thesis was that the malls were worth more than the debts and that the problem was frozen credit. He valued the shares at $10 to $30, joined the board, and with two partners pledged $6.5bn of new capital at $15 a share (Institutional Investor 2010, above). The company left bankruptcy in November 2010 and spun off Howard Hughes to its shareholders. He sold the last 27.6m shares back to the company for $556m in February 2014 (Wikipedia). Holding period: about five years.

Where “$60 million into $1.6 billion” comes from

The figure most often attached to General Growth is that about $60m became $1.6bn. The $1.6bn is Ackman’s own statement to Bloomberg in 2011, when he still held the shares, and it reaches this profile only through later citations (not verified). A final profit figure from a filing was not found. The MBIA profit has the same problem: the publisher of the main book says more than $1bn, a review of the book says $1.1bn, a 2011 profile says $1.3bn and another review says $2bn.

Canadian Pacific, 2011–2016. He began buying in September 2011 and built a stake of 12% to 14%. The thesis was that this was the worst-run large railway in North America and that a new chief executive, Hunter Harrison, could fix it. In May 2012 about 90% of votes went to his candidates (Wikipedia, above). He sold the last shares in August 2016. Profit: about $2.6bn (Reuters 2022, above). Holding period: about five years.

Allergan, 2014. From February 2014 he built 9.7% of Allergan, about $4bn, largely through options, while the drug company Valeant prepared a takeover bid for it. The stake was disclosed on 22 April 2014 (Fortune, 22 Apr 2014). Allergan sold itself to a third company in November 2014. Profit: about $2.2bn (Fortune, 3 Dec 2014). Allergan shareholders who had sold during those weeks sued, alleging insider trading. The case was settled in December 2017 for $290m, of which the Pershing funds paid $193.75m, without admission (prospectus, above). Holding period: about one year.

Chipotle, 2016–2025. He disclosed 9.9% in September 2016, after food-safety incidents had damaged the restaurant chain’s sales, and helped recruit a new chief executive in 2018. By February 2020 it was more than a fifth of the portfolio and a third was sold. About 85% of the stake was sold over the years for $2.4bn of profit, at just under 22% a year. The rest was sold in November 2025 after the shares halved in a year. The whole position earned about 16% a year, against 15% for the index (Institutional Investor, 21 Nov 2025). Holding period: nine years.

MBIA, 2002–2009. After the 2002 report he bought credit default swaps on MBIA. The first cost $16,000 a year for each $10m insured; later ones cost up to $580,000 (MarketFolly, 9 May 2010). He paid premiums for about six years before the bond insurers failed in the credit crisis, and closed the position on 16 January 2009. Profit: about $1.1bn, with the caveat above.

Others, in brief: Hilton, held from 2018 to February 2026 while its earnings per share rose 150%. Universal Music, 10% bought in 2021 for about $4bn and sold in June 2026 after the company rejected his merger proposal, with a reported profit above $600m (Billboard, June 2026; not verified). Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac, bought in October 2013 and still held: they added about 11 points to the 2025 return and were the two largest detractors in the first half of 2026, at −2.8 and −2.7 points (interim report).

Losses#

Valeant, 2015–2017. After the Allergan deal he bought Valeant itself from February 2015, at prices reported between $161 and $190 a share. The thesis was that its management was unusually good at buying drug businesses and running them cheaply. The stake peaked at $5.1bn in mid-2015. Then the company’s pricing and accounting came under attack and the shares fell 95%. He did not sell near $200, predicted $306 on a conference call of more than three hours in October 2015, joined the board in March 2016, and sold 27.2m shares on 13 March 2017 at about $11 (Fortune, 13 Mar 2017). Loss: at least $2.8bn by Bloomberg’s count, nearly $4bn by Forbes’s (Bloomberg, 13 Mar 2017; Forbes, 13 Mar 2017). Holding period: 25 months.

Herbalife, 2012–2018. On 20 December 2012 he presented a $1bn short position in Herbalife, a seller of nutrition products through a network of distributors, and called it a pyramid scheme whose shares would go to zero. On 25 January 2013 he and the investor Carl Icahn argued on live television, and Icahn became the company’s largest shareholder (CNBC, 28 Feb 2018). In July 2016 the Federal Trade Commission settled with the company for $200m and ordered changes to its business, without calling it a pyramid scheme. In November 2017 he replaced the short with put options (contracts that gain if a share falls, and can lose only what they cost). He left on 28 February 2018, with the shares at a record $95.93, more than double their level before the campaign (Fortune, 28 Feb 2018). The firm never published the loss. Fortune estimated about $500m by mid-2016 (Fortune, 15 Jul 2016). Holding period: five years and two months.

J.C. Penney, 2010–2013. He bought 39.1m shares of the department store chain, about 18%, at an average near $22 to $25, and brought in an Apple executive, Ron Johnson, as chief executive. Johnson removed discounts and rebuilt the stores, and customers left. Ackman resigned from the board in August 2013 and sold everything two weeks later at $12.90, for $504m. Loss: about $500m (Bloomberg, 27 Aug 2013). Reuters put it nearer $700m; the difference could not be reconciled from the sources opened and may reflect swaps. Holding period: about three years.

Target, 2007–2011. He raised a separate fund of about $2bn, Pershing Square IV, to hold only Target shares and options. Options magnify both gains and losses. When the shares fell in 2008 the fund lost about 85% to 90% of its value at the low. He apologised to its investors and lost a proxy fight for five board seats on 28 May 2009 (Fortune, 10 May 2009; these details come from search summaries and were not verified in full). Holding period: about four years.

Netflix, 2022. He bought about 3.1m shares for about $1.1bn in January 2022. In April the company’s first-quarter report changed his view. He sold within a day, saying he could no longer predict the business. Loss: more than $430m in three months (Bloomberg, 20 Apr 2022), or 4.5 points of that year’s return. He bought Netflix again in 2026.

The blank-cheque company, 2020–2022. Pershing Square Tontine Holdings raised $4bn in July 2020 to buy a private company. In June 2021 it agreed to buy 10% of Universal Music instead. The SEC objected to the structure and the deal was dropped in July 2021. A shareholder suit, with a former SEC commissioner among the lawyers, argued that the vehicle was an unregistered investment fund (Digital Music News, 18 Aug 2021). The $4bn was returned in 2022. A successor vehicle has existed since September 2023 and had made no deal by the end of 2025.

Nike, 2024–2025. Bought in spring 2024, converted into call options, sold in November 2025. Loss: about 30%, more than $600m, while the index rose 33% (Institutional Investor 2025, above).

The US fund, 2024 and 2026. In July 2024 a US closed-end fund, Pershing Square USA, was talked of at $25bn, cut to $2bn, and withdrawn (Fortune, 1 Aug 2024). It was listed on 29 April 2026 together with the management company, Pershing Square Inc. It raised $5.0bn at $50 a share, of which $2.0bn came from the public offering, and buyers received free shares in the management company (prospectus, above). The fund’s shares fell about 18% on the first day (Renaissance Capital; not verified).

Criticism and mistakes#

Talking his own book. On 18 March 2020 he said on CNBC that “hell is coming” and urged a 30-day national shutdown (CNBC, 18 Mar 2020). Commentators noted that he held a hedge that gained when markets fell. The record shows the hedge had been disclosed on 3 March and that he had started selling it on 12 March, six days before the interview, and was buying shares (2019 annual report). The criticism of motive cannot be settled from documents. The sequence can.

Herbalife: research or manipulation. In March 2014 the New York Times reported that he had hired lobbyists, given money to community groups that then wrote to regulators, and that several letters to state officials appeared to share an author. A former SEC chairman, Harvey Pitt, said it was starting to look like manipulation unless Ackman believed his claims (Fortune, 10 Mar 2014). Icahn called him a “crybaby in the schoolyard” (CNBC 2018, above). Ackman’s reply was that the campaign exposed abusive practices. The regulator’s order supports part of his case and not his conclusion.

Concentration. One position, Valeant, lost a sum equal to roughly a fifth of the firm’s peak assets (computed from about $4bn and about $20bn).

The cost of the discount. A shareholder of the listed fund has earned about two points a year less than the fund’s assets since 2004. A closed-end fund analyst, John Cole Scott, said of the 2024 launch that such funds usually fall to a discount (Fortune 2024, above).

Conflicts in the new structure. Howard Hughes now pays Pershing Square a fee for running it. Some Howard Hughes shareholders are suing in Delaware to have that agreement declared invalid (prospectus, above).

Public campaigns outside investing. From late 2023 he campaigned on X against the presidents of Harvard, the University of Pennsylvania and MIT over their handling of antisemitism on campus. Harvard’s president resigned on 2 January 2024 after plagiarism allegations. Two days later Business Insider reported similar problems in the dissertation of his wife, Neri Oxman, who apologised for citation errors. He disputed the reporting; the publisher’s owner reviewed it and stood by it (Fortune, 15 Jan 2024). He endorsed Donald Trump in July 2024 after many years of supporting Democrats. This matters here only because his public profile is now part of the business.

Routine and mindset#

On being wrong. He writes it down in public. In January 2016 he listed four mistakes to investors, with prices. In March 2017 he took full responsibility for Valeant and wrote: “I deeply and profoundly apologize” (CNBC, 29 Mar 2017). He gave four lessons:

  • Skill at buying companies does not make a durable business.
  • Politics and regulation can destroy value in ways that cannot be forecast.
  • A good management team can still make large mistakes.
  • A collapse in the share price can itself damage the business, through morale and hiring.

On changing his mind. He is slow when the position is public and fast when it is not. Herbalife lasted 62 months and Valeant 25, the second with a board seat that restricted his ability to sell. Netflix lasted three months.

On permanent capital. This is the lesson he repeats most. Investors in his private funds left near the low in 2016 to 2018, and he had to sell Canadian Pacific to pay them. By the end of 2025, 96% of fee-paying capital could not be withdrawn.

Habits. A 2011 profile describes regular tennis, no coffee, and a competitive streak in the office gym (Commercial Observer, above). The firm works from its office five days a week for ten months, and the investment team works from the Hamptons, on Long Island, in July and August (Fortune 2026, above). Daily hours and reading habits were not found.

Giving. The Pershing Square Foundation, founded in 2006, has committed between $500m and $750m depending on the source, and he has signed the Giving Pledge (Forbes and Wikipedia, above).

What carries over#

His method has no content for trading within a day or a week. What carries over is how risk is set when positions are few.

Investing (years)#

  • Capital that cannot be forced out. He kept his holdings through 2015–2018 and bought in March 2020 because nobody could withdraw the money. An investor who adds to index funds on a schedule and does not borrow is in the same position: no one can force a sale in a pullback.
  • The comparison that matters. A concentrated, audited, professionally run fund returned 12.5% a year from the end of 2012 to August 2026. The index returned 15.2%. This is evidence about how hard the index is to beat, from the manager’s own report.
  • Price and value of a fund. A fund bought at a discount can go to a larger discount. The first half of 2026 cost shareholders 23.9% when the assets fell 12.6%.
  • Recovery arithmetic. A 34% fall needed a 52% gain. It arrived in one year, 2019, which is unusual.

Swing trading (days to weeks)#

  • Size is the stop when there are few positions. He uses no price stops, so a position of a fifth of the fund can cost a fifth of the fund. A swing trader who holds five to ten leading stocks is concentrated in the same way, with the difference that a stop can cap each loss. The useful question is what the account loses if the largest position gaps through its stop.
  • Adding to a loser against cutting it. The table below sets his two approaches side by side.
  • A hedge with a fixed cost. The March 2020 trade risked $27m. A put option on an index has the same shape: a known premium, a payoff only in a sharp fall. His own rule is to buy such protection only when it is cheap, which in 2020 meant when few others wanted it.
  • Trimming a winner by weight. He sold a third of Chipotle when it passed a fifth of the portfolio. He also named, as mistakes, not trimming Valeant near $200 and Canadian Pacific near C$240.
  • A public thesis is harder to drop. His two longest-held losers were the two he had argued for most publicly.

Day trading (minutes to hours)#

Nothing in his process works on an intraday time frame. He holds for years, uses no charts and no stops, and makes a handful of decisions a year. Three habits transfer.

  • The thesis is written before the entry. Each of his positions comes with a stated reason and a stated condition for being wrong. A day trader’s equivalent is the setup and the invalidation level noted before the order.
  • A fixed risk per idea. His hedges had a fixed, known cost. A fixed risk unit per trade does the same job at a different scale.
  • Squeeze risk in heavily shorted shares. Herbalife rose sharply on days when well-known buyers appeared, because short sellers had to buy back. A short squeeze is a rise driven by short sellers closing their positions. The same mechanics apply within a day to any stock with a large short interest.

Seven questions#

Question What the record shows
Sizing up when right, cutting when wrong Large at entry, about a tenth of capital. Adds after market-wide falls (March 2020). Trims above about a fifth. Has added to losers and called it a mistake. No price stop
After a string of losses No rule for reducing size was found. The response in 2017–18 was structural: fewer staff, no fund-raising, no shorts, simpler businesses
Keeping gains Sells at his estimate of value (Starbucks 2020, Hilton 2026). Buys a hedge when he fears the market. Regrets not trimming two winners at their highs
Market-wide filter None that changes the stock holdings. Hedges only when cheap, and none between 2008 and 2020
Separate books by horizon Separate vehicles for single ideas: a Target fund, co-investment funds, a blank-cheque company. The Herbalife short, expected to resolve quickly, became a five-year position
Record-keeping Two public letters a year with the gain or loss from each position, and written post-mortems. A personal journal was not found
Preparation against execution Almost all preparation. Six new positions in 2026 was described as one of the busiest periods in the firm’s history
Hypotheses worth checking
  • In a trade log, the largest positions by risk earn more per unit of risk than the smallest. If they do not, conviction is not adding anything and equal sizing would serve as well.
  • Trades in which size was added while the position was below its entry price end worse, on average, than the same trades would have ended if closed at the point of the add.
  • For stocks on a watch list written in advance, entries made in the five days after a one-day fall of more than 10% on news do better over the following three months than entries made at other times.
What to take from it
  • With few positions, the size of each one is the main risk decision.
  • His worst years were made worse by investors who could leave. His recovery was made by capital that could not.
  • The hedges worked because the cost was small and fixed, and because they were sold in stages as they paid.
  • He cut quickly where he had said little in public and slowly where he had said a lot.
  • The listed fund has trailed the index since its launch in 2012, in its own numbers.

What cannot be copied#

  • Permanent capital. $15bn in a listed fund that nobody can redeem, plus fee income to pay the staff through bad years.
  • Bonds without margin calls. $3.6bn at an average 3.6%, some not due until 2039.
  • Influence. Board seats, proxy fights, and a call that a chief executive will return.
  • The platform. Television access and more than 2 million followers.
  • The hedging instruments. Index credit default swaps and swaptions are traded between institutions.

The 16% a year depended on several of these, most of all the first. The habits in the previous section depend on none of them.

What to learn first#

  • Closed-end fund, NAV and discount: a listed fund’s shares can trade below the value of what it owns.
  • Activism, Schedule 13D and proxy fight: how a large shareholder pushes for change and when the stake becomes public.
  • Short selling and short squeeze: selling borrowed shares, and what happens when the price rises instead.
  • Options and total return swaps: ways to hold a position without owning the shares.
  • Credit default swap, swaption and negative carry: the tools of the hedges, and the cost of waiting.
  • Fees and the high-water mark: what a fund charges and when.

All of these are covered in the hedge fund equity primer. The value investing primer covers intrinsic value and free cash flow.
