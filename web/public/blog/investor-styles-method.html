Investor styles: 30 names, one set of questions

The plan for a series on how 30 well-known investors actually invest, the 14 questions every profile answers, and how the records will be graded and ranked.

This series documents how 30 well-known investors, fund managers and finance personalities actually invest or trade. The list runs from managers with decades of reported returns to television hosts and convicted fraudsters. Every profile answers the same 14 questions, so that a hedge fund manager, a venture investor and a television personality can be read side by side. This note sets out those questions, how the evidence is graded, and what the series will and will not claim.

The starting point was a joke ranking that put an actor in the top tier and a television host next to a Ponzi schemer at the bottom. That ranking is dropped. A new one is made at the end, from the evidence, with the criteria stated here in advance.

Why profiles, and why the same questions#

Most writing about famous investors repeats the legend: the one great trade, the favourite quotation, a round number for the returns. That is of little use to someone who wants to learn from them. What is useful is the process: where ideas come from, how a position is sized, what happens after a loss, and how much of the result depended on advantages an individual does not have.

Asking every name the same questions does three things. It shows where the record is thin. It makes the styles comparable. And it separates what can be copied from what cannot.

The 14 questions#

# Question What it is for
1 Snapshot. Style, instruments, holding period, leverage, assets, status today. Orientation in one glance.
2 Origin. Training, mentors, first fund, the formative loss. Styles are usually shaped by an early mistake.
3 Source of the edge. Analysis, information, behaviour, structure, speed, or one lucky era. The most important question. It decides what can be copied.
4 Process. Finding ideas, forming a thesis, timing, sizing, managing, exiting. The part an individual can actually study.
5 Risk and drawdowns. Rules, worst losses, behaviour during and after them. Survival matters more than any single trade.
6 The record. Returns by year and by era, net of fees, against a benchmark. Separates reputation from results.
7 Signature trades. Three wins and at least two losses, each with thesis, trigger, size and outcome. Shows the process on real cases.
8 Mistakes and criticism. The best-argued case against them, and what they changed. A profile without this is an advertisement.
9 Psychology and routine. Daily habits, handling of being wrong, breaks, sparring partners. Execution is where most traders lose.
10 Evolution and regime. How the style changed with size and era; whether it still works. Many records come from one kind of market.
11 What carries over. By time frame: investing, swing trading, day trading. The reason the series exists.
12 What cannot be copied. Scale, leverage terms, access, permanent capital, teams. Prevents drawing the wrong lesson.
13 Prerequisites. The concepts needed first. These become the style primers.
14 Sources. Letters, filings, books, long interviews, court documents. So that every claim can be checked.

Where the edge comes from#

The third question does the most work. Returns always come from somewhere, and the source decides whether an individual can learn anything from them.

Seven questions asked of every name#

Question 11 is where an active trader spends the most time, so it is made specific. Each profile answers these seven, with evidence:

  1. How do they size up when right and cut when wrong? Do they add to winners, and on strength or on pullbacks?
  2. What do they do after a run of losses? Is there a rule for trading smaller?
  3. How do they keep gains: fixed targets, trailing exits, or selling when the reason is gone?
  4. Do they use a market-wide filter, such as central bank policy, liquidity, trend or breadth (how many stocks are rising), to decide how aggressive to be?
  5. Do they separate books by time horizon? Has a short-term trade ever turned into a long-term holding by default?
  6. Which record-keeping or review habit do they credit: journals, decision logs, post-mortems?
  7. How much of the day is preparation and how much is execution?

How the record is graded#

A return figure is only as good as its source. Hedge funds do not have to publish returns, venture funds rarely do, and personalities usually publish only their winners. Each profile therefore grades the record before discussing it.

Grade Meaning Typical example
A Audited, or filed with a regulator. A listed company’s annual report; a mutual fund’s published returns.
B Reported consistently by several reliable outlets that cite investor letters. Most large hedge funds.
C Self-reported, or from a single outlet. A manager’s own summary of a private fund.
D Claimed, unverifiable or contradicted by the evidence. Returns quoted in marketing; any figure from a fraud.
Three traps the series tries to avoid
  • Survivorship. These 30 are famous because they succeeded, or failed loudly. For every one of them there were many with the same approach who are unknown. A process is not proven by the fact that a famous person used it.
  • Legend. The best-known stories about great trades usually come from the trader’s own later account. Where no independent record exists, the profile says so.
  • Scale. A method that earned 30% a year on $50 million may be impossible at $50 billion, and the reverse is also true: some methods only work at scale.

How the ranking will be made#

The ranking comes last, after every record has been checked. Six questions decide it, and the evidence for each is collected as the profiles are written.

The fraud cases and several of the personalities will rank at the bottom by construction. They are in the series for a different reason: knowing how a scheme worked, and which warning signs were visible at the time, is worth more to most investors than one more account of a great trade.

Order of publication#

The series is published one style group at a time, and each group starts with its primer. Global macro comes first: the primer, then Stan Druckenmiller, then Ray Dalio. The series page shows the map of all 30 names and what has been published.

What to take from it
  • Every profile answers the same 14 questions, and the question that matters most is where the returns came from.
  • Records are graded A to D before they are discussed. Famous stories without an independent source are marked as such.
  • Each profile ends with what carries over to investing, swing trading and day trading, and with ideas written as tests, never as advice.
