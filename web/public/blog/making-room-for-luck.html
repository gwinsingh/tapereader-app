Making room for luck: a reading of the Luck Field Manual, with the evidence checked

A popular 33-rule guide says luck can be engineered; its ideas reduce to three jobs (meet more chances, survive the bad ones, notice the good ones), the research behind them is strong for some rules and thin for others, and the advice only works where a failed attempt is cheap.

“The Luck Field Manual” is a 33-rule guide by the newsletter Polymath Investor. Its claim is that lucky people are not born lucky: they behave in ways that put them in the path of more chances. This note summarises the guide in a few hundred words, checks its main claims against the studies behind them, and asks what carries over to investing and trading. The short version: the 33 rules reduce to three jobs, the evidence is strong for some and thin for others, and the central advice to take more shots holds only where a failed shot is cheap.

The guide was published for the newsletter’s paying subscribers on 4 January 2026 under the title “How To Manufacture Luck” (Polymath Investor). A six-page version circulated on social media, and this note was written from that version. The rules themselves are the author’s work and are not reproduced here. What follows is a summary in other words, with the sources checked independently.

The guide in three jobs#

The guide’s central image is rain and a bucket. Nobody controls the rain, but the size of the bucket is a choice. The idea has an older name, the luck surface area, from a 2010 essay by Jason Roberts: luck grows with what a person does multiplied by how many people hear about it (Codus Operandi, 2010).

The 33 rules sit in nine parts. Read together they describe three jobs.

The guide’s own one-line formula says the same: more exposure, better positioning to recognise a chance, and enough resilience to still be playing when it arrives.

An older map: four kinds of chance#

The neurologist James Austin sorted luck into four kinds in his 1978 book Chase, Chance, and Creativity. The venture investor Marc Andreessen summarised them in a 2007 essay that is still the easiest introduction (Andreessen, 2007).

This matters for the guide’s opening claim that luck is not random. Part of it is. A 2018 simulation by three Italian researchers gave a population normally spread talent and random lucky and unlucky events. The most successful individuals were almost never the most talented. They were people of middling talent with more lucky events (Pluchino, Biondo and Rapisarda, 2018). A model is not a measurement, but it is a fair warning: the stories of lucky people are told by those for whom it worked.

What the evidence supports#

Claim in the guide Evidence How strong
Loose acquaintances bring more opportunities than close friends Granovetter’s 1973 survey; a randomised experiment on 20 million LinkedIn members (2022) Strong, with limits
Linking separate groups produces better ideas Burt’s 2004 study of 673 managers at a large US electronics company Good, observational
More attempts produce more hits Simonton’s work on creative careers: the share of hits per work stays roughly constant Moderate; describes people who were already eminent
A relaxed mind notices more Wiseman’s newspaper experiment Suggestive; one researcher’s popular account
Persistence predicts success better than talent Duckworth’s grit studies; a 2017 review of 88 samples Overstated
Most jobs are never advertised (“85%”) An online survey of self-selected respondents Weak
Never risk total ruin; prefer small losses and large gains Arithmetic, not psychology Strong

Weak ties. Mark Granovetter asked people who had found a job through a contact how often they saw that contact. The paper has become one of the most cited in sociology (Granovetter, 1973).

In 2022 the idea was tested as an experiment. LinkedIn’s connection suggestions were varied at random for more than 20 million members over five years. Weaker ties did lead to more job moves, but only up to a point: moderately weak ties worked best, and the very weakest added little. The effect was strongest in digital industries; in less digital ones strong ties did more (Rajkumar and co-authors, Science, 2022).

Bridging groups. Ronald Burt found that managers whose contacts spanned separate groups had their ideas rated more highly by senior executives, and were paid and promoted better (Burt, 2004). The study observes; it cannot show that bridging caused the results.

Volume. Dean Simonton’s studies of scientists and composers found that the chance of any single work being a hit does not rise with experience, so those who produce more have more hits (Simonton, 1997). The guide adds its own caution in a footnote: quality and distribution still matter.

Noticing. Richard Wiseman gave people who called themselves lucky or unlucky a newspaper and asked them to count the photographs. The second page carried a half-page message stating that there were 43. Self-described unlucky people took about two minutes, because they missed it. Lucky people took seconds (Wiseman, 2003). The account comes from a magazine article about his book, drawn from 400 volunteers who answered advertisements, without the sample sizes of the experiment itself. It is a memorable demonstration, not a settled finding.

Persistence. Angela Duckworth’s studies found that grit (sustained effort and interest over years) predicted who finished a military academy’s first summer and who went furthest in a national spelling contest (Duckworth and co-authors, 2007). A later review pooled 88 samples and 66,807 people. It found that grit is “only moderately correlated with performance” and overlaps very strongly with conscientiousness, a long-known personality trait (Credé, Tynan and Harms, 2017). Persistence helps. The claim that it beats talent goes further than the data.

The arithmetic under the advice#

Two of the guide’s parts are arithmetic. Taking more shots raises the chance of at least one hit. Protecting the downside keeps the player alive to take them. The same number of attempts drives both, in opposite directions.

So “take more shots” is good advice only for a certain kind of shot.

One more condition is missing from the guide. More attempts help only when each attempt has a positive expected result. In a game with a negative expectation, such as a casino bet or a trading rule with no edge after costs, more attempts make a loss more certain, not less. The quant primer shows the arithmetic and how many trades it takes to tell an edge from chance.

What carries over#

Investing (years)#

  • Survival comes first. Warren Buffett keeps large cash reserves for the same reason the guide gives for keeping slack: the best opportunities appear when others are forced to sell. Ken Griffin’s firm bought distressed trading books in 2006 and 2007 because it had spare capacity, and nearly failed in 2008 when it did not.
  • Being early needs staying power. Michael Burry was right about mortgages two years before it paid. He survived the wait because the bet could lose only a known amount.
  • A watch list is a prepared mind. Bill Ackman’s firm follows several hundred businesses for years and buys when a specific event makes one cheap. That is Austin’s third kind of chance in practice.

Swing trading (days to weeks)#

  • Many small bets with a fixed risk are the trading form of “more shots”. They belong in the top left cell only if the setup has a tested edge and each loss is capped by a stop.
  • Reversible decisions fast. An entry with a stop is a reversible decision: the cost of being wrong is known in advance. Jeff Bezos made the general point in his 2015 letter to Amazon shareholders, calling such choices “two-way doors” (Amazon, 2015 letter). Position size and overnight exposure are less reversible and deserve the slower thought.
  • Slack. An account that is always fully invested cannot act on the best setup of the month.

Day trading (minutes to hours)#

  • Attention. Wiseman’s experiment has a trading reading: a trader counting ticks on one chart can miss the larger message on the screen, such as the direction of the index or a level from the daily chart. Preparation before the open is the cheap fix, because it decides in advance what is worth noticing.
  • Ruin is a daily question. A daily loss limit is the intraday form of “never risk total ruin”. It keeps one bad morning from removing the next hundred attempts.
  • Volume is not a virtue by itself. For strategies such as 5-minute opening range breakouts, more trades help only while each trade still meets the setup’s conditions. Extra trades taken to “stay active” have a different, usually negative, expectation.
Hypotheses worth checking
  • In a trade log, days with a written plan before the open show more trades that match a defined setup, and a higher average result in units of risk, than days without one.
  • Trades taken after the day’s planned setups were exhausted have a lower expectancy than the planned ones.
  • Ideas that were written down and shared with at least one other person before the trade were sized more consistently than ideas that were not.
What to take from it
  • The 33 rules reduce to three jobs: meet more chances, survive the bad ones, notice the good ones.
  • The network rules rest on the best evidence. The persistence and attention rules rest on less than the guide suggests.
  • “Take more shots” applies only where a miss is cheap and the average shot is worth taking.
  • Part of luck stays random. The rules shift the odds; they do not remove the dice.
