Global macro: a primer on trading currencies, interest rates and whole markets

Global macro traders bet on whole economies with borrowed money, and the part of their method that an individual can use is the free public data they watch to decide how much risk to carry.

Global macro is the style of investing that bets on whole economies instead of single companies. A macro trader forms a view on interest rates, currencies, stock markets and commodities, and backs it with borrowed money through futures and similar contracts. Stan Druckenmiller and Ray Dalio, both profiled in this series, built their records this way. The one thing worth taking from the style: its best practitioners read the central bank, credit and the market’s trend to decide how much risk to carry, and those readings come from data that is free and public.

This primer is the background for those two profiles. It is a study of a style, not advice.

What global macro is#

Global macro means taking positions on the big forces that move all prices in a country at once: growth, inflation, interest rates and exchange rates. The trader can be long (positioned to gain if a price rises) or short (positioned to gain if it falls) in any market in any country. Positions are usually leveraged (made larger than the trader’s own money by borrowing).

A stock picker asks whether one company is worth more than its share price. A macro trader asks whether a whole market is priced for the wrong economy, and holds a few large positions such as “short the pound” or “long 10-year US government bonds”.

There are two schools:

  • Discretionary macro. A person decides. Druckenmiller, George Soros and Paul Tudor Jones are the best-known names (Mallaby, More Money Than God).
  • Systematic macro. Written rules decide and a computer applies them. Bridgewater, the firm Dalio founded, is the usual example. Trend followers (funds that buy what has been rising and sell what has been falling, by rule) are the best-known group.

The two schools earn money in different years. HFR, a firm that tracks hedge funds (private investment funds, described below), publishes an index for each. In 2020 the discretionary index gained 13.94% and the systematic index 2.61%. In 2022 the order reversed: systematic 12.13%, discretionary 1.99% (HFR, Jan 2024).

The four things macro traders trade#

First, the contracts. A future is a standard contract, traded on an exchange, to buy or sell something at a set price on a later date. A forward is the same agreement made privately with a bank. An option is the right, but not the duty, to buy or sell at a set price; the buyer pays a fee and cannot lose more than that fee. An ETF (exchange-traded fund) is a fund that trades like a share and tracks a market.

Currencies. An exchange rate is the price of one currency in another, so every trade is a pair: buying dollars means selling something else. About $9.6 trillion changed hands per day in April 2025, and the US dollar was one side of 89% of trades (BIS, 2025). Bets are made with forwards, futures, options or currency ETFs. The main drivers are the gap between two countries’ interest rates, inflation and central-bank action. Example: one dollar bought 103.79 yen on average in January 2021 and 147.05 yen in October 2022, as US rates rose and Japanese rates stayed near zero (FRED, the St. Louis Fed’s free database). The yen lost 29% of its dollar value.

Interest rates and bonds. A bond is a loan that can be bought and sold. US government bonds are called Treasuries. Bets are made with government bond futures, with futures on the central bank’s future rate, or with bond ETFs. The drivers are expected central-bank policy, inflation and government borrowing. Example: the yield on the 10-year US Treasury bond averaged 0.62% in July 2020 and 3.98% in October 2022 (FRED).

Stock indexes. An index is a basket of shares; the S&P 500 holds 500 large US companies. Bets are made with index futures, ETFs and options. The drivers are company profits, interest rates and investors’ appetite for risk. Example: the S&P 500 fell 33.9% in 23 trading days, from 3,386.15 on 19 February 2020 to 2,237.40 on 23 March 2020 (FRED).

Commodities. These are raw materials: oil, gold, copper, grain. Bets are made mostly with futures. The drivers are supply, demand, weather, war and the dollar. Example: US crude oil averaged $16.55 a barrel in April 2020, $114.84 in June 2022 and $102.07 in October 2026 (FRED).

Interest rates and bonds from zero#

A bond pays a fixed amount of interest, called the coupon, and returns the loan at maturity (the end date). Its yield is the yearly return a buyer earns at today’s price.

Price and yield move in opposite directions. Take a 10-year bond that pays 4% a year and costs $100. If new 10-year bonds start paying 5%, nobody will pay $100 for the old one. Its price must fall until its fixed payments also return 5%. That price is $92.21, a loss of 7.8%. If yields fall to 3%, the price rises to $108.58.

The longer the bond, the larger the move. The same 1 percentage point rise costs a 2-year bond 1.9% and a 30-year bond 15.5%. Duration is the name for this sensitivity: a duration of 8 means roughly an 8% price change for a 1 point change in yield. The 10-year bond in the example has a duration of about 8.2.

This happened in 2022. The 10-year Treasury yield rose from 1.47% in December 2021 to 3.62% in December 2022, and the bond returned −17.83% for the year (Damodaran, NYU).

The yield curve#

The yield curve is the line of yields from short loans to long loans. Normally long loans pay more. The curve is inverted when short loans pay more than long ones. That happens when the central bank has pushed short rates up and investors expect it to cut them later, usually because they expect a weaker economy.

A recession is a broad fall in output and jobs lasting months. In the United States an inversion has come before every recession since 1970. The wait has varied from about 5 to about 24 months, and there have been false signals (FRED; NBER).

The curve was inverted from July 2022 to September 2024. As of November 2026 no US recession has been dated since April 2020. The 2020 recession was caused by a pandemic, which the 2019 inversion could not have foreseen.

Central banks and liquidity#

The Federal Reserve (the Fed) is the US central bank (the public body that sets a country’s interest rate and issues its money). It does two things that matter to markets.

It sets the policy rate. The federal funds rate is the rate at which banks lend to each other overnight. The Fed sets a target for it at scheduled meetings. Every other dollar interest rate, from mortgages to company loans, is built on top of it.

It changes the size of its balance sheet. The Fed can create money and use it to buy bonds. This is quantitative easing (QE). It pushes long-term yields down and adds cash to the financial system. The reverse is quantitative tightening (QT): the Fed lets its bonds mature without replacing them, which removes cash. Liquidity is the loose word for how much cash and credit is available to buy assets.

The policy rate since 2006 (Federal Reserve; FRED):

  • 5.25% in June 2006, then cut from September 2007 to a range of 0–0.25% in December 2008.
  • Held at zero for seven years, until December 2015. Raised to 2.25–2.50% by December 2018.
  • Cut back to 0–0.25% on 15 March 2020.
  • Raised 11 times from March 2022 to 5.25–5.50% in July 2023.
  • Cut six times from September 2024 to 3.50–3.75% in December 2025.
  • Raised to 3.75–4.00% on 16 September 2026, with the Fed saying inflation “remains elevated” (Federal Reserve, 16 Sep 2026).

The business cycle and the debt cycle#

The business cycle is the repeating pattern of the economy. In an expansion, output, jobs and profits grow. At the peak, growth stops. In a recession, output and jobs fall. The trough is the low point, and recovery follows. The National Bureau of Economic Research (NBER) dates the US cycle. Since 1970 it has dated eight recessions, lasting from 2 to 18 months and 85 months in total (NBER).

The debt cycle explains why the business cycle exists. This is the core of Dalio’s short film “How the Economic Machine Works” (Dalio). In plain words:

  1. People spend both income and borrowed money (credit).
  2. When credit is cheap, people borrow and spend more. Incomes and asset prices rise, so lenders lend more.
  3. Spending outruns what the economy can produce, and prices rise. The central bank raises rates.
  4. Borrowing becomes expensive and spending falls. One person’s spending is another’s income, so incomes fall too. That is the recession.
  5. The central bank cuts rates and the cycle restarts.

Over decades, debt can grow faster than income until rate cuts no longer help because rates are already at zero. What follows is a deleveraging: debts are cut through defaults, lower spending and money creation. The US in 2008 is the modern case. Output fell 4.3%, unemployment peaked at 10%, and the S&P 500 fell 57% from its October 2007 peak to March 2009 (Federal Reserve History).

One market price shows the credit side of the cycle. The credit spread is the extra yield investors demand to lend to companies instead of the government. For mid-grade US companies it reached 6.16 percentage points in December 2008 and 4.31 in March 2020. It was 1.61 on 20 November 2026 (FRED).

Currencies, pegs and carry trades#

Most large currencies float: the market sets the rate. A peg is a government promise to hold its currency at a set rate against another. To keep the promise, the central bank must buy its own currency whenever others sell, using its reserves (its store of foreign currency), or raise interest rates so that holding the currency pays more.

A peg breaks when the interest rate needed to defend it is one the home economy cannot bear. Reserves are finite, so traders sell and wait.

Black Wednesday, 16 September 1992#

In October 1990 the UK joined the European Exchange Rate Mechanism (ERM), a system of pegs between European currencies. The pound was fixed at 2.95 German marks, with a floor of 2.778 (Bank of England, Nov 1992).

The peg tied British interest rates to German ones. Germany was holding rates high after reunification. Britain was in recession, and higher rates meant higher mortgage payments, which the government was unwilling to impose (Naef, Cambridge University Press). The market judged that the government would not raise rates far enough for long enough.

The bet was lopsided. At the floor, the pound could not fall further unless the peg broke, and few expected it to rise much while the government struggled to hold it. A trader who sold pounds risked a small loss and stood to gain a large one.

The Bank had sold over $22 billion of reserves in a few hours (Naef). The pound fell to 2.50 marks by 30 September, 10% below its old floor. Against the dollar it fell from $2.00 on 2 September 1992 to $1.42 on 12 February 1993, a drop of 29% (FRED). The UK Treasury later put the cost to taxpayers at about £3.3 billion (Central Banking, 9 Feb 2005).

Soros’s Quantum Fund, where Druckenmiller ran the portfolio, was the best-known seller. Forbes reported weeks later that the fund had sold about $7 billion of pounds and bought about $6 billion of marks (Forbes, Nov 1992). The profit on the pound is usually given as about $1 billion.

Legend against record

The $1 billion figure comes from press reports and the fund’s own account. No audited statement is public. Forbes at the time gave about $1.5 billion for all of the Soros funds’ positions in September 1992. The Bank of England’s timeline and the exchange rates are the solid part of the story.

The yen carry trade and August 2024#

A carry trade borrows in a currency with low interest rates and invests in one with high rates. The trader collects the gap. The risk is that the borrowed currency rises, because the debt then costs more to repay.

In July 2024 the US policy rate was 5.33% and Japan’s was near zero. Borrowing yen to hold dollar assets paid about 5 percentage points a year. On 31 July the Bank of Japan raised its rate to 0.25% (Bank of Japan). On 2 August a weak US jobs report raised the odds of US rate cuts. The gap was closing from both sides.

The dollar fell from 161.73 yen on 10 July to 143.95 on 5 August, an 11% move that wiped out two years of carry (FRED). Traders who had borrowed yen sold other assets to repay. Japan’s Nikkei 225 index fell 12.4% on 5 August, its worst day since 1987, and regained about 10% the next day (AP via Fortune). The S&P 500 fell 6.1% in three sessions and was back above its starting level by 15 August (FRED; BIS, Sep 2024).

Leverage and futures#

Futures give leverage without a loan. The buyer does not pay the full value but posts margin, a deposit held to cover losses. The full value controlled is the notional exposure.

A worked example with the E-mini S&P 500 future, a widely used stock index contract:

  • One contract is worth $50 times the index. With the S&P 500 at 6,910.39 on 20 November 2026, that is $345,520 of notional exposure.
  • One broker’s overnight margin was $28,629 per contract in September 2026 (TradeZero). That is 8.3% of the notional, or leverage of about 12 times.
  • A 1% move in the index is $3,455, or 12% of the margin.
  • The index fell 6.8% between 16 and 20 November 2026. On one contract that is $25,310, or 88% of the margin posted.

A 10-year Treasury note future controls $100,000 of bonds for a margin quoted at roughly $2,300 to $3,000 (MetroTrade). Margins are set by the exchange and change often; the figures here are broker quotes.

Margin call

When losses reduce the deposit below a set level, the broker demands more money at once and closes the position if it is not paid. A leveraged trader can therefore be right about the direction and still lose: the position is closed before the view pays off.

How a macro fund is built#

A hedge fund is a private investment pool open only to wealthy investors and institutions. It can borrow, sell short (sell borrowed assets to gain from a fall) and use derivatives (contracts such as futures and options whose value depends on another price) with few limits (SEC).

Fees. The classic terms are “2 and 20”: 2% of assets each year plus 20% of profits. The SEC, the US markets regulator, describes 1–2% and 20% as typical. A worked example: $1,000,000 earns 10% a year before fees for ten years. Without fees it becomes $2,593,742. After 2 and 20 it becomes $1,859,586, a return of 6.4% a year. The investor paid $483,517 in fees and ends $734,156 short of the no-fee result, because the fees paid would also have grown.

Lock-ups. Investors cannot leave at will. Withdrawals are allowed monthly, quarterly or yearly, and often not at all in the first year or more (SEC).

Prime brokers. A prime broker is the bank that lends to the fund, holds its assets and settles its trades. It sets the fund’s margin and can raise it in a crisis.

Drawdown limits. A drawdown is the fall from a fund’s highest value to its current value. Funds cut risk after losses for three reasons. Losses compound against the trader: a 20% loss needs a 25% gain to recover, and a 50% loss needs 100%. The 20% is usually charged only above the previous high (the high-water mark), so a fund deep in drawdown earns no performance fee. And investors and prime brokers can pull money. Some firms that employ many traders state in advance the loss at which a trader is dismissed (Man Group).

A short history in nine episodes#

  • 1971–73, the end of Bretton Woods. Since 1944 currencies had been fixed to the dollar and the dollar to gold at $35 an ounce, a system named after the town where it was agreed. On 15 August 1971 President Nixon ended the dollar’s link to gold. By March 1973 the major currencies floated (Federal Reserve History). Floating rates created the market macro traders work in.
  • 1985, the Plaza Accord. The dollar had risen 44% in five years. On 22 September 1985 five governments, meeting at the Plaza Hotel in New York, agreed to push it down. It fell 4% the next trading day and 40% by 1987 (Frankel, NBER).
  • 1987, the crash. On 19 October the Dow Jones index of large US shares fell 22.6% in one day. The Fed promised cash to the banking system the next morning. The index regained 57% of the loss in two sessions (Federal Reserve History).
  • 1992, sterling. The pound left the ERM, as described above.
  • 1994, the bond sell-off. The Fed doubled its rate from 3% to 6% in twelve months from February 1994. The 10-year yield rose from 5.75% to 7.96%, and the 10-year bond returned −8.04% for the year (FRED; Damodaran).
  • 1997–98, Asia and LTCM. Thailand’s peg broke on 2 July 1997 and the crisis spread; $118 billion of rescue loans followed. In August 1998 Russia defaulted. Long-Term Capital Management (LTCM), a hedge fund carrying about $30 of debt per $1 of capital, lost 44% that month. Fourteen banks put in $3.6 billion on 23 September 1998 (Federal Reserve History).
  • 2008. The S&P 500 returned about −37% and 10-year Treasuries +20.1%. The Fed cut to zero and began QE.
  • 2020. The S&P 500 fell 33.9% in five weeks. The Fed cut to zero on 15 March and its balance sheet grew by $3 trillion by June. The index finished the year up 18%.
  • 2022. Inflation brought seven rate rises in one year. The S&P 500 returned −18.0% and 10-year Treasuries −17.8%. A portfolio of 60% stocks and 40% bonds (a “60/40” portfolio, the standard balanced mix) lost about 18%, its third-worst year since 1928 after 1931 and 1937 (Damodaran). Since 1928 stocks and bonds had both lost in the same year only four times before.

How the style has performed as a group#

The HFRI Macro (Total) Index averages the returns, after fees, of macro hedge funds that report to HFR. The S&P 500 figures include dividends.

Sources: HFR guides of May 2011 and January 2024; press reports of HFR data for 2011, 2012, 2024 and 2025. S&P 500: Damodaran.

What the numbers show:

  • Macro made money in the two worst years for stocks. +4.83% in 2008, when the S&P 500 returned −36.55%, and +8.98% in 2022, when it returned −18.04%. The average hedge fund lost 19.03% in 2008.
  • The 2010s were weak. From 2010 to 2019 the index compounded at 1.3% a year against 13.4% for the S&P 500.
  • Results improved after 2020 to 5.7% a year for 2020–2025, still well below the 14.9% from stocks.

What they do not show:

  • The best funds. An index is an average. The records in the Druckenmiller and Dalio profiles sit far above it.
  • Funds that died. Funds that close stop reporting, and new funds can add their good early years later. A study of hedge fund data for 1996–2003 put the first effect at about 3.7 percentage points a year and the second at over 5 (Malkiel and Saha, 2005). Index returns are likely overstated.

Why macro is hard#

Few bets. A stock picker makes many separate decisions. A macro trader makes a few large ones a year, so skill is hard to tell from luck.

Timing. A correct view with the wrong timing is a loss when the position is leveraged. The yield curve table shows the problem: the wait after an inversion ran from 5 to 24 months.

Crowding. Good macro ideas are public, and many funds hold the same position. When one sells, all are hit, as in August 2024.

It got harder after 2008. Macro traders earn from large moves in rates and currencies and from differences between countries. From December 2008 the Fed held its rate at zero for seven years and bought bonds, taking its balance sheet from $0.9 trillion to $4.5 trillion. Rates stopped moving, and volatility (the size of price swings) fell. The VIX, an index of the price swings investors expect in the S&P 500, averaged 11.1 in 2017, its lowest year since the series began in 1990 (FRED).

It got easier after 2021. Inflation returned. The Fed raised rates 11 times in 16 months while Japan stayed near zero, and the yen lost 29% against the dollar in under two years. Rates, currencies and commodities trended again. One macro manager, an interested party, links the style’s returns to central-bank activity (Graham Capital, Jan 2025).

What an individual can and cannot take from macro#

The inputs macro traders watch are free:

Signal Where Latest reading
Yield curve (10-year minus 2-year) FRED, series T10Y2Y +0.29 points, 23 Nov 2026
Fed policy Fed statements 3.75–4.00%, raised 16 Sep 2026
The dollar against a basket of currencies FRED, series DTWEXBGS 123.60, 20 Nov 2026
Credit spread for lower-rated (“high-yield”) companies FRED, series BAMLH0A0HYM2 3.76 points, 20 Nov 2026
Volatility (VIX) FRED, series VIXCLS 35.89, 20 Nov 2026
Market breadth (the share of stocks that are rising) most charting sites varies by measure

Professionals rarely trade these one by one, because none gives a date. They read them together to judge whether conditions favour taking risk, and set position size to match. A rising policy rate, a widening credit spread, a rising dollar and narrowing breadth describe conditions in which leveraged buyers are being squeezed.

What to take from it
  • Macro data works better as a setting for how much risk to carry than as a signal for when to buy or sell.
  • The Fed’s direction is the first input. In 1994 and 2022 its rate rises caused the losses. In 2008 and 2020 its response marked the turn.
  • A view is not a position. Macro traders lose most often by being early with leverage.

For a long-term investor#

The record supports sitting through declines more than it supports acting on macro signals. The S&P 500 fell 33.9% in early 2020 and ended the year up 18%. The yield curve inverted in July 2022 and stocks then gained 26% in 2023 and 25% in 2024. An investor adding to index funds on a schedule who had stopped at the inversion would have missed both years. The year that did hurt a balanced holder, 2022, came from rising rates and not from recession.

A hypothesis to check

Scheduled index purchases made in months when the credit spread was above its five-year average earned higher returns over the next five years than purchases made in other months. FRED and index data are enough to check it.

For a swing trader holding days to weeks#

Strategies that buy leading stocks on pullbacks and breakouts depend on other buyers following through, and that depends on liquidity. A swing trader who wants to avoid giving back gains when the market turns could test the macro set as a filter on exposure: full size when policy, credit and breadth are supportive, reduced size when they are not.

Two hypotheses to check
  • Breakout trades entered while the high-yield credit spread was rising over the prior 20 days gave back more open profit than trades entered while it was falling.
  • The largest give-backs in a trade history cluster in the weeks after a VIX close above 25.

For a day trader#

Macro matters to intraday trading mainly through the calendar. Fed decisions, inflation reports and jobs reports arrive at known times. Opening range breakout strategies on a 5-minute chart rest on the first minutes setting a range that holds, and a scheduled release can reset that range.

Two hypotheses to check
  • Opening range breakouts have a lower win rate, or a different average result in R (the fixed amount risked per trade), on Fed decision days than on other days.
  • A fixed profit target in R is reached more often on days when the VIX closed above 20 the day before, because ranges are wider.

What does not transfer#

  • Leverage terms. A fund borrows through a prime broker on institutional terms, with staff and risk controls around the position. The leverage can be copied; the controls cannot.
  • Information flow. Large macro funds talk with banks, policy makers and other funds. Soros met the head of Germany’s central bank days before the 1992 trade (Naef).
  • 24-hour markets. Futures trade from Sunday evening to Friday evening. A fund has people awake for all of it.
  • A team and time. Lock-ups let a fund hold a losing position for months. A margin account can be closed out in a day.
