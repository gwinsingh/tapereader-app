Michael Burry: the price of being early

Burry's one documented record (2000 to mid-2008, about 26% a year after fees, known from investor letters) came from reading documents nobody else read and from bets whose loss was capped at a premium; since 2008 no returns have been published, and his public market warnings since 2019 were followed by a higher index far more often than by a lower one.

Michael Burry is a former doctor who ran the hedge fund (a private fund that can bet on falls as well as rises) Scion Capital from 2000 to 2008, a second firm, Scion Asset Management, from 2013 to 2025, and since November 2025 a paid newsletter. He is known for one trade: from 2005 he bought insurance against the failure of subprime mortgage bonds (bonds backed by home loans to weak borrowers), paid for it while the fund fell behind a rising market for two years, and was proved right in 2007. The first fund’s record is strong and reasonably well documented. Nothing after 2008 can be measured, and his many public warnings since 2019 were mostly followed by rising prices. The one thing worth taking from him is the design of the bet: a thesis with a mechanism and a date, held through an instrument that could lose only a known amount.

The vocabulary used here (fund fees, lock-ups, side pockets, 13F filings, short selling, put options, credit default swaps) is explained in the hedge fund equity primer. How each profile in the series is researched and graded is in the method post.

How he got started#

Burry studied economics at UCLA alongside the courses required for medical school, then trained as a doctor at Vanderbilt. By his own account he read Benjamin Graham and Warren Buffett during those years and owned more investment books than medical ones by 1996 (MSN biography page, archived 2002). He has said in public that he lost his left eye to a childhood cancer at the age of two, and that he has Asperger’s syndrome (Vanderbilt lecture, 5 Apr 2011).

Most accounts start in 2005. The record starts in 1996, on the internet. He posted stock analysis on message boards (Moneyweek, 12 May 2017), opened his own site in 1997, and wrote for MSN while working as a neurology resident at Stanford. Lewis reports that he wrote between midnight and three in the morning, around 16-hour hospital shifts (Vanity Fair, above). In 2000 and 2001 MSN ran a public contest called Strategy Lab, in which he managed a model portfolio and explained every purchase and sale. Archived copies survive (collection of the MSN pages) and are the best primary source on his method.

He left medicine on 30 June 2000. His capital was small: Lewis gives $40,000 of assets against $145,000 of student loans, plus $50,000 from his mother and brothers. Two readers of his site then changed the scale. Joel Greenblatt’s Gotham Capital paid $1m, after tax at Burry’s request, for about a quarter of the new firm. The insurer White Mountains paid $600,000 for a further share and promised $10m to manage (Vanity Fair, above). His first letter records the stakes as options on 22.5% and up to 15.44% of the management company, and says he had more than $1m of his own in the fund (Scion letter, 8 Jan 2001).

The fund opened on 1 November 2000. Its fee terms were unusual. There was no yearly fee on assets, only actual costs, and a share of profits above a 6% return (Scion letter and Vanity Fair, above). Most hedge funds then charged 2% of assets a year whatever the result.

Where the edge came from#

Behaviour: holding an unpopular position for years Paid premiums and reported a loss in 2006 while investors demanded their money In principle
Structure of the bet: loss capped, gain many times larger Swaps costing about 2% a year on bonds that a 7% loss in the loan pool would wipe out Partly. Listed options yes, custom swaps with banks no
Structure of the fund: locked capital and a side pocket Investors could not withdraw the swap positions in 2006–07 No
Information None that was private. He used public filings and a filings database Yes
Luck or era One very large pay-off in 25 years; the first three years coincided with the collapse of the technology bubble, which favoured cheap small stocks No

The analytical edge is the clearest. The mortgage bonds were sold with prospectuses that listed the features of the loans inside: how much was lent against each house, how many loans had no proof of income, how many charged interest only. Burry read them and went looking for the worst pools. The banks that sold him protection priced it by the bond’s credit rating and did not distinguish between pools (Vanity Fair, above).

The second edge was structural. A credit default swap (a contract that pays out if a bond fails, in return for a yearly premium) had a known maximum cost. In Lewis’s account he says of the swaps: “my downside was defined and certain.” A plain short sale (selling borrowed shares to buy them back cheaper) has no such limit.

Like Bill Ackman, he runs a concentrated book and once made a very large credit bet with swaps. Ackman campaigns in public to change companies. Burry reads, waits and writes.

The process#

Finding ideas#

The MSN strategy page sets it out (MSN strategy page, archived 2001). He screened large numbers of companies on enterprise value (the market value of the shares plus debt, less cash) divided by EBITDA (profit before interest, tax, depreciation and amortisation). He ignored price-to-earnings ratios, preferred companies with little debt, and looked mainly in industries that the market had abandoned. A second group, which he called rare birds, covered companies priced below the value of their assets and durable businesses of the kind Warren Buffett favours, when they were cheap.

Lewis gives an example from 2001. Avanti, a software company, had executives facing prison for stealing a rival’s code. It also had $100m in cash, about $100m a year of free cash flow (cash left after running and maintaining the business) and a market value of only $250m. Burry judged it worth more than its price even after the fines (Vanity Fair, above).

Forming a thesis#

For stocks the thesis was value with a margin of safety (a price far enough below estimated worth to absorb mistakes). He wrote that the level of the general market mattered little to him and that he needed no known catalyst (MSN strategy page, above). The background is in the value investing primer.

The mortgage thesis was different in kind. It had a mechanism and a date. Loans made in early 2005 carried a low fixed rate for two years and then reset, in Lewis’s example from 6% to 11%. Borrowers who could barely pay the first rate would default at the second. Losses would start in 2007, and he expected the trade to take about three years (Vanity Fair, above).

Timing the entry#

He used price, which is unusual for a value investor. He called it “barebones technical analysis” on the MSN strategy page, and gave a rule: buy within 10% to 15% of a 52-week low that has shown support (a price level where selling has repeatedly stopped). On 9 August 2000 he passed on stocks he liked because they had risen more than 15% above support (MSN collection, above).

Sizing#

In the MSN period he held 12 to 18 stocks and stayed fully invested. The later filings show a more concentrated habit: between 1 and 33 positions a quarter, with the five largest making up 29% to 100% of the shares reported (SEC EDGAR, computed). The clearest recent data point is his own statement that the Palantir puts reported in November 2025 cost $9.2m (Stocktwits, Nov 2025). Against the $155m in the firm’s last adviser form, that is about 6% of capital at risk in one idea.

Managing and exiting#

The early rule was mechanical. If a stock other than a rare bird broke to a new low, he usually sold. On 28 September 2000 he sold Caterpillar for that reason and wrote that one position must never ruin a portfolio (MSN collection, above). He also sold into strength: he was content to take a quick 40% to 50% gain.

In the mortgage trade he did the opposite of cutting. He held, and in 2005 kept adding, while the position cost money and showed no gain. In 2026 he again added to losing Nvidia puts (TheStreet via Yahoo, 11 Apr 2026). The difference between the two habits is the instrument. A stock at a new low can keep falling. A swap or a put (the right to sell at a set price until a set date) cannot lose more than was paid for it.

Most of what he does today is not known: his cash, his short positions, his private holdings and his results.

Risk and drawdowns#

The stated rules are short: a margin of safety, no single position large enough to ruin the portfolio, a cut on a new low. From 2005 he added a preference for instruments with a fixed maximum loss.

The worst year on record is 2006. The fund lost 18.16% while the S&P 500 returned 15.79% with dividends, a gap of 34 points (table attributed to Scion’s letters). Summaries of Lewis’s book give 18.4%; the letters table is taken here. He was paying premiums on the swaps, and the banks that had sold them set their value at each month end, usually against him (Vanity Fair, above).

Side pocket and negative carry

A side pocket is a separate account inside a fund for positions that cannot be valued or sold easily. Investors who leave cannot take that part with them until it is sold. Negative carry means a position costs money every day it is held, as an insurance premium does, until the event it insures against arrives.

The danger was greater than a bad year. His swap contracts let a bank cancel if the fund’s assets fell by set amounts: he described triggers of roughly 15% in a month, 30% in a quarter and 40% in a year (interview with Financial Crisis Inquiry Commission staff, 2010, transcript copy). Withdrawals could therefore have ended the trade before it paid. By his own later telling he closed the Hong Kong office, cut pay, laid off staff, sold his swaps on financial companies at low prices, and moved the mortgage swaps into a side pocket. He considered liquidating the fund in December 2006. Investors threatened to sue (Vanderbilt lecture, above).

The record#

Scion Capital, November 2000 to mid-2008#

Lewis gives the headline. An investor who stayed from 1 November 2000 to 30 June 2008 made 489.34% after fees and expenses, and the fund made 726% before them, against a little more than 2% for the S&P 500 (Vanity Fair, above). Over 7.66 years, 489.34% is 26.1% a year.

A year-by-year table attributed to the fund’s letters circulates online (hedgefundalpha, above). Its yearly figures compound exactly to its own cumulative figure of 472.40% to 31 March 2008, a check on its consistency. Lewis’s figures for 2001, 2002 and 2003 (55%, 16%, 50%) match the table’s before-fee column. That explains a common confusion: 2007 was 166.91% before fees and 138.27% after, and press figures of about 150% sit between the two.

Record confidence
  • Scion Capital, 2000 to mid-2008: grade B. Several reliable outlets report the same figures, Lewis worked from the letters and emails, and the first letter can be read. The one underlying source is the firm. Nothing is audited in public.
  • Scion Asset Management, 2013 to 2025: grade D. No return was ever published. The filings are a reliable list of holdings and say nothing certain about results.
  • Own money since November 2025: grade D. Positions and gains are self-reported in a paid newsletter.

Scion Asset Management, 2013 to 2025#

He reopened under a new name in 2013. The firm never published a return, and no reliable outlet has reported one. Bloomberg put its assets at about $343m in September 2019 (Bloomberg via InvestmentNews, 4 Sep 2019), and the last adviser form shows $154,933,555 in four accounts (Form ADV, above). In November 2025 he told Bloomberg that the firm’s final version had been in effect a fund for friends and family that he never marketed (Hedgeweek, 19 Nov 2025).

What exists is the quarterly Form 13F. The firm filed it for four quarters in 2015–16 and for every quarter from December 2018 to September 2025. All 33 filings (32 quarterly reports and one amendment) were read for this profile.

The filings show a small book of shares with occasional very large option lines. Shares never exceeded $165.4m in any quarter. Four quarters carry option rows that push the reported total above $1bn.

The share positions follow the old method: unloved, often small companies. Examples include private prison operators, shipping companies, health insurers and Chinese internet groups (Alibaba, JD.com and Baidu were among the largest holdings in 2023–24, at times with puts on the same names as a hedge). In the first quarter of 2025 he sold all but one stock and held puts on Nvidia and five Chinese companies. One quarter later the puts were gone and the filing listed call options (the right to buy at a set price) instead.

Signature trades#

The mortgage trade, 2005 to 2008#

Thesis. Lenders were handing out loans that borrowers could afford only at a two-year opening rate. When the rate reset, defaults would follow. The bonds built from those loans were cut into layers called tranches (slices that take losses in a set order). The BBB-rated slices sat near the bottom and would be worth nothing if about 7% of the loan pool was lost (Vanity Fair, above).

Instrument. He could not sell the bonds short, because they could not be borrowed. Shorting home builders was indirect and dangerous. A swap on a specific bond did the job. The price depended only on the rating: about 0.20% a year for the safest slice, 0.50% for A-rated slices and about 2% for BBB (Vanity Fair, above). He told the inquiry staff that he paid roughly 0.85% to 2.75% a year across the slices he bought (FCIC transcript, above).

Size. Sources give different totals because they measure at different dates. Lewis has $750m at the end of July 2005 and at least $1bn in October. Burry’s own later figure for the peak is about $1.8bn on mortgage bonds and about $6.6bn on the debt of financial companies such as AIG, Countrywide and Washington Mutual, bought from nine banks (Vanderbilt lecture, above). The fund was about $600m.

The wait. His investors had hired a stock picker. Lewis records their objections: why would Goldman Sachs sell something so good, and why call the top of a 70-year housing cycle. One original backer wanted him to return to stocks. Greenblatt pressed him to drop the trade and questioned the side pocket, and later said he had asked for money because his own clients were withdrawing (summary of the book’s chapter 8). Burry answered that he was not betting on catastrophe: the swaps would pay if only a small share of the pools went bad.

Outcome. By Lewis’s account the trade made $725m for investors and $100m for Burry. The fund returned 138.27% after fees in 2007. He closed the mortgage swaps by about March 2008 (FCIC transcript, above). Holding period: about three years, as planned.

What he gave up. To survive 2006 he sold the swaps on financial companies. Several of those companies failed or were rescued in 2008. The famous trade was the smaller half of what he had built.

Unloved stocks, 2000 to 2003#

The first three years were ordinary stock picking with extraordinary results: +44.67% after fees in 2001 against −11.88% for the index, and +13.10% in 2002 against −22.10% (letters table, above). By mid-2005 the fund was up 242% since its start while the index was down 6.84% (Vanity Fair, above). He used no borrowed money and did not short stocks. Positions were held for months.

GameStop, 2019 to 2020#

In 2019 he bought shares of the video-game retailer GameStop and wrote three letters to its board, on 28 July, 16 August and 26 August, urging it to use its cash to finish a $300m share buy-back. A filing of 10 April 2020 shows 3,400,000 shares, 5.3% of the company, bought for about $13.6m in total (Schedule 13D, 10 Apr 2020). The quarterly filings then show the stake shrinking: 2,750,000 shares in June 2020, 1,703,400 in September, none in December.

In January 2021 buyers organised online drove the stock up in a short squeeze (a rise that forces short sellers to buy, which pushes the price higher still). The closing peak was $347.51 on 27 January 2021, in the share count of the time. He called the rally “unnatural, insane, and dangerous” (Bloomberg via Al Jazeera, 27 Jan 2021). A profit of about $100m is often quoted for the trade (not verified). The filings do not support it.

Losses and misses#

Tesla puts, 2021. The filing of 17 May 2021 listed puts on 800,100 Tesla shares, reported at $534.4m. That figure is the value of the shares, not the cost of the puts. The next filing, on 16 August, listed puts on 1,075,500 shares, reported at $731.0m, and puts on the ARK Innovation fund. By 30 September 2021 every option line was gone (SEC EDGAR). Tesla’s share price rose 16% across the two quarters in which the puts were reported, and 89% in the six months after the first disclosure. He told CNBC in October 2021: “No, it was a trade,” and added that the press had overstated its size many times over (Reuters via Euronews, 16 Oct 2021). Cost and result are unknown; the price moved against it.

“Sell.” and the index puts, 2023. On 31 January 2023 he posted one word, “Sell.” (Fortune, 1 Feb 2023). On 30 March he posted: “I was wrong to say sell” (Kitco, 31 Mar 2023). The filing for 30 June 2023 then listed puts on 2,000,000 shares each of funds tracking the S&P 500 and the Nasdaq 100, reported at $1.6bn together: again the value of the shares covered, with the cost unknown. They were gone a quarter later. The two funds fell 3.6% and 3.0% during that quarter, so the puts may have made a little. The S&P 500 was 18.9% higher a year after the “Sell.” post.

Semiconductor puts, 2023. The filing for 30 September 2023 listed puts on a semiconductor fund, reported at $47.4m. The fund rose 21.6% in the following quarter. The puts were gone by December. The Nvidia puts of 2025–26 are covered in the next section.

What the tally can and cannot show
  • A post is not a position. He may have held nothing, or a hedge, or have sold within days.
  • A filing shows a position six or seven weeks late, without its cost, strike price or expiry. The puts of 2021 and 2023 were gone by the next filing.
  • Some calls were right about something else. His warning of February 2021 to prepare for inflation (date not verified) was followed by US inflation of 9.1% in the year to June 2022 (Bureau of Labor Statistics).
  • The index fell 25% between January and October 2022, after his 2021 warnings. Twelve-month windows are a blunt measure, and fourteen calls are a small sample.

Two cases stand on the other side. In 2000 he was publicly short Amazon near the top of the technology bubble, as he recalled in his first newsletter post (CNBC, 24 Nov 2025, above). In 2005 to 2007 he was early and right at scale. The base problem is the cost of the wait. A warning that is three years early costs money every day until it is right, and the investor has to still be solvent, and still be trusted, on the day it turns.

The AI calls, November 2025 to November 2026#

On 3 November 2025 the firm’s last filing listed puts on 5,000,000 Palantir shares, reported at $912.1m, and on 1,000,000 Nvidia shares, reported at $186.6m. Palantir’s chief executive, Alex Karp, said on television that betting against the two companies making the money was “super weird” (Fortune, 4 Nov 2025). Burry replied that he had paid $1.84 a share for the Palantir puts, $9.2m in all (Stocktwits, above).

A letter to investors dated 27 October 2025 said his sense of value had been out of step with markets for some time and that capital would be returned by the end of the year (Financial Times via Irish Times, 14 Nov 2025). The registration ended on 10 November 2025 (SEC adviser record, above). On 24 November he launched a newsletter, Cassandra Unchained, at $379 a year (CNBC, above), writing that the rules for a registered adviser had limited what he could say in public (newsletter’s “about” page). By his own count it had 300,044 subscribers in July 2026, free and paid together; the paid share is not disclosed (Stocktwits, 13 Jul 2026).

His main argument concerns depreciation (the yearly charge that spreads the cost of equipment over its useful life). The large cloud companies have lengthened the assumed life of their servers and chips. He estimated that this would understate depreciation by $176bn over 2026 to 2028 and overstate profits by 26.9% at Oracle and 20.8% at Meta (Fortune, 13 Nov 2025).

Nvidia answered in a memo to analysts. It rejected any likeness to past accounting frauds and said he had miscounted its share buy-backs by including tax paid on staff share awards; other reports add that it put its customers’ depreciation periods at four to six years (not verified) (Business Insider via Yahoo, 25 Nov 2025). He replied that the memo answered claims he had not made: “No one cares about Nvidia’s own depreciation” (Business Insider via AOL, Nov 2025). His point was about Nvidia’s customers.

Measured at 12 months, on 3 November 2026, the S&P 500 was up 10.5%, Nvidia was down 0.1%, Palantir down 15.3% and Oracle down 51.1%. Then came a sharp fall. The S&P 500 lost 4.4% on 5 November 2026, and by 17 November Nvidia was 25% below its level of 3 November 2025. The Associated Press named as causes the price of AI shares, high interest rates and the midterm election result, and reported that Nvidia’s chief executive had resigned (AP via Atlanta Journal-Constitution, 17 Nov 2026). None of those is the depreciation argument.

Whether he profited cannot be known. By reports of his newsletter, his Nvidia puts had strike prices of $100 to $115, and Nvidia’s lowest confirmed close in the period was $155.46. He sold the puts expiring in December 2026 on 9 September 2026, before the loss of value that options suffer near expiry, having bought call options as a hedge in August (IBTimes UK, 10 Sep 2026). In late September he swapped short positions in Micron and a semiconductor fund for puts (CNBC, 28 Sep 2026); Micron fell by about a third by 17 November. He has said his shorts were profitable. One reviewer noted that the claim rests on his word (24/7 Wall St., 6 Aug 2026).

Criticism and mistakes#

The long list of warnings. The columnist Proinsias O’Mahony listed forecasts that missed, among them a 2022 suggestion that the S&P 500 could fall below 1,900 when its low was near 3,500, and noted that critics call him a one-trick pony, which the columnist thought harsh (Irish Times, 1 Dec 2025). The table above supports the criticism for market-wide calls and not for single assets.

The targets answer back. Cathie Wood wrote in August 2021, after the ARK puts appeared: “I do not believe that he understands the fundamentals” (CNBC, 17 Aug 2021). Her fund fell 57% in the following year. Karp and Nvidia are quoted above; on those the outcome is still open.

The depreciation argument. The checkable facts lean his way on direction: Meta lengthened the life of some servers to 5.5 years from January 2025, and The Economist estimated that a three-year life would cut the large firms’ combined yearly pre-tax profit by about $26bn, or 8% (Fortune, 13 Nov 2025, above). Against him, CoreWeave’s chief executive said most customer contracts run five years and older chips keep their value (24/7 Wall St. via Yahoo, 24 Mar 2026). Two limits apply. Depreciation is an accounting charge: it changes reported profit, not the cash a company produces. And the $176bn is his own estimate; no source found checks it.

No record since 2008. A Bloomberg columnist asked in November 2025 whether he really understood value investing or AI, and called him too famous for his own good (Bloomberg Opinion, 18 Nov 2025). Fame from 2007 gives his posts a weight that 17 years without published results cannot support.

The side pocket, from the investors’ side. They had hired a stock picker, found their money in a credit bet several times the size of the fund, asked for it back, and were refused. The fund documents allowed it and the outcome favoured them. It worked only because the thesis was right.

Posts that vanish. He has deleted his account or his posts many times, often within hours, including the “Sell.” post. He has said that compliance rules forced him to be cryptic (newsletter about page, above). For a reader the effect is that warnings are loud and corrections quiet.

A paid newsletter about his own positions. He now earns subscription income by writing about securities he says he holds or is short. Readers cannot check the positions, prices or timing. No named critic making this point was found in a source that could be opened.

Regulators. He said in September 2021 that he had received a subpoena in the regulator’s GameStop inquiry (CNBC, 27 Sep 2021). The adviser record shows no disciplinary events.

Routine and mindset#

He works alone. Lewis describes the decision-making of a $600m fund as one man in a room with the door shut, reading. Burry told him that meetings went well only with people who had read his writing first, so he raised money by writing and waiting for readers to find him (Vanity Fair, above).

He dislikes defending ideas in conversation, for a reason that carries over. Once he had argued for a position in front of investors, he became its defender and found it harder to change his mind.

By his own account he wanted to fall asleep and wake in 2007. When he was proved right, almost none of his investors said so. He stopped managing outside money twice: in 2008, after the revolt and the pay-off, and in 2025, writing that his view of value no longer matched the market’s.

Between the two funds he invested his own money. He told Bloomberg Television in 2010 that he was buying farmland with water on site, gold, and small technology companies in Asia (Bloomberg via InvestmentNews, 12 Sep 2010).

The film’s director said he listens to speed metal (History vs Hollywood). He is married with children.

What carries over#

Investing (years)#

  • A warning from a famous name is not a signal. None of his six market-wide calls was followed by a lower index six months later. For investors who add to index funds on a schedule, this is evidence on the cost of acting on such warnings.
  • Being right and early is the same as being wrong until the market agrees. In 2006 he was right about the loans and down 18%. He stayed in the game because the position could not lose more than its premiums and because investors could not withdraw.
  • Primary documents are open to everyone. His edge in 2005 came from prospectuses that anyone could download.

Swing trading (days to weeks)#

  • A thesis needs a trigger and a date. His case against Tesla in 2021 rested on valuation, and the stock rose 89% in six months. The mortgage case rested on a rate reset due in 2007. A swing trader who fades extended stocks could record, for each trade, whether a dated trigger existed.
  • The instrument decides who survives the wait. A short position in a rising stock grows as it loses and can be squeezed. A put loses at most its price, but loses that price steadily. Neither is free.
  • Buy weakness only at a level that has held, and leave if it breaks. His 2000 rule was to buy within 10% to 15% of a 52-week low with support and to cut on a new low: a counter-trend entry with a stop set by the chart.
  • Headlines built from filings are stale and mis-sized. They appear up to 45 days after the quarter and report the shares under an option, not the money at risk.

Day trading (minutes to hours)#

Nothing in his method is intraday. He reads for months and trades a few times a year. Three things transfer honestly, and no more.

  • Fading a strong trend needs a trigger and a fixed risk. His successful counter-trend bet had both. Most of his later ones had only the fixed risk.
  • Cut on a new low. The MSN-era rule is a price stop in plain form. He credited it with keeping any one position from damaging the whole.
  • A famous name’s post is not a setup. The price move in the minutes after such a post reflects attention, not information.

Hypotheses worth checking
  • In a trader’s own log, trades taken against the prevailing trend have a lower expectancy (average result per trade, in units of risk) than trades taken with it. A second cut: counter-trend trades with a dated trigger against those without.
  • Buying a three-month index put on each of the dated market-wide warnings in the table lost money in total. Historical option prices are needed to check it.
  • For stocks bought near a 52-week low, a rule of selling on a close below that low reduces the worst losses more than it reduces the average gain.
What to take from it
  • The forecast was the smaller part of 2007. The structure of the bet (a known maximum loss, capital that could not leave) was the larger part.
  • A thesis with a mechanism and a date can be checked as time passes. A view that prices are too high cannot.
  • The wait costs money and trust. He paid both, and sold the larger half of the position to keep the smaller.
  • Since 2008 there is no record, only posts and filings. Both are poor signals.

What cannot be copied#

  • The swaps. Custom contracts with large banks, under legal agreements he negotiated bank by bank from 2003 to 2005 (FCIC transcript, above), are not open to individuals. Listed puts are the nearest tool, and most expire within months.
  • Locked capital. Investors in the first fund could not leave at will, and the side pocket held the key position in place. An individual has the opposite problem: nothing stops a nervous exit.
  • The time. The edge was thousands of hours of reading.
  • The moment. Protection on the weakest mortgage bonds at 2% a year was on offer for a few months in 2005.

The 2007 result depended on all four. The reading habit and the early rules did not.

What to learn first#

  • Fund terms: performance fee, lock-up, side pocket.
  • Short selling and squeezes: why the loss on a short has no ceiling.
  • Options: premium, strike price, expiry, loss of value over time.
  • Notional value: the amount covered, against the amount paid.
  • Credit default swaps and tranches.
  • Negative carry: a position that costs money to hold.
  • Form 13F: what it shows and what it leaves out.

These are covered in the hedge fund equity primer and the value investing primer.
