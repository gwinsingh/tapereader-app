Value investing: a primer on buying businesses for less than they are worth

Value investing means buying a share of a business for well below a careful estimate of its worth, and the record shows the idea is sound while cheap stocks lagged for most of 2007 to 2025 and most stock pickers trail an index.

Value investing is the style of buying shares as part-ownership of a business, at a price well below what the business is worth, and holding them for years. Warren Buffett and Peter Lynch, both profiled in this series, built their records this way. The one thing worth taking from the style: over long periods a share returns what the business earns, and the price paid decides how much of that the buyer keeps. Everything needed to check both is free and public.

This primer is the background for those two profiles. It is a study of a style, not advice.

What value investing is#

A share is a piece of a business. The price of that piece and its value are different things. The value investor buys only when the price is well below the value.

A trader working from price movement asks where the price is likely to go next. A macro trader asks whether a whole market is priced for the wrong economy (see the global macro primer). A value investor asks what one business will earn over the next ten years and what that is worth today.

What a share is and where the return comes from#

A share (also called a stock) is a fraction of a company. Its owner has a claim on a fraction of the profits. The return from holding it has three parts:

  • Dividends: cash the company pays out to its owners.
  • Growth in earnings: earnings is another word for profit. If profit per share doubles and nothing else changes, the share price doubles.
  • Change in the multiple: the multiple is the price divided by yearly earnings, written P/E. If buyers pay 20 times earnings today and 25 times later, the price rises 25% with no change in the business.

The first two come from the business. The third comes from the mood of buyers. John Bogle, the founder of the index fund firm Vanguard, called the first two the investment return and the third the speculative return. By his figures, US stocks returned 9.1% a year from 1900 to 2009: 4.3 points from dividends, 4.5 from earnings growth and 0.3 from the multiple (Bogle’s table, as reported by Ben Carlson).

The same split can be worked out for the S&P 500, an index of 500 large US companies, from year-end data kept by Aswath Damodaran of New York University (index, earnings and dividends; yearly returns). The figures below were calculated for this primer from those tables. From 1961 to 2025 the index returned 10.65% a year. Dividends gave 2.82 points, earnings growth 7.12 and the multiple 0.46, as the P/E rose from 18.7 to 25.2.

Over one decade the multiple can dominate. In the 1970s earnings grew 9.08% a year, but the P/E fell from 15.1 to 7.4 and the return was only 5.92%. In the 1990s the P/E doubled from 14.5 to 28.4. The next ten years returned −0.95% a year.

In the 2010s almost all of the 13.44% a year came from the business: earnings grew 10.53% a year from a low base after the 2008 crisis, and the multiple added 0.63. From 2020 to 2025 the return was 14.93% a year, and 4.02 points of it, about a quarter, came from the P/E rising from 19.9 to 25.2.

What to take from it
  • Dividends and earnings growth have been the steady part of the return. The multiple swings both ways and has added little over 65 years.
  • A period that begins at a high multiple starts with the speculative part working against it.

Reading the three financial statements from zero#

Every US listed company files an annual report, called a 10-K, with the Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC, the US markets regulator). The reports are free on its EDGAR site. Each holds three statements. The example here is Costco Wholesale, a retailer that sells groceries and general goods in bulk from 939 warehouses to 84.1 million paying members. All figures are from its 10-K for the 52 weeks ended 30 August 2026 (Costco 10-K, filed 7 Oct 2026).

The income statement: what it earned#

The income statement starts with revenue (everything customers paid) and subtracts costs until profit is left.

Of every $100 that came in, $87.18 paid for the goods and $8.97 ran the company. Operating income was $3.85. The membership fees of $5,907 million equal 51% of operating income: the fees are about half the profit, and the goods are sold near cost.

The balance sheet: what it owns and owes#

The balance sheet is a list on one date. Assets are what the company owns. Liabilities are what it owes. The difference is equity (also called book value), the owners’ share.

Owns (assets), $ million Owes (liabilities), $ million
Cash 20,207 Bills owed to suppliers 22,591
Goods in stock (inventories) 19,324 Long-term debt 6,162
Land, buildings, equipment 35,633 Membership fees paid in advance 3,006
Everything else 13,881 Everything else 21,483
Total assets 89,045 Total liabilities 53,242
Equity (assets minus liabilities) 35,803

Costco owes its suppliers more ($22.6 billion) than the stock on its shelves cost ($19.3 billion), so suppliers pay for the inventory. Its cash is more than three times its debt.

The cash flow statement: profit against cash#

Profit is an accounting figure. Cash is what arrived in the bank. They differ because some costs are not paid in cash that year (depreciation spreads the price of a building over its life), some pay is in shares, and the timing of payments moves cash without touching profit.

Costco reported net income of $9,226 million but its operations brought in $15,817 million of cash. It then spent $6,435 million on new warehouses and equipment, which is called capital spending. What is left is free cash flow: cash from operations minus capital spending, the money the owners could take out without shrinking the business. For Costco that was $9,382 million. Dividends took $2,458 million and share buybacks (the company buying its own shares) $848 million.

A company whose profit stays well above its free cash flow for years is spending heavily to stand still, or is booking sales it has not collected.

The numbers describe two different things. The business is excellent: it earns 23% on the money invested in it and has almost no debt. The price is high: a buyer receives $1.88 of profit a year for each $100 paid, when a US government bond paid $4.92 (FRED). The price assumes many more years of growth; profit grew 14% in fiscal 2026.

One year’s dividend can mislead

On 10 November 2026 Costco declared a special dividend of $30 a share, payable on 4 December (8-K). That is 2.7% of the share price and about $13.3 billion, most of the cash surplus on the August balance sheet. Ratios taken from a single date need a look at what has happened since.

The whole market#

The same measures exist for the S&P 500. On 23 November 2026 its P/E was about 24.7, against an average of 16.2 since 1871 (multpl). Its earnings yield was 4.05% and its dividend yield 1.12%, near the lowest on record.

One year’s earnings can be distorted by a recession. The economist Robert Shiller’s fix is the cyclically adjusted P/E, or CAPE: the price divided by the average of ten years of earnings, adjusted for inflation. It stood at 38.2, against an average of 17.45. Its record is 44.19, in December 1999 (multpl, from Shiller’s data).

Intrinsic value and discounting#

Intrinsic value is what a business is worth to an owner who keeps it: the cash it will pay out over its life, expressed in today’s money. Buffett, following the economist John Burr Williams, defines it as the cash an asset will produce, discounted (1992 letter).

Discounting is the step that needs explaining. A dollar next year is worth less than a dollar today, because today’s dollar can be invested in the meantime. If money can earn 9% a year, $91.74 today becomes $100 in a year. So $100 due in a year is worth $91.74 today, and $100 due in ten years is worth $42.24. The 9% is the discount rate: the return the buyer demands.

A discounted cash flow (DCF) applies this to every future year. Take an imaginary business:

  1. It will pay its owner $100 next year.
  2. The payment grows 6% a year for ten years, reaching $168.90 in year 10.
  3. After that it grows 3% a year for ever.
  4. The buyer wants 9% a year.

Each of the first ten payments is discounted to today: $91.70, $89.20, $86.80 and so on, down to $71.40 for year 10. They sum to $812. All payments after year 10 are valued in one step: the year-11 payment ($174) divided by the gap between the discount rate and the growth rate (9% − 3% = 6%) gives $2,900 at year 10, which is $1,225 today. The business is worth $812 + $1,225 = $2,037.

Sixty percent of that answer comes from the years after year 10, and small changes in the two rates move it a long way.

The honest conclusion is that the method gives a range and not a number. Its use is to show what a price assumes. A price of $2,900 for this business assumes cheap money and fast growth for ever.

Margin of safety and “Mr. Market”#

Both ideas come from Benjamin Graham’s book The Intelligent Investor, first published in 1949 (overview).

Margin of safety (chapter 20) is the gap between the price paid and the estimated value. It exists because the estimate will be wrong. In the example above, a buyer who insists on paying one third less than the middle estimate pays at most $1,358. That is below the lowest of the nine values. The forecast can fail in every direction shown and the buyer has still not overpaid. Buffett writes that a value only slightly above the price is not enough to justify buying (1992 letter).

Mr. Market (chapter 8) is Graham’s picture of the stock market as a business partner who offers every day to buy the investor’s share or sell his own. His prices swing with his mood. The investor is free to ignore him, and a new offer comes tomorrow (summary). A recent example: the S&P 500 closed at 7,340.53 on 17 November 2026 and at 6,910.39 three sessions later, a fall of 5.9% (FRED). The cash that 500 businesses will produce over the next twenty years did not change by 5.9% in three days. The price of it did.

How the style evolved#

Graham: net-nets and cigar butts (1930s–1950s). Graham bought shares priced below the company’s net current asset value: cash, stock and money owed by customers, minus every debt. Such a “net-net” was cheaper than its own liquid assets, with the factories free. A study of these shares for 1971–1983 found 33.7% a year against 12.1% for the benchmark (Oppenheimer, cited here). Buffett later named the method the cigar butt approach: a discarded business with one profitable puff left (1989 letter).

Fisher: growth at quality (1958). Philip Fisher looked for outstanding companies that could grow for decades, checked them by talking to customers, suppliers and competitors, and held on. He bought Motorola in 1955 and kept it until his death in 2004 (profile).

Munger: a wonderful business at a fair price. Buffett’s 1989 letter sets out the change. A cheap price on a poor business turns out less cheap than it looks, because the business keeps earning poor returns. He credits his partner Charlie Munger with seeing this first, and concludes that it is better to buy “a wonderful company at a fair price” (1989 letter).

Lynch: growth at a reasonable price. Lynch’s One Up on Wall Street (1989) made the PEG ratio popular: the P/E divided by the yearly growth rate of earnings in percent. By his rule of thumb a fairly priced company has a P/E equal to its growth rate, a PEG of 1 (PEG ratio). Costco’s P/E of 53.1 against 14% growth is a PEG of 3.8.

Fama and French: value by formula (1992). The economists Eugene Fama and Kenneth French sorted all US stocks by book value against price and found that the cheap group had earned more than the dear group. Their factor HML (“high minus low”) measures that gap every month (three-factor model).

Moats#

A moat is a durable advantage that stops competitors from taking a company’s profits. The term is associated with Buffett’s letters (overview). The usual kinds, with one example each chosen for this primer:

Kind How it protects profit Example
Brand Customers pay more for the name Coca-Cola
Switching costs Leaving is expensive or disruptive Microsoft’s office software
Network effects Each user makes the product more useful to the others Visa’s card network
Cost advantage The company can sell for less than rivals and still profit Costco
Scale The market supports only one or two suppliers A railroad such as Union Pacific
Licences and patents The law keeps others out A drug under patent; a regulated utility

The evidence for a moat is a high return on invested capital held for many years. Without a moat, high returns attract competitors until they fall to the cost of capital (the return lenders and owners demand). In Damodaran’s January 2026 data, US soft-drink companies earned 29.0% on capital against a cost of 6.3%, and software companies 29.3% against 9.3%. Airlines earned 7.9% against 6.7%, steel makers 6.7% against 7.8% and car makers 2.3% against 9.4% (Damodaran).

Compounding#

Compounding is earning a return on past returns. At 10% a year, $10,000 becomes $11,000, and the next year’s 10% is earned on $11,000.

The rule of 72 gives the doubling time: 72 divided by the yearly rate. At 10% money doubles in about 7.2 years, at 15% in 4.8, at 20% in 3.6.

$10,000 at 10 years 20 years 30 years 40 years
10% a year $25,937 $67,275 $174,494 $452,593
15% a year $40,456 $163,665 $662,118 $2,678,635
20% a year $61,917 $383,376 $2,373,763 $14,697,716

Two things follow. Small differences in rate become very large differences in money: 20% is twice 10%, and after 40 years it gives 32 times as much. And most of the money comes at the end: at 10%, 63% of the 40-year gain arrives in the last ten years. This is why the style prizes businesses that can reinvest at high returns for decades.

Fees compound too. A 1% yearly fee turns 10% into 9%. Over 40 years $10,000 then grows to $314,094 in place of $452,593. The fee has taken $138,498, or 31% of the final sum.

Does value work? The evidence#

The academic factor#

Kenneth French publishes the HML factor back to 1927. Averaged by decade, cheap stocks beat dear ones in every decade until the 2010s. Over 1927–2006 the gap averaged 5.9 points a year. Then it reversed: over 2007–2020 it averaged −6.7 points a year and was positive in 4 of 14 years. In 2020 alone it was −46.9 (French data library; averages calculated here).

The index version#

The Russell 1000 Value and Russell 1000 Growth indexes split large US companies by style. The chart uses the two iShares funds that track them, after their 0.18% yearly fee (fund returns).

From 2007 to 2020 the value fund multiplied money 2.3 times and the growth fund 5.3 times (6.2% against 12.6% a year, calculated here). In 2022, when interest rates rose, value lost 7.75% and growth 29.31%. Growth then led again for three years. In 2026 to 20 November the value fund is up 18.88% and the growth fund down 13.80%, the largest lead for value in the period. French’s HML was about +7% for January to September 2026 (monthly figures compounded here).

Two meanings of “value”#

An index needs a rule. FTSE Russell sorts stocks mainly by book value against price, together with two measures of growth (FTSE Russell). On 20 November 2026 the value fund’s holdings traded at 3.3 times book and 23.1 times earnings, the growth fund’s at 12.8 times book and 32.7 times earnings (iShares value fund; growth fund).

Buffett means something else. In his 1992 letter he calls the term “value investing” redundant, says growth is one of the inputs to value, and says a low P/E, a low price-to-book or a high dividend yield does not settle whether a purchase is good value (1992 letter). By his definition a company at 13.6 times book can be cheap and one at 0.5 times book can be dear. The drought in the charts above is a drought of the index test.

Value traps#

A value trap is a share that looks cheap on the measures and keeps falling because the business is shrinking.

  • Eastman Kodak. Revenue peaked near $16 billion in 1996. A Kodak engineer had built the first hand-held digital camera in 1975, and digital photography then removed the film business. Kodak filed for bankruptcy on 19 January 2012 (history).
  • Sears Holdings. Formed in 2005 from Sears and Kmart, it was held by some investors for its property. Revenue fell from about $53.0 billion in 2007 to $16.7 billion in 2018. It filed for bankruptcy on 15 October 2018 (history).

In both cases the assets were real and the earnings on them were vanishing. A low multiple of a falling number is not a margin of safety.

Can stock pickers beat an index? The base rates#

A base rate is how often something has happened in the past. Three matter here.

Most professional funds lag. S&P’s SPIVA scorecard compares actively managed funds (funds whose managers choose the stocks) with an index. Over the 20 years to December 2025, 92.9% of US large-company funds returned less than the S&P 500. Only 34.6% of the 760 funds that existed at the start still existed at the end (SPIVA U.S. Year-End 2025). At mid-2026 the 15-year figure was 90.5% (SPIVA data).

A few stocks produce the gains. Hendrik Bessembinder studied every US common stock since 1926. Most had lifetime returns below those of one-month Treasury bills (short US government loans, the usual stand-in for cash). The best-performing 4% of companies account for the entire net gain of the US stock market over bills; the rest together matched bills (Bessembinder, 2018). The net wealth created to December 2016 was $34.8 trillion, and 86 stocks produced half of it (Arizona State University).

Investors earn less than their funds. Morningstar’s “Mind the Gap” study compares a fund’s return with what the average dollar in it earned, which depends on when investors bought and sold. Over the ten years to December 2025, funds returned 9.9% a year and their investors earned 8.7%. The gap of 1.2 points a year came from buying after gains and selling after losses. It was 0.4 points for broad US stock funds and 1.2 for funds that hold one sector (WealthManagement.com, 6 Aug 2026).

What the base rates do and do not say

They do not say that skill is absent: the records in the two profiles are real. They say that the starting assumption for any stock picker is a result below the index.

Holding structures in one page#

The legal wrapper around a portfolio decides who can take money out and when, what the manager is paid and how gains are taxed. Lynch ran a mutual fund. Buffett ran partnerships and then a holding company. The structure shaped what each could do.

Insurance float#

Berkshire Hathaway, the holding company Buffett ran, owns insurers. An insurer is paid first and pays claims later. The money it holds in between is called float.

A worked example: an insurer collects $1,000 of premiums on 1 January. Claims and costs of $970 are paid, on average, a year later. Meanwhile the $1,000 is invested at 5% and earns $50. The insurer makes $30 from insurance and $50 from investing other people’s money. Even if claims and costs came to $1,020, a loss of $20 on insurance, it would have had the use of $1,000 for a year at a cost of 2%, less than a bank would charge.

Berkshire’s float was $176 billion at the end of 2025, up from $88 billion at the end of 2015 (2025 annual report).

What to take from it
  • Over decades the return comes from the business. Over a year or two it comes mostly from the multiple.
  • A valuation is a range. The margin of safety is the allowance for being wrong.
  • “Cheap on the measures” and “worth more than the price” are different tests.
  • The base rate for picking stocks is a result below the index.

For a long-term investor#

The base rates are the case for the index. An index fund holds every stock, so it owns the 4% that produce the gains, and its fee is a small fraction of 1%. The compounding table shows what a 1-point difference in fees is worth over 40 years.

The cost of the index is sitting through its falls. The five deepest falls of the S&P 500 since 1929, with dividends reinvested:

Four of the five took between 21 and 74 months to recover. Purchases made on a schedule during those months were made at lower prices.

A hypothesis to check

Scheduled index purchases made in months when the Shiller P/E was above 30 earned lower returns over the following ten years than purchases made below 20, yet still earned more than cash. Shiller’s monthly data is enough to check it.

For a swing trader holding days to weeks#

Fundamentals explain why a stock leads. The oldest evidence is the post-earnings-announcement drift: after a company reports a profit well above what was expected, its shares tend to keep rising for weeks. Ball and Brown documented it in 1968. Bernard and Thomas found in 1989 that stocks with the best earnings surprises beat those with the worst in 41 of 48 quarters from 1974 to 1985 (summary). Studies report 10% to 25% a year for buying the best and selling short the worst, though a 2018 paper found the pattern far weaker stock by stock than in the averages (Katz, McCubbins and McMullin).

Valuation is a poor timing tool. The Shiller P/E began 2023 at 28.3, 2024 at 32.0 and 2025 at 37.1, far above its average each time (multpl). The S&P 500 then returned 26.1%, 24.9% and 17.8% (Damodaran). A high multiple says little about the next few weeks.

Two hypotheses to check
  • In a trade history, breakouts and gap entries in stocks whose latest quarterly earnings grew more than 25% gave a higher average result than the same setups in stocks with flat or falling earnings.
  • Earnings gaps that came with a raised company forecast held their gains over the next 20 sessions more often than gaps without one.

For a day trader#

Almost nothing transfers. A holding period of minutes is too short for earnings, margins or moats to matter. Two things remain. One is the earnings calendar: results arrive on known dates, mostly outside trading hours, and the session after is not a normal one. The other is the discipline of knowing what is held and why, so that a trade meant to last an hour is not kept for a month on the strength of a story found afterwards.

A hypothesis to check

Opening range breakouts in a stock on the session after its earnings report have a different win rate and average result in R (the fixed amount risked per trade) than breakouts in the same stock on other days.

What does not transfer#

  • Permanent capital. A holding company cannot be forced to sell. An individual who may need the money can be.
  • Float. Cheap, long-dated borrowed money requires owning an insurer.
  • Access. Large investors speak to managements and are offered private deals.
  • Time. Reading one 10-K properly takes hours, and a professional reads hundreds a year.
