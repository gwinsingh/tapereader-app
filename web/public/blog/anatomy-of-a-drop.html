Anatomy of a drop: how market falls unfold and end

A 24-page study of US stock-market drops since 1950, built around the comparison of autumn 2026 with autumn 2018, and with 2000 and 2007 as the other path.

This is a multi-page study with interactive charts. It asks what a market drop looks like from the inside: what falls first, what refuses to fall, what a failed bounce looks like next to a real low, and how much of the recovery has to be given up before the turn can be trusted. It describes what happened and how often. It tests no trading strategy and gives no advice.

The data runs to the close of 20 November 2026. A drop is defined as a fall of 10% or more from the highest close of the previous 52 weeks.

What the study found#

  1. A 10% fall has been a fork, not a forecast. Of 35 earlier falls of 10%, 16 ended before −15% and 11 went to −20% or more.
  2. A chart that looks like an earlier one is weak evidence. Measured from the peak, 2018 ranked 53rd of 95 as a match for 2026, and the closest matches had no forecasting value.
  3. On the economic numbers, the October 2026 peak is closest to September 2018. March 2000 ranks third.
  4. What is falling looks more like early 2000 than 2018. The typical large stock is about flat and half of all stocks are up, while the previous leaders are down by a fifth and semiconductor stocks by more than 40% from their June high.
  5. What ended the 2018 fall is weaker now. Inflation gave the Federal Reserve room to change course then (2% and falling). It does not now (3.7% to 3.9% and rising). Valuations and the growth of borrowing to buy stocks are both far higher.
  6. A sharp bounce is not a low. There were 91 failed rallies against 35 real lows, and the two looked the same in their first five sessions. How many stocks took part, and whether the move lasted, separated them.
  7. Proof costs part of the move. The index was back above its 50-day average a median 7% off the low, and above its 200-day average a median 10% off the low.
  8. December 2018 gave no second chance to buy the low. The stages of proof took three weeks.
  9. Deep lows came with very few stocks in an uptrend. At every deep low since 1999, fewer than 21% of stocks were above their 200-day average. On 20 November 2026 the figure was 41%.
  10. In 2018 the whole fall came during regular trading hours. Gains made overnight were sold during the day two times in three, and that pattern flipped at the low.
  11. The safe groups lagged after every low. Consumer staples, utilities and health care fell less on the way down and rose less on the way up. Groups whose strength against the index turned up early went on to lead.
  12. Washout readings mark a zone, not a day. Readings of extreme selling showed where lows tend to form. A washout followed by a broad surge did best: 57% of those were final lows against a base rate of 19%, on only 12 lows.
  13. Profitable giants can still take decades to recover. Cisco, Microsoft, Intel and Oracle fell 60% to 90% from their 2000 highs and needed 15 to 25 years to regain them.
Limits

The stock lists are today’s index members, so results before about 2010 flatter the past: companies that failed are missing. Twelve lows is a small sample. The sections about 2026 describe the market as it stood on 20 November 2026 and are not updated automatically.

What is in it#

  • An atlas of every drop, with the failed rallies marked on each chart.
  • Bounces against bottoms: what the first five days looked like in each.
  • Sectors and leaders: what fell most, what held, what turned first, what led afterwards.
  • The day’s shape: overnight moves against regular hours.
  • Leverage, valuations and the news flow, then and now.
  • Chart drills for training the eye, and a glossary.
