Bill Ackman: ten positions and capital that cannot leave

Ackman's audited record is about 16% a year since 2004 from roughly ten large positions and a few cheap hedges, but it includes four losing years in a row, and the listed fund has trailed the S&P 500 since its own launch in 2012.

Michael Burry: the price of being early

Burry's one documented record (2000 to mid-2008, about 26% a year after fees, known from investor letters) came from reading documents nobody else read and from bets whose loss was capped at a premium; since 2008 no returns have been published, and his public market warnings since 2019 were followed by a higher index far more often than by a lower one.

Stan Druckenmiller: big bets, small losses

Druckenmiller's results came less from being right than from betting large in liquid markets when conviction was high, cutting fast when wrong, and standing aside when cold; the famous 30% a year rests on press reports of his own firm's numbers, not on a public audited series.

Ray Dalio: rules, many small bets, and a record in two halves

Ray Dalio built the largest hedge fund by writing his decisions down as testable rules and spreading risk over many unrelated bets, and the record shows the method worked very well until 2011, poorly from 2012 to 2024, and well again once the fund shrank.

Warren Buffett: patience, float and a record that changed after 1998

Buffett's 19.7% a year over 61 years is the best-documented record in investing, but it rests on cheap insurance money and permanent capital as much as on stock picking, nearly all of the lead over the index was built before 1999, and what an individual can take from it is the behaviour, not the structure.

Peter Lynch: a category for every stock

Lynch's 29.2% a year at Magellan is real and well documented, but two-thirds of its margin over the index came in four early years when the fund was tiny and closed to the public; what lasts is the habit of deciding what kind of position a stock is before buying it, because the kind sets what to expect and when to sell.

Jim Simons: a small edge, repeated millions of times

Medallion's reported 66% a year before fees is not in audited public filings but is corroborated by a Senate report and by the firm's own statements to a US regulator; it came from being right slightly more than half the time on millions of small, short, heavily borrowed trades in a fund capped near $10bn and closed to outsiders, and the funds Renaissance sold to the public did not repeat it.

Ken Griffin: the firm is the edge

Griffin's record comes from an organisation, not from one trader's calls: hundreds of hedged books under central risk control, financed with heavy leverage and locked-up capital, with costs charged to investors; the one year the financing failed, 2008, the funds lost 55%.
