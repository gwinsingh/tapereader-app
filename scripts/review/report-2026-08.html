The leak

Confirmed

When the market puts you 1R up, you more often than not give all of it back and finish negative.

Numbers
Of 33 positions that reached +1R while still held, median capture of their in-window peak is −10%. Across all 52 that reached +1R at any point, 16 closed green (31%). Before 21 Aug: 15 of 41 (37%). After: 1 of 11 (9%).
Mechanism
The stop never trails past entry, so a position 1R up still carries its full initial loss. Every decision to bank is discretionary, taken under live P&L pressure, and the default outcome of doing nothing is a round trip.
Verdict
This is the leak, and it connects the stop behaviour, the drawdown, and your own notes about exiting early.
Confirmed

Your stop has never once protected a profit — not in 296 trades across both books.

Numbers
0 of 70 live and 0 of 226 practice trades ever had the stop trailed to or beyond entry, including all 25 live trades that reached 2.5R (median stop high-water: 0.12R below entry). You raise stops on 38 of 70 trades, up to 7 times — never across entry. 0 of 15 winners were ever stopped out.
Mechanism
Read directly from the order ladder. Your stop system does one job — limiting the initial loss — and none of the work of protecting a gain.
Caveat
This does not prove a breakeven stop would earn more. That is untested, and every mechanical-stop counterfactual in this review is a null.
Deteriorated

Peak risk-at-stake got worse — the most statistically solid result in the review.

Numbers
10% of live trades exceeded 2× committed risk vs 2% in practice (p = 0.0089). Worst: CRM on 28 Aug at 6.95× — $15 committed, $106 actually at stake across 3 entries and 5 stop raises. GOOGL on 3 Aug at 4.02×. Two of the seven breaches were single-entry, so it is not purely a pyramid effect.
Mechanism
When you add, the stop does not move up enough to hold total exposure at one unit. A max-loss rule does not survive a 7× exposure event.
Verdict
Fix this before anything else. It is mechanical: after each add, the stop must hold total risk at one unit.
Improved

Your add rule genuinely tightened — a counted behaviour, not a P&L outcome.

Numbers
Adds placed while underwater: 0 of 27 live vs 7 of 76 practice. First add now taken at a median +0.60R into the move vs +0.40R. 69% of live adds came at ≥+0.5R vs 45%.
Mechanism
Adding to a loser converts a −1R trade into −2R. You did it seven times in practice and never once live. Because these are counts, three lucky trades cannot manufacture the result.
Caveat
The profitability half is not established: lift p = 0.33, and excluding the top three trades the pyramid lift is −2.9R. The median adding trade loses 0.46R against holding the starter alone.
Not the tape

The drawdown after 21 August was an exit collapse, not a market change.

Numbers
Positions reaching +1R: 76% before vs 69% after (p = 0.29), on a tape that was more often up (SPY direction up 52% → 71%). Selection unchanged. Median duration 6.1 → 2.1 minutes. Of positions reaching +1R you converted 15 of 41 before, 1 of 11 after. Two MRNA trades that went +7.2R and +10.6R both closed red.
Verdict
Four sessions is not a finding, but every independent measure points the same way and none points at the market.
Hypothesis

Your 2.5R target is unreachable by construction on the trades you commit to most.

Numbers
Each add raises the last entry’s risk while the stop trails behind, so on the 27 multi-entry trades Rlast is a median 1.96× initial risk — “2.5R” becomes a ~4.9× move. Target met on 1 of 27 pyramids vs 3 of 43 single entries; 4 of 70 overall. The market offered it on 16; you converted 4, undershot 7, and turned 5 into losses.
Verdict
The target definition is more likely wrong than the exits are. Anchor it to initial risk, or to the built position’s average cost — not to the last add.

Where the money came from

Your starter lot alone loses money — in both books

Single-entry trades, which are their own starter, run −0.33R live and −0.21R in practice. Both confidence intervals exclude zero across 199 combined trades and 73 sessions, and the figure is unchanged between periods.

This is heavily selection-confounded — “single entry” largely means “it never went my way, so I never added” — and it is not actionable as “stop taking single-entry trades”, because you cannot know at entry which kind a trade is, and refusing them also refuses the starters that become pyramids. Its value is framing: your edge is not the entry. It is what you do in the two minutes after.

Withdrawn from the previous review

Each of these rested on hand-typed risk values that the order ladder has since shown to be wrong.

  • Cap the day at 3 losersWithdrawn
    Would have destroyed 94% of lifetime profit when first tested. Never adopted.
  • Adopt a −1.5R daily stopWithdrawn
    On measured risk it earns +0.8R across the entire month — inside the noise of a single trade. The “one green session in 27 after hitting −2R” figure that drove it is 4 in 29 on corrected data. Trades taken after a bad start are a coin flip, not a bleed. Your max-loss rule is the correct rule and the data cannot beat it.
  • Discretionary cutting beats a mechanical stopWithdrawn
    Live and practice point in opposite directions and every p-value exceeds 0.5. There is no stop-level edge in either direction — leave the stop alone and stop optimising it.
  • Use breakout volume ratio as an entry filterWithdrawn
    It is measured on the first bar after 09:35 that breaks the range, so it is a property of the day, not of your trade, and it does not exist at the moment of an anticipatory entry.
  • Three enrichment columns were look-aheadFixed
    #1m, #5m and #1H counted the entry bar’s close, which prints after the entry. On the hourly that meant a 09:31 entry was scored on whether the first hour finished green. The tell: #5m = 0 had a 0% win rate across 67 trades in two independent books. Now lagged to the last completed bar — and lagged, all three are a clean null.

What this review does not establish

  • Nothing here establishes that you have an edge. The month’s 95% CI is [−14.2R, +56.8R], it is indistinguishable from the practice book at session level (p = 0.38), and dropping its three best trades makes it negative.
  • Do not scale risk on the basis of this month. You already scaled mid-month, $14 → $18 → $28; this review provides no evidence that was justified.
  • 478 tests were examined across three tracks. At this sample size roughly 24 would reach p < 0.05 by chance, which is why almost everything above is labelled hypothesis rather than finding.
  • Capture depends entirely on the benchmark, so every figure is quoted with its benchmark. Against the day-long peak it is ~5%; against a stop-aware benchmark ~40% at a 2.5R target; against your own target ~60%; among positions that reached +1R while you actually held them, the median is −10%. The day-long figure is the loosest, and it overstated the leak roughly twofold.
