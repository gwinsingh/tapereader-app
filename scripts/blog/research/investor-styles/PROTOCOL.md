# Investor styles: research protocol

What every profile in the series must find out, and how the result is written. This is the brief for whoever
researches a name (a person or an agent). The series covers 30 investors, fund managers and finance personalities.
Each one gets a profile; each style group gets a primer with the background needed to follow the profiles.

**Outputs per name** (both in `scripts/blog/research/investor-styles/`):

1. `<slug>.facts.md`: the fact sheet. Every number and event with its source. Terse. This is the checked record.
2. `<slug>.draft.md`: the narrative draft in the post format below, written only from the fact sheet.

## The reader

Three kinds of reader, and the profile must be useful to each:

- A **long-term investor** who adds to broad index funds on a schedule and wants to know how to sit through pullbacks.
- A **swing trader** (days to weeks) who buys leading stocks on pullbacks, breakouts from bases and earnings gaps, and
  wants to keep gains when the market turns.
- A **day trader** (minutes to hours) who trades setups such as opening range breakouts on a 5-minute chart with a fixed
  risk unit (1R), a profit target in R, a daily trade limit, and smaller size after a losing streak.

The reader is intelligent and reads charts well, but is not an economist, a credit analyst or a venture investor.
Assume no finance vocabulary: define each term the first time it appears, in a few plain words in brackets.

**The site is public.** Never write anything personal: no "you", no "your morning breakout", no accounts, no trades.
Word every link to trading in general terms: "this fits 5-minute opening range breakout strategies", "a swing trader
who buys pullbacks in leading stocks could test...". Third person and impersonal throughout.

## What to find out (the fact sheet)

Answer every question. Where the answer cannot be found, write `NOT FOUND` and say where you looked.
Where sources disagree, give both and say which is closer to the primary record.

### 1. Snapshot
- Style in one line. Instruments and markets. Typical holding period. Typical leverage. Assets managed at peak and now.
- Status as of November 2026: still managing outside money, family office, retired, deceased, convicted.

### 2. Origin
- Education, first job, who taught them, the first fund. The formative loss or mistake, if there is one.

### 3. The source of the edge
Classify where the returns came from. This is the most important section. One or more of:
- **Analysis** (seeing the same public facts better), **information** (knowing more, legally), **behaviour** (acting
  differently from the crowd: patience, size, cutting losses), **structure** (permanent capital, cheap leverage, fees,
  deal access, tax), **speed and technology**, **luck or a single era** (one regime that suited the style).
- For each claimed edge: the evidence, and whether an individual with a brokerage account has access to it.

### 4. The process, step by step
For each step, what they actually do, with one concrete example:
- **Finding ideas:** top-down or bottom-up, screens, people, data.
- **Forming a thesis:** what must be true, over what horizon, what would prove it wrong.
- **Timing the entry:** do they use price charts, catalysts, valuation levels, or nothing? Quote the evidence.
- **Sizing:** how big is a normal position and the largest ever, as a share of capital. Do they add to winners or to losers?
- **Managing:** stops (price, time or thesis), hedges, trimming.
- **Exiting:** on target, on a broken thesis, on a better idea, never.

### 5. Risk and drawdowns
- The stated risk rules. The worst drawdowns with dates and size. What they did during and after a drawdown
  (cut size, stopped trading, changed the system, raised money). Did they ever come close to ruin?
- Concentration: share of the portfolio in the top one and top five positions when known.

### 6. The record
- Annual or annualised returns, the period, net or gross of fees, against which benchmark. Calendar-year numbers when published.
- Returns by era: early small-capital years against late large-capital years.
- **Record confidence grade:** A = audited or in regulatory filings; B = reported consistently by several reliable outlets
  citing investor letters; C = self-reported or from a single outlet; D = claimed, unverifiable or contradicted.
- How the record held up in each stress period that falls inside their career: 1987, 1994, 1998, 2000–02, 2008, 2020, 2022.

### 7. Signature trades
Three wins and at least two losses. For each: date, instrument, the thesis, what triggered the entry, the size, how it was
managed, the result in money or percent, and the lesson they drew from it in their own words (paraphrased).

### 8. Mistakes and criticism
- The best-argued criticism of the person and the style, from named critics. Regulatory or legal history, with documents.
- What changed in their process after each large mistake.

### 9. Psychology and routine
- Daily routine and information diet where documented. How they handle being wrong and how fast they change their mind.
- Sparring partners, team, mentors. Breaks from the market. Anything they say about emotions, ego and conviction.

### 10. Evolution and regime
- How the style changed as money grew and as markets changed. Which market regimes paid them and which hurt
  (falling or rising rates, inflation, bubbles, crashes, low volatility).
- Is the style still working in the last five years? Evidence.

### 11. What carries over, by time frame
Three short lists. Each item names the practice, the evidence that they did it, and a general way to apply it.
- **Investing (years).** **Swing trading (days to weeks).** **Day trading (minutes to hours).**
Specific questions to answer for every name, because they matter most to an active trader:
1. How do they size up when right and cut when wrong? Do they add to winners, and on strength or on pullbacks?
2. What do they do after a string of losses? Is there a rule for reducing size?
3. How do they keep gains: targets, trailing exits, or selling when the reason is gone?
4. Do they use a market-wide filter (liquidity, central bank, breadth, trend) to decide how aggressive to be?
5. Do they separate books or accounts by time horizon, and has a short-term trade ever turned into a long-term holding?
6. What record-keeping or review habit do they credit (journals, decision logs, post-mortems)?
7. How much of the day is preparation against execution?
- Then **tests worth running:** two or three ideas an individual could check on their own trade history or on price data.
  Word them as hypotheses, never as rules or advice.

### 12. What cannot be copied
- The structural advantages an individual does not have (scale, leverage terms, access to management or deals, permanent
  capital, a research team, low costs), and the parts of the record that depend on them.

### 13. Prerequisites
- The concepts a reader needs before the profile makes sense, each in one sentence. These feed the style primer.

### 14. Sources
- Primary sources ranked by usefulness: their letters, books, filings, long interviews, court and regulator documents.
  Then the best secondary sources. Every entry with a working URL where one exists.

### Extra questions by type
- **Fraud cases:** how the scheme worked step by step, how long, how much, who was fooled, the warning signs that were
  visible at the time, who raised them and when, how it ended, sentences and recoveries. A checklist an individual could
  have used.
- **Media and personality names:** how they are paid (the incentive map), what they claim, what record can be checked,
  and any published study of their picks.
- **Venture names:** fund sizes and vintages, the few deals that made the record, published fund returns (multiple of
  money, internal rate of return) where they exist, and what angel investing by an individual looks like against it.

## Ranking inputs (collected per name, used at the end of the series)

Score nothing yet. Collect the evidence for six questions: (1) how well the record is verified, (2) its length and
consistency, (3) the risk taken and the drawdowns, (4) the scale at which it was earned, (5) how repeatable the process
is, (6) integrity. The original joke tier list is dropped; the ranking is made from these once all 30 are done.

## Research rules

- Today is 23 November 2026. Check what has changed recently (latest filings, roles, lawsuits, deaths, returns).
- Prefer primary sources and fetch them directly: SEC EDGAR (13F, 10-K, ADV), court and regulator documents, the person's
  own letters, books and interview transcripts. Then reliable outlets (Financial Times, Wall Street Journal, Bloomberg,
  Reuters, Institutional Investor, academic papers). Avoid content farms, quote sites and unsourced listicles.
- A number in the fact sheet without a URL is not a fact. Mark anything unconfirmed `(not verified)`.
- Much of what is repeated about famous investors is legend. For each famous quote or return figure, find where it first
  appeared. If the origin is the person's own later retelling, say so.
- Web search is a shared, limited allowance. Stay within the number of searches your brief gives you, do not start
  helper agents, and prefer fetching a known URL to searching.
- Copyright: paraphrase. At most one short quotation (under 15 words) per source, in quotation marks, attributed.

## The draft: format

A Markdown file with this header, then the body. Target length is in your brief.

```
---
title: "Stan Druckenmiller: big bets, small losses"
date: 2026-11-23
tags: [research, investor-styles, global-macro]
series: investor-styles
summary: One sentence that states the main point of the post.
---
```

Body rules:

- Open with the answer: three or four sentences on who this is, what the style is and the one thing worth taking from it.
- Use `##` for sections and `###` inside them. Suggested order: At a glance · How they got started · Where the edge came
  from · The process · Risk and drawdowns · The record · Signature trades · Criticism and mistakes · Routine and mindset ·
  What carries over (investing, swing trading, day trading) · What cannot be copied · What to learn first · Sources.
- Point first, then evidence. Short sentences, plain words, one idea per sentence. No hype, no rhetorical questions,
  no metaphors that need decoding. Dry is fine.
- Every number carries its unit, its date and what it measures.
- Cite inline, right after the claim, as a Markdown link: `([Bloomberg, 14 Aug 2025](https://...))`.
- Tables are welcome (Markdown tables, under 7 columns).
- Callouts use this syntax, which works in Obsidian and on the blog. Types: `take` (what to take from it),
  `warn` (limits, small samples, legend against record), `define` (a term explained), `test` (a hypothesis to check).

```
> [!take] What to take from it
> - First point.
> - Second point.
```

- Figures: do not draw them. Where a picture would help a layman, leave a block like the one below with the exact data.
  Suggest many: timelines, bar charts of yearly returns, a diagram of how a trade worked, a flow of the process,
  a comparison table. Repeating data from the text in a figure is welcome when it helps understanding.

```
<!-- FIGURE
kind: bar | line | timeline | flow | compare | scatter
title: Quantum Fund against the S&P 500, 1989–2000
note: what the reader should see in it
data:
year, fund %, index %
1989, 31.6, 31.7
-->
```

- This is a study of how someone invests, not advice. Never say what to buy or sell. A sentence that sounds like a rule
  must be worded as an observation or as a hypothesis to test.
