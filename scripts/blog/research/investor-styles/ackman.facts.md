## 1. Snapshot

- Style: concentrated long-only stock picking in large, high-quality North American companies ("8 to 12 core holdings" [AR25]), with engagement with managements (activism, now mostly private), plus rare, large "asymmetric" macro hedges bought with options or credit default swaps. Public activist short selling ended; "no current intention to initiate a public equity short position" [AR25 p.23].
- Instruments: listed shares; total return swaps, forwards and options to build stakes; index credit default swaps, interest-rate swaptions and currency options for hedges [AR25 investment policy; AR22].
- Typical holding period: years. Computed from the signature trades in section 7: winners held about 1 to 14 years (median about 5); losers 3 months to 5 years.
- Leverage: no margin borrowing; PSH carries long-dated bonds, target 15–20% of total assets, 19.5% of total capital at 31 Dec 2025, no mark-to-market covenants [AR25].
- Assets: Gotham about $500m at peak [WP-A][MF09]. Pershing: $54m at the start (2004) [WP-A]; "above $20bn" at the 2015 peak [C18b] ($18.3bn in another count, Business Insider via search, not verified); $8.2bn in April 2018 [C18b]; $8.5bn in early 2020, $7.2bn of it PSH [AR19]; core strategy $17.0bn at end 2025, excluding $3.6bn of PSH debt [AR25 p.7]; total $30.7bn at 31 Dec 2025 on the prospectus definition, which counts HHH; fee-paying $20.7bn [PROS]; about $35bn in Aug 2026 after the PSUS listing [F26].
- Staff: 40 in 2011 [CO11]; 56 → 46 in January 2018 (Reuters via search, not verified) → 38 at end 2018 (Business Insider via search, not verified); 44 employees and nine investment professionals at 31 Dec 2025 [PROS]; 48 in Aug 2026 [F26].
- Status, Nov 2026: age 60 (born 11 May 1966 [WP-A]). Founder and chief executive of Pershing Square; Ryan Israel is chief investment officer (since August 2022 [AR25]). Executive chairman of Howard Hughes Holdings since May 2025 [AR25]. Largest owner of the listed parent, Pershing Square Inc. (25% directly plus stakes in two holding entities) [PROS]. Forbes net worth $9.7bn on 24 Nov 2026 [FORBES]; Bloomberg $8bn in April 2026 [WP-A].

## 2. Origin

- Born in Chappaqua, New York. Harvard College BA 1988 (social studies, magna cum laude); Harvard Business School MBA 1992 [WP-A]. Family business: the Ackman-Ziff real estate financing firm (The Real Deal, via search; not verified).
- **Gotham Partners.** Co-founded with David Berkowitz straight out of business school. An SEC proxy filing of 1998 says Ackman acted as co-investment manager of the Gotham funds "since January 1, 1993" (First Union proxy material, search result on sec.gov: https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/0000037008/000089534598000153/0000895345-98-000153.txt). Wikipedia says founded 1992. Seed capital $3m; early investors included Marty Peretz, Michael Steinhardt, Seth Klarman, Leon Levy and the Ziffs [MF09][CO11]. Returns "40% a year" [MF09] (not verified; grade C/D). Peak assets about $500m (around 1998) [WP-A][MF09].
- 1995: bid for Rockefeller Center Properties with Leucadia National; lost, but it made the firm's name [WP-A].
- 1998: proxy fight at First Union Real Estate, a Cleveland property trust; Ackman chairman of its trustees from June 1998 (SEC proxy material above). [CO11] calls it the first successful hostile takeover of a real estate investment trust.
- Farmer Mac: short position with a published report, "Buying the Farm"; profit about $75m [CO11].
- Gotham Golf: a golf-course operator, illiquid; a planned merger with First Union was blocked by a court in late 2002 (not verified: no opened source). Gotham announced the wind-down of its funds at the turn of 2002–2003 after redemption requests it could not meet from illiquid holdings (ThinkAdvisor citing the Wall Street Journal, 13 Jan 2003, via search summary: https://www.thinkadvisor.com/2003/01/13/gotham-begins-shutting-down-hedge-funds/; page returned an error) [CO11][MF09]. He "vowed never to invest in private companies again" [CO11].
- 9 December 2002 (date from the brief, not verified): report "Is MBIA Triple-A?" published while Gotham was winding down [WILEY][WP-A].
- **Investigation.** The SEC and New York Attorney General Eliot Spitzer investigated Gotham in 2003, for about six months; no charges were brought [CO11]. Spitzer later: "we put him through the wringer" [CO11]. Wiley: the investigations "led nowhere" [WILEY]. A document formally closing the inquiry: NOT FOUND.
- Formative mistake: illiquid, private holdings financed with capital that could be withdrawn. The same mismatch returned in 2015–2018 (open-ended funds were two-thirds of assets [PROS]).
- Feud with Carl Icahn began in 2003 over a deal for Hallwood Realty shares; Ackman sued; in 2011 Icahn was ordered to pay $4.5m plus interest, about $9m in all [WP-A].
- Pershing Square: founded 1 January 2004 [AR25] with $54m from his own money and Leucadia National [WP-A] ("three investors" in [MF09]). [AR25] endnote 2 refers to "the sole unaffiliated limited partner" in 2004, which fits Leucadia as the one outside investor.

- His own statement of the principle: businesses that are "simple, predictable, and free cash flow-generative", with strong competitive positions, little debt and good management, bought below intrinsic value [H126 p.6]; "limited downside" and "formidable barriers to entry" [AR25 p.4]; prospectus adds "limited exposure to extrinsic factors" [PROS].

## 4. The process

- **Finding ideas:** bottom-up. A watch list of several hundred qualifying businesses is kept up to date; the firm waits for a price [H126 p.6]. Example: Visa, Mastercard, Netflix, S&P Global, Intercontinental Exchange and Alcon all bought in 2026 after price falls [H126].
- **Thesis:** quality first (the checklist above), then valuation against the firm's estimate of earnings several years out; Hilton bought at under 23 times earnings in 2018, sold at 32 times in Feb 2026 [AR25 p.22]. What proves it wrong: loss of confidence in the ability to predict the business (Netflix, April 2022 [AR22 p.4]).
- **Timing:** no price charts found. Entries follow a dislocation in the share price caused by a specific event: Alphabet after the ChatGPT launch, Amazon "in the days after" the April 2025 tariff announcement, Meta after a 20% fall on spending plans [AR25 p.9, p.12].
- **Building the stake:** shares plus options, forwards and total return swaps (contracts that pay the return on a share without owning it). Examples: McDonald's 4.9% held through options, 2005 (Fortune, 15 Nov 2005, via search: https://money.cnn.com/2005/11/15/news/fortune500/mcdonalds_fortune/); General Growth 19.9% including derivatives [II10]; Target through stock and derivatives [WP-P]; Hertz through swaps, 2025 [AR25 p.17]; Nike converted to deep in-the-money call options, 2025 [II25].
- **Sizing:** 8 to 12 core holdings, so roughly 8% to 12% each on average. Chipotle was over 20% of the portfolio in February 2020 and a third of it was sold "to reduce what had become an outsized position" [AR19]. Starbucks re-bought at 10% of capital in March 2020 [AR19]. Valeant: Ackman later wrote the position was too large for the risk (GuruFocus copy of the 2016 letter, via search; not verified). Exact Valeant weight at peak: NOT FOUND (stake peaked at $5.1bn [F17] against firm assets of roughly $20bn, so about a quarter: computed, rough).
- **Adding:** adds to existing holdings after market-wide falls: in March 2020 stakes rose by 16% (Agilent), 39% (Berkshire Hathaway), 34% (Hilton), 158% (Howard Hughes), 46% (Lowe's), 26% (Restaurant Brands) [AR19]. Has also added to single-name losers: Platform Specialty at $25, which he later called a mistake [R16]; Valeant (joined the board in March 2016 after the collapse) [F17].
- **Managing:** no price stops. Positions are resized "to manage concentration risks", not on short-term views [AR22 p.10]. Core holdings are not sold on a macro view; a hedge is bought instead [AR22 p.9–10].
- **Exiting:** (a) price reaches the estimate of value: Starbucks, January 2020 [AR19]; Hilton, February 2026 [AR25]; (b) thesis no longer predictable: Netflix, sold within three months [AR22]; Nike, sold after 18 months [II25]; (c) forced: Canadian Pacific sold in 2016 to repay withdrawing investors, later "one of the firm's greatest investment regrets" [R22].
- **Activism:** loud until 2017 (proxy fights at Target 2009, Canadian Pacific 2012, ADP 2017; multi-hour public presentations). Since then mostly private. March 2022: "we have permanently retired from this line of work" (activist short selling); new approach branded "Pershing Square 3.0" [C22]. The 2026 UMG proposal and the Fannie/Freddie plan published on X (18 Nov 2025) show public campaigning has not stopped [AR25 p.16].
- **Going public: evidence.** Helped: Canadian Pacific (about 90% of votes for his slate in May 2012 [WP-A]); General Growth. Hurt: Herbalife (a public short drew Icahn and others to the other side; shares rose about 25% on the day of one of his later presentations [F18]); the 2024 fund launch, where he said the "$25 billion number in the media" anchored investors [F24 and search summary]. No study isolating the effect: NOT FOUND.

## 5. Risk and drawdowns

- Stated rules: avoid permanent loss of capital; no margin debt because shares can "trade at any price in the short term" [R16]; hedges only when asymmetric; concentration managed by trimming.
- Calendar losses: 2008 −13.0%; 2011 −1.1%; 2015 −20.5%; 2016 −13.5%; 2017 −4.0%; 2018 −0.7%; 2022 −8.8% [AR25]. 2026 to 17 Nov: −6.0% [NAV].
- **Worst drawdown:** on year-end figures, end 2014 to end 2018: −34.4% while the S&P 500 gained 32% (computed, section 6). The firm dates the underperformance to August 2015–December 2017 [PROS]; Ackman: about 21% a year until July 2015 [AR22]. Month-end peak-to-trough figure: NOT FOUND (deeper than the year-end figure, since the first half of 2015 was positive).
- Assets fell from above $20bn (2015) to $8.2bn (April 2018) through losses and withdrawals; "most of the outside investors" in the private funds left, Blackstone among them [C18b]. PSH's capital could not leave.
- Single-fund disaster: Pershing Square IV (Target only, with options), down about 85–90% at the low in early 2009 (section 7).
- Never close to ruin at firm level: no margin debt; 2008 loss was 13% against the index's 37%.
- Concentration: 8–12 holdings; one position above 20% in Feb 2020 [AR19]; top-five share by date: NOT FOUND in the opened sources.
- Response to the drawdown: section 9.

### PSH as a vehicle

- Guernsey company incorporated 2 Feb 2012; began 31 Dec 2012; closed-ended from 1 Oct 2014; shares traded on Euronext Amsterdam from 13 Oct 2014; London listing 2 May 2017; delisted from Amsterdam 31 Jan 2025 [AR25].

## 9. Psychology and routine

- **The reset, in his words.** "Beginning in late 2017, we returned to our roots as an investment-centric operation"; stopped raising money for the open-ended funds; cut the organisation; moved offices in May 2019; the investment team no longer does marketing [AR22 p.7][AR19]. Result claimed: +207% from the start of 2018 to end 2022 against +57% for the index [AR22]; 22.3% a year 2018–2025, 8 points a year ahead of the index [PROS].
- **Admitting error.** January 2016: listed four mistakes to investors (not selling Valeant at $200, becoming restricted, not trimming Canadian Pacific at C$240, adding to Platform Specialty at $25) [R16]. March 2017: took "100 percent of the responsibility" for Valeant and apologised; four lessons: skill at deploying capital is not a durable business; politics and regulation can destroy value; good managers can still fail; a collapse in the share price can itself damage the business [C17].
- **Slow to change his mind when public.** Herbalife held 62 months; Valeant 25 months including a board seat. Fast when private: Netflix sold in three months.
- **Conviction.** 2011: he wanted "one of the great investment records of all time" [CO11].
- **On volatility.** Shares can trade at any price in the short term, so no margin debt [R16]; the market sometimes "feels broken" because leveraged short-term funds are forced sellers, which he sees as an opening for stable capital [AR25 p.8].
- **Routine.** Office five days a week for ten months; the investment team works from the Hamptons in July and August [F26]. Tennis regularly; no coffee; kept the records in the office gym (2011) [CO11]. Played a professional doubles match in July 2025 at 59 [WP-A]. Daily reading and hours: NOT FOUND.
- **Team and mentors.** Nine investment professionals; Ryan Israel chief investment officer; early backers Marty Peretz and Leucadia's founders; he cites Buffett's Berkshire Hathaway as the model for Howard Hughes ("a modern-day Berkshire Hathaway" [AR25 p.10]).
- **Philanthropy.** Pershing Square Foundation founded 2006; more than $750m committed [WP-A] (about $500m per [FORBES]); signed the Giving Pledge [WP-A].

## 11. What carries over

Seven questions:

1. Sizing up and cutting: sizes large at entry (about a tenth of capital); adds after market-wide falls (March 2020); trims winners that pass about a fifth of the portfolio (Chipotle, Feb 2020); has added to losers and called it a mistake (Platform, Valeant). No price-based stop.
2. After losses: no size rule found. The response was structural: fewer staff, no fund-raising, no shorts, simpler businesses, permanent capital.
3. Keeping gains: sells at his estimate of value (Starbucks 2020, Hilton 2026); hedges instead of selling; regrets not trimming Valeant and Canadian Pacific at their highs.
4. Market filter: none that changes exposure. Occasional hedges when cost is small and payoff large.
5. Separate books: separate vehicles for single ideas (Pershing Square IV for Target, co-investment funds for ADP and UMG, the SPAC). A short meant to resolve quickly (Herbalife) became a five-year position.
6. Record-keeping: twice-yearly public letters with attribution by position and written post-mortems.
7. Preparation against execution: almost all preparation; a handful of new positions a year; six in 2026 was called one of the busiest periods ever [H126].

Tests worth running: (a) do the largest positions in a trade log earn more per unit of risk than the smallest; (b) does adding to a losing position help or hurt against closing it; (c) do entries made in the days after a stock's sharp fall on news outperform entries made at other times, for stocks on a pre-written list.
