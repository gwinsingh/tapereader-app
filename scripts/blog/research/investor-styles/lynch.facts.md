## 1. Snapshot

- Style: bottom-up stock picking across six kinds of company, "growth at a reasonable price"; a very wide portfolio, fully invested at all times, no market timing [AAII][CSM87].
- Instruments: US common stocks, later also foreign stocks (Nestle, Unilever, SmithKline Beecham in the ten largest holdings in Jan 1990 [D90]; Volvo profit $79m [WL]; the usual start date of 1985 for the foreign holdings rests on a GuruFocus excerpt, (not verified)). No short selling, options or leverage in the fund's method as he describes it; he opposes stop orders [OUW].
- Holding period: early years about three months on average at about 300% annual turnover (Lynch's own estimate, quoted in [HUB]); fiscal years to March 1987–1990: turnover 96%, 101%, 87%, 82% [P96][P97], i.e. about a year. Best positions took "two to four years to pay off" [D90].
- Leverage: none. Fully invested "at the bottom and the top" [CSM87].
- Assets: about $18m [WL][Y23] to $22m [T90][IA94] at May 1977 (Fidelity: $0.02bn [FQ]); about $100m when opened to the public in 1981 [CBS10]; $1bn in April 1983 [FL]; $5bn in February 1986 (GuruFocus summary of *Beating the Street*, search excerpt, (not verified)); $9.89bn at 31 Mar 1987; $8.44bn at 31 Mar 1988; $9.63bn at 31 Mar 1989; $13.16bn at 31 Mar 1990 [P96]; about $12bn before the October 1987 crash [CSM87]; "$13.3bn" [T90] to "$14bn" [FL][FQ] when he left. Over 1 million shareholders [T90][D90]; about one American in 100 held the fund [WSJ15][C25].
- Magellan now: $36.7bn at Sept 2026 [WM]; manager Sammy Simnegar since 2019; expenses 0.64%; no sales charge [P26].
- Status, Nov 2026: age 82 (born 19 Jan 1944 [WL]). Vice chairman of Fidelity Management & Research (stated in 2023 [CNBC23][F23][Y23]; in Oct 2025 he said he works with young analysts and fund managers and may not discuss his own holdings under Fidelity rules [C25]). Chairman/president of the Lynch Foundation [WL][C25]. Manages no public fund since 31 May 1990. No first-hand statement from 2026 was found; 2026 items found were retellings ([IN26]; a Motley Fool piece of 2 Oct 2026 https://www.fool.com/investing/2026/10/02/peter-lynch-annual-return-fidelity-magellan/ ).
- Recent statements: 7 Dec 2021, Bloomberg Radio: "The move to passive is a mistake"; his "active guys" had beaten the market for 10, 20, 30 years [BBG21]. 25 Apr 2023, CNBC: regrets missing Apple and Nvidia [CNBC23][F23]. July 2023: says he never told readers to "invest in the stock market"; right "six times out of 10" [Y23]. Oct 2025: "I have zero AI stocks"; could not pronounce Nvidia until about eight months before; no view on whether AI is a bubble; calls himself the lowest-tech person, works on yellow pads [C25].
- Family: married Carolyn Hoff 1968; three daughters; Carolyn Lynch died Oct 2015 [WL][WSJ15].
- Philanthropy: Lynch Foundation about $125m, about $80m granted since inception, $8m in 2013 (New York Times, 8 Nov 2013, as cited in [WL], article not opened); $10m to Boston College (Lynch School of Education) and $20m in 2010 for the Lynch Leadership Academy [WL]; Dec 2021 gift of 30 works of art to Boston College's McMullen Museum (search excerpt of the Bloomberg original, (not verified)). Causes: education, health care, cultural preservation, medical research [C25]; Teach for America, Partners in Health, Posse Foundation [WSJ15]; inner-city Catholic schools in Boston [IA94].

## 2. Origin

- Born Newton, Massachusetts. Father, a Boston College mathematics professor, fell ill when Lynch was seven and died of cancer when he was ten [IA94][WL]; died aged 46 [T90][C25].
- Caddied from age 11 (about 1955) at a club whose members talked stocks; a caddie scholarship ($300) helped pay for Boston College [FL]. BA 1965 (history, psychology, philosophy per [WL]; "finance" per other sources). MBA, Wharton, 1968 [WL].
- First stock: Flying Tiger Airlines, bought as a student (100 shares at $7 [WL]; "$1,000" [FL]); rose about tenfold; sold in stages between $20 and $80; paid for graduate school [FL].
- Fidelity: summer intern 1966, one of three hired from 75 applicants; he had caddied for Fidelity's president, D. George Sullivan [FL][WL]. Two years in the Army; permanent analyst from 1969 (textiles, metals); director of research 1974–77 [WL].
- Took over Magellan on 31 May 1977 at age 33 [FL]. The fund had been run by Edward Johnson III (1963–71) and Richard Habermann (1972–77) [WM][FQ].
- Early find as an analyst: Hanes, after his wife bought L'eggs pantyhose in the supermarket; he bought 48 pairs of the rival brand to test it; Hanes became his biggest position and was then acquired by Consolidated Foods, which he thinks cost him a thirtyfold gain instead of tenfold [FL][WH below].
  - [WH] Wharton Magazine profile: https://magazine.wharton.upenn.edu/issues/anniversary-issue/stock-superstar-who-beat-the-street-peter-s-lynch-wg68/
- Formative loss: no single one found. He names 1973–74 only indirectly; pre-Magellan Fidelity bought about 7m shares of Kaiser Industries at about $12 and watched it fall to about $3 [MPR93] (machine transcript, figures uncertain).

## 4. The process

- Finding ideas: bottom-up only, no screen [AAII]. Sources: company visits and calls, Fidelity analysts, his family's shopping (L'eggs [FL]; Dunkin' Donuts, where he was a customer, then studied the finances [WL citing *One Up* ch. 6]), other companies' managers (La Quinta came from a competitor's praise, (not verified) for the Holiday Inn detail; [OUW] gives the thesis). He keeps about 500 "secondary" names at about 1% of assets in total to monitor [BD][OUW].
- Categorising first [AAII][OUW]:
  - Slow growers: large, ageing, grow about with the economy, pay dividends. Sell after a 30–50% gain or when fundamentals weaken.
  - Stalwarts: large, earnings growth about 10–12% a year (Coca-Cola, Procter & Gamble, Bristol-Myers). Expect no more than about 50% in two years; sell after 30–50% or when the P/E moves well above its normal range; rotate into another stalwart.
  - Fast growers: small, aggressive, 20–25% a year, preferably in a dull industry; source of the tenbaggers; wary of growth above 25%. Sell when growth is ending: new stores disappoint, same-store sales fall, P/E about 30 against growth of 15–20%.
  - Cyclicals: autos, airlines, steel, chemicals. Timing is everything; sell when inventories build, new capacity arrives, prices fall.
  - Turnarounds: Chrysler, Penn Central, General Public Utilities. Least tied to the market. Sell after the turnaround has happened and everyone knows it (Chrysler reclassified as an ordinary cyclical at $48 in mid-1987).
  - Asset plays: hidden assets (land, cable, tax losses). Wait for the buyer or raider.
- Portfolio mix he describes: growth stocks never more than 30–40%; stalwarts 10–20%; cyclicals 10–20%; the rest turnarounds [OUW]. In *Beating the Street*: he doubts he was ever more than 50% in the growth stocks credited for the record [HUB].
- The story ("two-minute drill"): before buying, state aloud in two minutes why the company is interesting, what has to happen and what the pitfalls are; script differs by category [OUW]. On television in 1993 he put it as being able to explain it "to a ten-year-old in 2 minutes or less" (Charlie Rose, 4 Mar 1993, via https://en.wikiquote.org/wiki/Peter_Lynch).
- Numbers checked [AAII][OUW]: year-by-year earnings; P/E against its own history and the industry; P/E against growth (see next); the product's share of total sales (L'eggs mattered to Hanes; Lexan was under 7% of GE); net cash per share (Ford in 1988: $16.30 net cash on a $38 share); debt to equity (normal about 25% debt; bank debt is the dangerous kind); dividends and payout; inventories against sales; insider buying; share buybacks; low institutional ownership and analyst coverage.
- PEG rule: a P/E equal to the growth rate is fair; a P/E of half the growth rate is attractive; twice the growth rate is unattractive. With dividends: (growth + yield) / P/E of 2 or more is what he wants, 1.5 acceptable, below 1 poor [AAII][OUW].
- Timing the entry: no charts, no forecasts. A fall in price with the story intact is a chance to add [AAII]. He says nobody predicts the market, interest rates or the economy; about "13 minutes a year" on economics is too many [FL][MPR93][C25]. Declines of 10% came about every two years and of 25% about every six years since 1900 [FL][MPR93].
- Cocktail-party indicator: four stages, from nobody wanting to talk stocks (bottom) through the market up 15% and 30% to guests telling the fund manager what to buy (top) [QC]. He adds that he does not believe in predicting markets and did not trade on it [QC].
- Sizing: wide and shallow, then deeper as conviction grows. In 1989: 1,400 stocks; half the assets in 100, two-thirds in 200; about 1% spread over 500 monitored names [BD]. A US "diversified" fund may not put more than 5% of assets in one company at purchase; Chrysler was at that 5% by July 1982 (GuruFocus summary of *Beating the Street*, search excerpt, (not verified)). Ford was the largest holding at 3% in 1986 (Fortune 1986, search excerpt, (not verified)).
- Adds to winners: yes; "adds to the best ones" [D90]; Fannie Mae bought in 1977, 1982, 1984, 1987, 1988 [FOOL06]. Also adds on price falls when the story holds [AAII]. The rule against selling winners to keep losers: "cutting the flowers and watering the weeds" [FL]; Buffett phoned to ask permission to quote it (about 1989) [MPR93].
- Managing: re-check the story every few months [AAII]; no stop orders (a 10% stop is hit by normal volatility) [OUW]; no hedges.
- Exiting: when the story has played out, when it breaks, or when a similar company has a better story ("rotation") [AAII].

## 5. Risk and drawdowns

- Stated rules: stay fully invested; spread over categories; never use a market forecast; know why each stock is owned [AAII][CSM87]. No numeric loss limit. NOT FOUND: any rule for cutting size after losses.
- October 1987: on a first holiday in eight years, in Ireland. He gives the Dow as −55 on Thursday 15 Oct, −115 on Friday 16 Oct (the actual fall was 108), −508 on Monday 19 Oct; flew home [FL]. Fund lost about a third in two business days by his account [FL]; over $4bn of about $12bn [CSM87][T90]; "we lost about a third and the market lost about a third"; all but about 2% of the fall in assets was price, not redemptions [CSM87]. *One Up on Wall Street* opens with the episode; a review describes him on the phone deciding what to sell to meet redemptions (search excerpt, (not verified)); the widely quoted "18%, $2bn on the Monday" figure was NOT FOUND in an opened source.
- 1987 calendar year: +1.00% against +5.8% for the index [P96][SP]. Fiscal year to 31 Mar 1988: −9.64% [P96]. The one losing fiscal year in the filings for his tenure.
- Flows after the crash (own calculation from net assets and fiscal-year returns in [P96], treating distributions as reinvested): about −$0.5bn in the year to March 1988, −$0.7bn to March 1989, +$1.6bn to March 1990. Deseret News also reports net redemptions after the crash [D90].
- By his own account Magellan fell more than the market in every market decline during his tenure [C25].
- 1981–82: no fund drawdown figure found. Calendar 1981 +16.5%, 1982 +48.1% [WM].
- 1990: he left on 31 May, before the July–October fall. Calls 1990 far scarier than 1987 (Kuwait, banking crisis, recession) [FL]. Fund −4.51% for calendar 1990 against −3.1% [P96][SP].
- Near ruin: never. No leverage.
- Concentration: low. Top 100 names half the fund [BD]; ten largest in Jan 1990: Fannie Mae, Philip Morris, General Electric, Nestle, MCI, SmithKline Beecham, General Public Utilities, Unilever, Entergy, United Technologies; cash $1.4bn [D90]. Share of the top ten NOT FOUND.
- Active share (share of the portfolio that differs from the S&P 500), read from Petajisto's chart, approximate: 1981 92%, 1982 89%, 1983 80%, 1984 76%, 1985 74%, 1986 67%, 1987 59%, 1988 56%, 1989 56% [PET]. The fund moved toward the index as it grew.

### The claim that the average Magellan investor lost money

- Version 1, "half of Magellan's investors lost money, said Lynch / a Fidelity study": by 1996 quoted "literally hundreds of times". Duff Young checked: a Fidelity spokesman said it came from neither Lynch nor anyone at Fidelity and that Fidelity never made such a study; Ibbotson Associates, also credited, denied producing one. Young, who had repeated it himself, called it an urban myth. Micropal found only that the fund lost money in 17% of 12-month periods in the tenure. Dalbar: direct-fund buyers held about 12 months on average [GM96].
- Version 2, "the average investor lost money, per Fidelity's internal analysis": still printed in April 2026 without a document [IN26].
- Version 3: Spencer Jakab (2016) writes that Lynch "calculated" that the average investor made about 7% a year; Carlson repeats it and infers a lag of about 10 points a year [AWOCS]. No Lynch statement with that figure was found in any interview or book summary opened. NOT FOUND.
- The one estimate with a stated method: CBS MoneyWatch 2010, adjusting for size and timing of flows: the typical investor made 13.4% a year 1981–1990 against 21.8% for the fund and 16.2% for the index; inflows of $2.3bn in 1986 on $4.1bn of opening assets [CBS10]. Positive, below the index.
- Arithmetic check (own): the fund had no losing calendar year 1978–89; money that arrived at the end of 1986 and stayed was up 67% by the end of 1989. An average loss would require most money to have been sold at a loss.
- What Lynch himself says on timing: a third of shares were redeemed in his first three years, when returns were highest [FL]; investors wrote after the 1987 crash urging him to hold on [C25]; more money is lost preparing for corrections than in them [WL][C25]; his 1965–95 example: buying each year at the high earned 10.6% a year, at the low 11.7% [FL].

## 7. Signature trades

Wins:

- **Hanes (early 1970s, as analyst):** see section 2. Lesson: the product must matter to the company's sales [FL][OUW].
- **Taco Bell (1977–78):** bought on his first day running Magellan, 250 stores; PepsiCo bought the company (he says he was "robbed" of the larger gain) [MPR93][C25]. Prices in the transcript are garbled; NOT FOUND reliably.
- **La Quinta Motor Inns (from 1978):** fast grower in a dull industry; rooms about 30% cheaper than Holiday Inn by leaving out restaurants and conference space; growing about 50% a year at a P/E of 10; three analysts covered it. A fifteenfold gain [OUW].
- **Dunkin' Donuts:** noticed as a customer, then studied; ii.co.uk calls it a "15-bagger" (https://ii.co.uk/analysis-commentary/how-invest-best-peter-lynch-ii534756); [WL] cites *One Up*, ch. 6. Dates and size NOT FOUND.
- **Chrysler and Ford (1982–88):** recommended Chrysler in October 1982 near $10 [FL]; in the book a turnaround at $2, $5 and $10 (split-adjusted), reclassified as an ordinary cyclical at $48 in mid-1987 [OUW]; thesis: about $1bn of cash, $336m from selling its tank division and a $1.4bn government-guaranteed loan [OUW]; in the 2025 retelling about $2bn of cash, enough for three years, plus the minivan and Jeep under Iacocca [C25]. The $10 of the 1982 recommendation [FL] and the $2-to-$48 path in the book [OUW] are on different share-split bases; do not combine them. Position at the 5% maximum by July 1982 (not verified). Ford: profit $199m, second largest of the tenure [WL citing *Beating the Street*, p. 135]; 1988 analysis: $38 share with $16.30 of net cash [OUW]; cut in late 1989 [D90].
- **Volvo (from 1985):** profit $79m [WL]; found on a 1985 trip to Europe (GuruFocus excerpt, (not verified)).
- **Fannie Mae (1977; 1982–89):** first bought 30,000 shares in 1977 and sold too early; bought again in 1982, 1984, 1987, 1988; profit about $500m for Magellan in about five years and over $1bn for all Fidelity funds, which he thought might be a record for one firm in one stock [FOOL06][WL]. Largest holding in January 1990 [D90]. Position size as a share of the fund NOT FOUND (usually given as the 5% maximum).
- **Philip Morris:** profit $111m [WL]; his 1993 example of not selling a winner because it has risen [MPR93].
- Ten most profitable stocks per *Beating the Street* p. 135 [WL]: Fannie Mae $500m, Ford $199m, Philip Morris $111m, MCI $92m, Volvo $79m, General Electric $76m, General Public Utilities $69m, Student Loan Marketing $65m, Kemper $63m, Lowe's $54m. Together $1,308m (own sum).

Losses and errors:

- **Bildner's (J. Bildner & Sons, 1986–88):** bought at the $13 flotation in September 1986; the chain expanded too fast into new cities; stock bottomed at 12.5 cents; sold at losses of 50–95%. Lesson: wait until the concept works in a second place [OUW].
- **Long shots:** about 25 companies with a story and no earnings; never broke even on one [MPR93].
- **Selling too early:** Flying Tiger sold on the way up; Fannie Mae sold in 1977 [FL][FOOL06]; Home Depot, Toys "R" Us and Dunkin' Donuts are the names repeated in secondary sources (InvestSmart, 14 Nov 2022, https://investsmart.com.au/investment-news/dont-cut-the-flowers-and-water-the-weeds/151925, gives no source), (not verified) against the books.
- **Missed:** over 100 stocks rose more than tenfold in his tenure and he owned none of them [FL]; Wal-Mart and Marriott [D90]; Apple (saw the iPod margins, did no work), Nvidia, Starbucks, Netflix [CNBC23][WSJ15][C25].
- **Airlines:** lost money on some [MPR93]. Texas Air specifically NOT FOUND in an opened source.
- **1987:** did not see the crash coming and was fully invested [CSM87].
- **Technology:** by design avoided makers of technology in favour of users [AAII]; "zero AI stocks" in 2025 [C25].

## 8. Mistakes and criticism

- **Size and era (William Bernstein, 2002):** the public could buy the fund for nine years; the last four were a struggle for a thin margin; more than a dozen funds beat the index by 6 points a year over ten years, about what chance predicts [AWOCS].
- **The edge is not available now (John Huber, 2015):** early results came from very fast trading of small gains with information from managements; Regulation FD (2000) ended selective disclosure [HUB][FD].
- **Investors did not get the return (CBS 2010; Carlson 2016):** above.
- **"Invest in what you know" misleads:** Lynch's own complaint in 2015: he never said to buy a stock because one likes the shop; the phrase leaves out the research; buying without it is gambling [WSJ15]. Evidence: Norwegian investors hold 11% of their portfolios in their own industry and earn no excess return on it, in some tests a negative one [DH11].
- **Do amateurs beat professionals?** Lynch in 1993: 62% of investment clubs beat the market against a quarter of professionals [MPR93]. Barber and Odean: 60% of 166 clubs lagged the index 1991–97, average 14.1% against 17.9% [BO00b]; 66,465 households averaged 16.4% against 17.9% for the market 1991–96 and the most active 11.4% [BO00].
- **Passive remark (2021):** Michael Hiltzik (Los Angeles Times) called it wrong (search excerpt, article not opened, (not verified)). Magellan itself returned 7.8% a year from 1990 to June 2010, 0.4 points behind the index [CBS10]; 13.75% against 14.82% over the ten years to 2025 [P26].
- **Dated advice:** Euromoney's diarist in 2002 found his London speech a repeat of the 1989 book (https://www.euromoney.com/article/b1320pc1xzkkwg/investors-get-a-lynching).
- **The load:** 3% up front [P96].
- Regulatory or legal history: none found for Lynch.
- What changed after mistakes: Bildner's led to the "wait for the second location" rule [OUW]. NOT FOUND: any change of method after 1987.

## 9. Psychology and routine

- Hours: left home at 6 am six days a week [IA94]; at the desk 6:45 am, 13-hour days, six-hour Saturdays, later Sunday mornings [T90][D90]. One week a month, or 13–14 days, travelling; about 40 companies a month in person [D90][IA94]. Secondary figure of 200 company visits and 700 annual reports a year (search excerpt, (not verified)). Year-by-year contact counts from *Beating the Street*: NOT FOUND.
- Team: about 15 assistants [T90]; Fidelity's analysts; Stansky as assistant 1984–87 [WM].
- Being wrong: right about six times in ten is good [FL][Y23]; a few big winners pay for many mistakes [FL].
- Emotions: the stomach, not the brain, decides results (theme of [FL][C25]); fear is the main threat [C25].
- Record-keeping: a transaction diary is reproduced in *Beating the Street* ch. 18 [FOOL06]; notebooks and yellow pads [C25]. A formal review routine: re-check each story every few months [AAII].
- Why he stopped: announced at the end of March 1990 (a Wednesday per [T90]; the Los Angeles Times report is dated 29 March, so 28 March, derived), left 31 May; his father had died at 46; he wanted time with his wife and three daughters; said the fund's size was not the reason and that the claims it had peaked hurt [T90][C25][FL]. Pay reported up to $10m a year [T90]. Built $1.8bn of cash for redemptions before leaving [T90].
- After: part-time at Fidelity, mentoring analysts; from June 1993 kept Mondays free [IA94]; books in 1989, 1993, 1995 with John Rothchild [WL].

## 10. Evolution and regime

- 1977–81: about 40 stocks at the start ((not verified)), small and mid-size companies, turnover about 300% [HUB]. Fund closed; redemptions.
- 1982–85: autos (Chrysler, Ford, later Volvo) as turnarounds and cyclicals; assets $0.1bn to $4bn.
- 1986–90: financials (Fannie Mae, Sallie Mae, Kemper, savings and loans), foreign stocks, large stalwarts and utilities when no growth stocks appealed [D90]; 1,400 holdings; turnover 82–101% [P96]; active share down to 56% [PET].
- Paid by: recovery from the 1973–74 lows in small stocks; the 1982–87 bull market. Hurt by: 1984, 1987.
- Last five years: Magellan under Simnegar 11.39% a year against 14.42% for five years to 2025 [P26]. Lynch's own results since 1990 are not public.

## 11. What carries over

Investing:
- Fully invested, no forecast: [CSM87][FL]. Timing example 1965–95 [FL].
- Declines are normal: 10% every two years, 25% every six [FL][MPR93].
- Investor return against fund return: [CBS10].

Swing trading:
- Categorise before buying; sell rule by category [AAII][OUW].
- Two-minute story [OUW].
- Add to what is working; flowers and weeds [D90][FL].
- Many small starter positions, concentration built later [BD].
- No market filter; a mood gauge only [QC].

Day trading:
- None in the record. No stops [OUW].
- Principles only: exit condition defined before entry; hit rate about 60% with few large winners [FL].

Seven questions:
1. Sizing: starts small, adds as the story is confirmed, also adds on falls with the story intact; cuts when the story breaks; cap 5% per stock [D90][AAII].
2. After losses: no rule found.
3. Keeping gains: no targets except for stalwarts and slow growers (30–50%); otherwise holds until the story changes [OUW].
4. Market filter: none; cocktail-party gauge not acted on [QC].
5. Separate books: one fund; categories act as separate rule sets. A trade becoming a long holding: Fannie Mae, bought and sold in 1977, held as the largest position by 1990 [FOOL06][D90]. Chrysler moved from turnaround to cyclical [OUW].
6. Records: transaction diary, notebooks, story re-check every few months [FOOL06][AAII].
7. Preparation against execution: almost all preparation; 40 company visits a month [IA94].

Tests (hypotheses, worded in the post): (a) trades labelled by type without sight of the result differ in how far they run, so one exit rule for all types gives back gains on some; (b) trades with a written one-sentence reason are exited less often on noise; (c) for scheduled index-fund purchases, money-weighted return against the fund's published return measures the cost of the buyer's own timing; (d) not used in the post: additions after a position is in profit against additions after a fall, result per unit of risk.

Figures in the post that are computed here rather than quoted: growth of $1,000 (1977 = 1,000; 1989 = 24,466 fund, 6,249 index); era returns; shares of the twelve-year result (fund's own growth 43/34/23, growth beyond the index 67/28/5, logarithmic); 1989 portfolio split (50 / 17 / 32 / 1 from "half in 100, two-thirds in 200, 1% in 500"); implied net flows.
