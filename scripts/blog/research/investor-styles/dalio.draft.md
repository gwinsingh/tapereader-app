Ray Dalio founded Bridgewater Associates in 1975 and turned it into the largest hedge fund firm in the world. His style is systematic global macro (trading whole markets such as stock indexes, bonds, currencies and commodities, by written rules that a computer runs). The public image is of a man who predicts crises. The record says something else: his big public forecasts were often wrong, and the money was made by a process that did not depend on them. The one thing worth taking from him is that process: write down the reason for every decision, turn it into a rule, test the rule on history, and never let one bet be large enough to end the game.

## How he got started

Dalio grew up on Long Island, studied finance at C.W. Post College and took an MBA at Harvard in 1973 ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ray_Dalio)). He says he was hooked on markets at 12 ([Institutional Investor, 18 Sep 2017](https://www.institutionalinvestor.com/article/2bsvvqs6ph9xpipo69jb4/portfolio/the-education-of-ray-dalio)).

His first lesson came on the floor of the New York Stock Exchange in August 1971. President Nixon ended the dollar's link to gold. Dalio expected stocks to fall. The Dow rose almost 4% the next day. He concluded that one person's experience is too short, and that similar events in other countries and other centuries had to be studied ([Bridgewater, "The All Weather Story", 2012](https://www.bridgewater.com/resources/all-weather-story.pdf)).

He founded Bridgewater in 1975 in his apartment. For its first decade it was not a fund. It advised companies on their risks and sold a daily research letter. One early job was for McDonald's, which wanted a fixed chicken price before launching the McNugget. There was no chicken futures market. Dalio showed a producer how to hedge the cost of a chicken with corn and soymeal futures, so the producer could quote a fixed price (same source).

### The 1982 mistake

By 1981 Dalio had worked out that American banks had lent emerging countries more than could be repaid. He predicted a debt crisis, and then a depression as bad as the 1930s. Mexico defaulted in August 1982. He was invited to testify to Congress and appeared on television. Then the Federal Reserve cut rates, the stock market began one of its great rises, and the economy recovered. His verdict: "I was dead wrong" ([Institutional Investor, 18 Sep 2017](https://www.institutionalinvestor.com/article/2bsvvqs6ph9xpipo69jb4/portfolio/the-education-of-ray-dalio)).

He had bet his own and his clients' money on the forecast. He lost so much that he let every employee go, was the firm's only employee, and borrowed $4,000 from his father ([Fortune, 13 Aug 2025](https://fortune.com/2025/08/13/billionaire-ray-dalio-broke-early-career-borrowed-4000-from-dad/)).

> [!warn] Legend against record
> The Congress testimony and the television appearance are public events. The $4,000 loan and the size of the loss come only from Dalio's own later retelling, in his 2017 book and in interviews. No account statement from 1982 has been published.

He was right about the event and wrong about the market. He draws four lessons from it. First, stop asking whether the view is right and ask "how do I know I'm right?" ([Tim Ferriss Show, 1 Feb 2018](https://tim.blog/2018/02/01/the-tim-ferriss-show-transcripts-ray-dalio/)). Second, look for the smartest people who disagree. Third, diversify so that the same expected return comes with less risk. Fourth, study history beyond one's own lifetime (Institutional Investor, same article).

### From adviser to fund manager

In 1987 the World Bank's pension fund gave Bridgewater a $5m bond account. That was the first money it managed. In 1990 the firm wrote a memo for a large corporate pension plan on how to balance stocks with long-term bonds. Pure Alpha, the fund of active bets, started in 1991. All Weather, a passive mix, started in 1996 as a home for Dalio's own family trust ([Bridgewater, 2012](https://www.bridgewater.com/resources/all-weather-story.pdf)).

## Where the edge came from

Four sources can be separated.

**Analysis.** Bridgewater studied how economies behave across many countries and long periods and wrote the links down as rules. Dalio tests a rule by asking whether it held in different eras and places ([Tim Ferriss Show, 2018](https://tim.blog/2018/02/01/the-tim-ferriss-show-transcripts-ray-dalio/)). An individual can copy the habit. The data and the staff cannot be copied.

**Structure.** The firm spread money over many unrelated bets and used borrowed money to bring each to a useful size. Leverage [borrowing, or using futures, to hold more than the cash in the account] was about 4 to 1 in 2009 ([Fortune, 2009](https://fortune.com/2009/03/19/bridgewater-ray-dalio)). An individual gets this only in part and at a higher cost.

**Access.** The firm advised the US Treasury on the design of inflation-protected bonds in 1997 ([Bridgewater, 2012](https://www.bridgewater.com/resources/all-weather-story.pdf)). The journalist Rob Copeland argues that contacts with officials mattered to the trading. Bridgewater denies his account. An individual has no such access.

**Era.** The best years fell between 1991 and 2011, a period of falling interest rates and repeated crises. From 2012 to 2024 the main fund earned less than 3% a year ([Bloomberg via Fortune, 2 Jan 2026](https://fortune.com/2026/01/02/top-hedge-fund-performers-2025-bridgewater-de-shaw-citadel-millennium/)).

### Alpha and beta

Bridgewater's first idea is a formula: return = cash + beta + alpha ([Bridgewater, 2012](https://www.bridgewater.com/resources/all-weather-story.pdf)).

> [!define] Alpha and beta
> **Beta** is the return from simply holding a market, such as a stock index, above the return on cash. **Alpha** is the extra return from a manager's decisions. Bridgewater's view: betas are few, cheap, and pay over time; alpha is a contest in which one side's gain is another's loss.

The firm sold the two separately. Pure Alpha holds only the active bets. All Weather holds only the market exposures.

### The Holy Grail chart

Around 1990 Dalio asked a young mathematician to draw one chart. It shows how the risk of a portfolio falls as more investments are added, for different levels of correlation [how closely two things move together: 0 means unrelated, 1 means in step]. Each investment is assumed to have the same risk, 10% a year.

Dalio says the chart showed him that 15 to 20 good, unrelated return streams would cut risk sharply without cutting the expected return. It also showed that a thousand stocks that are 60% correlated diversify little more than five ([*Principles*, 2017, excerpt](https://operators.macro-ops.com/wp-content/uploads/2020/01/Ray-Dalio-on-The-Holy-Grail-Portfolio.pdf)).

> [!warn] The spoken numbers round up
> In interviews Dalio says 15 unrelated bets cut risk by over 80% and raise the return-to-risk ratio fivefold. The formula gives a 74% cut and a ratio 3.9 times higher at 15 bets. Eighty percent and five times need about 25. The larger caveat is practical: finding even five truly unrelated sources of return is hard, and he says so himself.

### The economic machine

Dalio's model of an economy has three parts: productivity growth, a short-term debt cycle of about 5 to 8 years, and a long-term debt cycle of about 75 to 100 years. When debts grow too large, they are reduced in four ways: spending cuts, defaults, transfers from rich to poor, and money printing by the central bank. He ends with three rules of thumb: debt should not rise faster than income, income should not rise faster than productivity, and raising productivity matters most ([transcript of "How the Economic Machine Works"](https://singjupost.com/economy-really-work-ray-dalio-transcript/)).

### All Weather and risk parity

The second big idea answers a different question: what should be held if the future cannot be predicted?

Bridgewater's answer starts from an observation. A typical pension portfolio has about 60% of its money in stocks, and so almost all of its risk in stocks, because stocks swing far more than bonds ([Bridgewater, 2012](https://www.bridgewater.com/resources/all-weather-story.pdf)).

The fix is to size each asset by its risk, not by dollars. That means holding much more of the calm assets, and using leverage to do it. Bridgewater's own example: $10 in stocks and $10 in bonds is dominated by stocks; $5 in stocks and $15 in bonds is balanced but earns less; add some leverage to the $5/$15 mix and it earns what stocks earn with less risk (same source). A consultant later named this approach risk parity.

The risk is then spread over four economic conditions. Growth can come in above or below what markets expect. So can inflation. Each of the four gets a quarter of the risk.

Dalio described building it as "like inventing a plane that's never flown before" ([Bridgewater, 2012](https://www.bridgewater.com/resources/all-weather-story.pdf)).

### The popular version is not the fund

Most readers know All Weather from Tony Robbins's 2014 book, which gave a simple recipe from a conversation with Dalio: 30% stocks, 40% long-term Treasury bonds, 15% intermediate Treasury bonds, 7.5% gold and 7.5% commodities, with no leverage ([A Wealth of Common Sense](https://awealthofcommonsense.com/back-testing-tony-robbins-weather-portfolio/)).

The real strategy uses leverage, inflation-linked bonds and global markets, and sets weights by risk. The closest public evidence is the exchange-traded fund that Bridgewater began advising in March 2025.

## The process

**Finding ideas.** Top-down. In 2017 the week began with a meeting of about 75 people called "What's Going on in the World", and a 600-page research pack went out on Fridays ([Institutional Investor, 2017](https://www.institutionalinvestor.com/article/2bsvvqs6ph9xpipo69jb4/portfolio/the-education-of-ray-dalio)).

**Forming a thesis.** The firm asks which kind of historical episode the present resembles, then applies the rules that held in those episodes. Before 2008 it had studied past debt collapses. It saw a bubble by late 2006 and warned in 2007 about reckless lending ([Institutional Investor, 20 Sep 2013](https://www.institutionalinvestor.com/article/2bsu6zgxx8hrj5koy3uo0/portfolio/culture-and-anticipation-helped-ray-dalio-survive-the-financial-crisis)).

**Timing the entry.** By rule. No source shows Dalio reading price charts for entries. Around 2001 the firm built a "depression gauge" into its system. The gauge switched on at a date, 30 September 2008, and the fund changed mode ([Fortune, 2009](https://fortune.com/2009/03/19/bridgewater-ray-dalio)).

**Sizing.** By risk, not by conviction. Each bet is scaled so that the whole portfolio swings by a target amount: 12% a year in one version of Pure Alpha, 18% in the other ([The Hedge Fund Journal, 1 Oct 2024](https://thehedgefundjournal.com/50-giants-bridgewaters-ray-dalio)). The largest position as a share of capital is not published.

**Managing.** The fund is meant to have no standing lean towards rising or falling markets ([Institutional Investor, 2013](https://www.institutionalinvestor.com/article/2bsu6zgxx8hrj5koy3uo0/portfolio/culture-and-anticipation-helped-ray-dalio-survive-the-financial-crisis)). No use of price stops was found in any source.

**Exiting.** Positions change when the rules change. No profit targets were found.

**Systemising.** This is the core. Dalio writes down the criteria for each decision, has them coded, and runs the computer's decision next to his own ([Tim Ferriss Show, 2018](https://tim.blog/2018/02/01/the-tim-ferriss-show-transcripts-ray-dalio/)). By 1991 he says he had about a thousand tested rules. The best were back-tested together, and on paper they raised return per unit of risk three to five times ([*Principles* excerpt](https://operators.macro-ops.com/wp-content/uploads/2020/01/Ray-Dalio-on-The-Holy-Grail-Portfolio.pdf)).

> [!warn] A disputed picture
> Rob Copeland's 2023 book *The Fund* says the process was less systematic than this. In his account about ten people could see or propose trades, and Dalio's own views decided ([Rational Reminder, episode 292](https://rationalreminder.ca/podcast/292)). Bridgewater called the book a "false and misleading depiction of our past" ([Fortune, 29 Nov 2023](https://fortune.com/2023/11/29/ray-dalio-bridgewater-billionaire-rob-copeland-book-fiction-tragedy/)). Outsiders cannot settle it. The fund's rules have never been published.

## Risk and drawdowns

Dalio's stated limits are a target return-to-risk ratio of about one and a personal drawdown limit [drawdown: the fall from a peak in value to the next low] of one third. His arithmetic: a 50% loss needs a 100% gain to recover ([The Hedge Fund Journal, 2024](https://thehedgefundjournal.com/50-giants-bridgewaters-ray-dalio)).

What the firm did after losses is the useful part. In March 2020 it cut risk. It was slow to add risk back and missed the rebound; risk returned to normal levels around August. Clients withdrew a net $3.5bn in seven months, and dozens of staff were let go ([Bloomberg via Fortune, 15 Sep 2020](https://fortune.com/2020/09/15/the-losses-continue-to-pile-up-for-hedge-fund-king-ray-dalio/)).

After the weak years of 2022 and 2023 the new chief executive changed the structure. Pure Alpha was capped at 20–30% below its peak of about $100bn, and about 100 jobs were cut ([Hedgeweek, 2 Mar 2023](https://www.hedgeweek.com/bridgewater-cap-funds-and-cut-jobs/)). Capital was handed back to clients. Firm assets fell 18.1% in 2024 to $92.1bn ([Hedgeweek, 1 Apr 2025](https://www.hedgeweek.com/bridgewater-aum-down-by-18-as-firm-shrinks-flagship-fund/)).

## Signature trades

### Wins

**2008.** The thesis was that a debt bubble would end in a deleveraging [an economy-wide reduction of debt], which behaves differently from an ordinary recession. Before the crisis the fund held government bonds, yen and gold and was short riskier assets. Pure Alpha II made 9.4% in 2008 while the S&P 500 lost 37%. Dalio's stated lesson: the only reason the fund should lose money is being wrong, never being carried down with the market ([Institutional Investor, 2013](https://www.institutionalinvestor.com/article/2bsu6zgxx8hrj5koy3uo0/portfolio/culture-and-anticipation-helped-ray-dalio-survive-the-financial-crisis)).

**2010.** The fund was positioned for a weak economy and for central banks creating money. Pure Alpha II returned 44.8% ([Hedgeweek, 2011](https://www.hedgeweek.com/steady-2010-performance-drives-surge-investor-confidence/)). It added about 25% in 2011.

**January to September 2022.** Stocks and bonds both fell as central banks raised rates. Pure Alpha II was up 34.6% by 30 September ([Fortune, 4 Oct 2022](https://fortune.com/2022/10/04/ray-dalio-gives-up-control-bridgewater-capital/)). One visible position was a short on European companies. It nearly doubled in a week in June, to $10.5bn across 28 large companies, and was mostly closed by mid-August ([Bloomberg, 17 Aug 2022](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2022-08-17/bridgewater-cuts-back-european-short-wagers-to-below-1-billion)).

### Losses

**1982.** Described above. A correct reading of the event, a wrong reading of the market, and a bet large enough to end the firm.

**March 2020.** The fund had a lean towards rising markets when the pandemic hit. It lost about 20% in the quarter and 12.6% in the year. Two months earlier, at Davos, Dalio had said "Cash is trash" ([CNBC, 21 Jan 2020](https://www.cnbc.com/2020/01/21/ray-dalio-at-davos-cash-is-trash-as-everybody-wants-in-on-the-2020-market.html)). A fund built on unrelated bets had, in practice, one large bet on calm markets.

**October and November 2022.** The fund gave back most of the year's gain in two months.

**The public calls.** In January 2018 Dalio said people holding cash would feel foolish. Cash was the best-performing major asset of 2018 ([Yahoo Finance, January 2019](https://au.finance.yahoo.com/news/one-ago-founder-worlds-biggest-091500765.html)). He repeated the view in January 2020 and dropped it on 3 October 2022: "I no longer think cash is trash" ([Fortune, 4 Oct 2022](https://fortune.com/2022/10/04/bridgewater-associates-ray-dalio-u-turns-on-long-held-view-that-cash-is-trash/)). Pure Alpha made 14.6% in 2018 all the same. The fund's positions and the founder's remarks are different things.

## Criticism and mistakes

**The culture.** In 1993 three colleagues wrote Dalio a memo saying he made staff feel belittled. His answer was to write down management rules and build what he calls radical transparency. Meetings are recorded. Staff rate one another in real time on an app, the Dot Collector. Each person has a "baseball card" of ratings. Votes are weighted by each person's record in the subject, which he calls believability. Copeland describes the result as surveillance, with the weights arranged so that Dalio's view counted most ([Rational Reminder, episode 292](https://rationalreminder.ca/podcast/292)).

**Opacity.** In October 2017 the newsletter writer James Grant questioned how little outsiders could see of Bridgewater. Bridgewater called the piece "inaccurate and misinformed". A week later Grant apologised and withdrew two of his factual points, about the auditor and the custodian bank. He kept his opinion that Dalio was distracted ([Institutional Investor, 19 Oct 2017](https://www.institutionalinvestor.com/article/2bsvvqs6ph9xpipdj9csg/opinion/jim-grants-botched-bridgewater-takedown)).

**Succession.** The handover began in 2010 and was meant to take two years. It took twelve. Seven people held the chief executive title on the way. When control finally moved, the co-chief executive said: "Ray no longer has the final word" ([Fortune, 4 Oct 2022](https://fortune.com/2022/10/04/ray-dalio-gives-up-control-bridgewater-capital/)). The New York Times reported that Dalio agreed only after months of talks over payments that could reach billions; the terms were never published ([Business Insider via Yahoo, February 2023](https://ca.finance.yahoo.com/news/ray-dalio-secretly-billions-dollars-164606105.html)).

**Did risk parity fail in 2022?** All Weather lost 22% when stocks and bonds fell together. Assets in risk parity funds across the industry fell from about $160bn in 2021 to about $90bn in 2023, and such funds trailed a global 60/40 mix [60% stocks, 40% bonds] every year from 2019 to 2023. Defenders point out that the fund's swings matched its design and that the 2010s were unusually kind to stocks ([Bloomberg via Fortune, 2024](https://fortune.com/2024/04/23/lackluster-returns-ray-dalio-investing-strategy-investors-pull-billions/)). A fair reading: the design worked as built, and the name promised more than the design.

**The warnings.** Dalio has warned of a 1930s-style crisis many times: in 1982, through the 2010s with a comparison to 1937, and again from 2025 ([Ben Carlson, 4 Mar 2025](https://awealthofcommonsense.com/2025/03/predicting-a-financial-crisis/)). His books carry the theme: *Big Debt Crises* (2018), *The Changing World Order* (2021), *How Countries Go Broke* (2025). Carlson's point is the important one: the forecasts did little damage to the funds, because the funds ran on rules.

**China.** In December 2021 Dalio compared China's government to a strict parent when asked about disappearances, then said he had answered sloppily. His own chief executive told staff he disagreed ([Fortune, 5 Dec 2021](https://fortune.com/2021/12/05/ray-dalio-bridgewater-china-comments-human-rights-misunderstood/)).

No regulatory action against Dalio or the firm was found.

## Routine and mindset

Dalio has practised Transcendental Meditation since 1969, about 20 minutes twice a day ([Tim Ferriss Show, 2018](https://tim.blog/2018/02/01/the-tim-ferriss-show-transcripts-ray-dalio/)).

He treats a mistake as a puzzle. Solving it yields a rule that can be reused. His formula is pain plus reflection equals progress. Bridgewater keeps an issue log in which mistakes must be recorded, and he built an app for noting a painful moment so that it can be reviewed later with a clear head (same source).

His working partners for decades were Bob Prince and Greg Jensen, who still run investments at the firm.

## What Dalio and Bridgewater are doing now

Dalio manages his own money at the Dalio Family Office. Its filing for the end of 2025 showed $503m of US-listed holdings, more than three-quarters of it in a gold fund ([Bloomberg Línea, 20 Feb 2026](https://www.bloomberglinea.com/mercados/family-office-de-ray-dalio-revela-una-apuesta-de-us500-millones-por-acciones-de-eeuu/)). In October 2025 he released "Digital Ray", an AI trained on his writings ([Fortune, 22 Oct 2025](https://fortune.com/2025/10/22/ray-dalio-ai-clone-training-advice-investment-mentorship-politics-economy/)). In August 2026 he compared enthusiasm for AI stocks with 1929 and 2000 ([Fortune, 4 Aug 2026](https://fortune.com/2026/08/04/ray-dalio-ai-bubble-1929-2000-ipos-wealth-is-not-money/)).

Bridgewater is led by Nir Bar Dea, with Bob Prince, Greg Jensen and Karen Karniol-Tambour as investment chiefs ([Bridgewater](https://www.bridgewater.com/people)). It manages about $102bn. Pure Alpha was up 18.4% in the first nine months of 2026.

## What carries over

### Investing (years)

- **Balance by risk, not by dollars.** A 60/40 portfolio is close to a single bet on stocks. This is an observation about arithmetic, not a case for copying a leveraged fund.
- **Keep the passive holding apart from the active bets.** Dalio put his family trust in the passive fund, not the active one.
- **A balanced portfolio still has bad years.** All Weather lost about 20% twice. It also lagged stocks through a ten-year stock boom. An investor who finds pullbacks hard would have found 2022 hard in this fund too.
- **A famous forecaster is not a timing tool.** The 1982, 2018 and 2020 calls were public and wrong, from the man with the best-known macro record.

### Swing trading (days to weeks)

- **Count bets, not positions.** The Holy Grail chart is the clearest picture of this. Stocks that move together at 0.6 stop diversifying after about five names. Five leading stocks from one sector, bought in the same week, behave like one or two bets.
- **A market-wide filter sets the level of aggression.** Bridgewater's whole method is such a filter. Its crisis gauge changed how the fund traded from a given date.
- **Giving back gains is unsolved here too.** Pure Alpha gave back about three-quarters of its 2022 gain in two months. A swing trader whose recurring problem is the give-back will not find the exit rule in Dalio. The filter is the nearer lesson.

### Day trading (minutes to hours)

- **Write the reason before the trade.** This is the habit Dalio credits above all others. A fixed-risk, rule-based intraday method already has the rule. What it often lacks is the written record of which trades followed it.
- **Log mistakes and review them later.** The issue log separates the moment of pain from the moment of analysis.
- **Run the rule beside the judgement.** Dalio compared what the system would have done with what he did. A set-and-forget version of a strategy, tracked next to the managed version, is the same test.

### Seven questions

1. **Sizing up when right, cutting when wrong.** Size comes from risk and correlation, not from confidence. 1982 was the confidence bet. One case of adding with the move is on record (the 2022 short on European stocks). No rule for adding on pullbacks was found.
2. **After a string of losses.** In 2020 risk was cut after the loss and restored about five months later. The cut limited further damage and also meant missing the recovery. No published step-down formula was found.
3. **Keeping gains.** No targets and no trailing exits were found. The record shows large give-backs.
4. **Market-wide filter.** Yes. Growth, inflation and central-bank policy against what is priced in.
5. **Separate books by horizon.** Yes. Active and passive money sit in different funds. One early client's portfolio ended up about 70% passive and 30% active. No case of a short-term trade turning into a long-term holding was found.
6. **Record-keeping.** Written criteria for each decision since the 1980s, coded rules, an issue log, a daily research letter since 1975.
7. **Preparation against execution.** No hours are published. The structure implies almost all preparation: research by hundreds of people, execution by system.

> [!take] What to take from it
> - The process outlived the forecasts. Rules that are written and tested do not depend on being right about the next crisis.
> - Risk falls with the number of *unrelated* bets, not the number of positions.
> - No one bet should be able to end the game. Dalio learned it by losing everything once.
> - Size was the enemy of returns. The fund did better after it shrank.

### Tests worth running

> [!test] Hypotheses to check on a trade history
> - **Hidden concentration.** On days or weeks with three or more open positions in one sector, the account swings about as much as one position three times the size. Check with the correlation of position returns.
> - **Written reason.** Trades with a reason and exit criteria written before entry have a higher average result in R than trades without. Tag both groups and compare.
> - **Regime and give-back.** A simple market-state label at entry (for example index above or below a moving average, crossed with volatility rising or falling) separates periods when gains were kept from periods when they were given back.
> - **Size cuts after losses.** Halving size after a set drawdown reduces the depth of the drawdown and also slows the recovery, as it did for Bridgewater in 2020. Replay the history both ways and measure both effects.

## What cannot be copied

- **Breadth.** Currencies, interest rates, commodities and stock markets in many countries are close to unrelated. A stock-only account sits on the 0.4 to 0.6 lines of the chart, where extra positions add little.
- **Leverage and instruments.** Four-to-one leverage through futures and swaps, at institutional cost.
- **Staff and data.** About 1,300 employees in 2023 and decades of coded rules.
- **Patient clients.** Institutions with an average tenure of 12 years stayed through thirteen weak years. An individual's own patience is rarely that long.
- **Access.** Central banks and sovereign funds as clients and contacts.
- **The best years.** 2008 and 2010 came from leveraged bond and currency positions that a small account cannot hold in balance.

## What to learn first

- **Global macro:** trading whole markets on views about economies.
- **Correlation:** how closely two things move together.
- **Volatility:** the usual size of an asset's swings in a year.
- **Return-to-risk ratio:** return above cash divided by volatility.
- **Alpha and beta:** skill return and market return.
- **Leverage:** holding more than the cash in the account.
- **Risk parity:** equal risk from each asset, which means more money in the calm ones.
- **Inflation-linked bonds:** bonds whose value rises with prices.
- **Deleveraging:** an economy reducing its debts.
- **Drawdown:** the fall from a peak to the next low.
