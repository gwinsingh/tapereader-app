Value investing is the style of buying shares as part-ownership of a business, at a price well below what the business is worth, and holding them for years. [Warren Buffett](/blog/warren-buffett) and [Peter Lynch](/blog/peter-lynch), both profiled in this series, built their records this way. The one thing worth taking from the style: over long periods a share returns what the business earns, and the price paid decides how much of that the buyer keeps. Everything needed to check both is free and public.

This primer is the background for those two profiles. It is a study of a style, not advice.

## What value investing is

A share is a piece of a business. The price of that piece and its value are different things. The value investor buys only when the price is well below the value.

A trader working from price movement asks where the price is likely to go next. A macro trader asks whether a whole market is priced for the wrong economy (see the [global macro primer](/blog/global-macro-primer)). A value investor asks what one business will earn over the next ten years and what that is worth today.

## What a share is and where the return comes from

A share (also called a stock) is a fraction of a company. Its owner has a claim on a fraction of the profits. The return from holding it has three parts:

- **Dividends:** cash the company pays out to its owners.
- **Growth in earnings:** earnings is another word for profit. If profit per share doubles and nothing else changes, the share price doubles.
- **Change in the multiple:** the multiple is the price divided by yearly earnings, written P/E. If buyers pay 20 times earnings today and 25 times later, the price rises 25% with no change in the business.

The first two come from the business. The third comes from the mood of buyers. John Bogle, the founder of the index fund firm Vanguard, called the first two the investment return and the third the speculative return. By his figures, US stocks returned 9.1% a year from 1900 to 2009: 4.3 points from dividends, 4.5 from earnings growth and 0.3 from the multiple ([Bogle's table, as reported by Ben Carlson](https://awealthofcommonsense.com/2025/06/expected-returns-in-the-stock-market/)).

The same split can be worked out for the S&P 500, an index of 500 large US companies, from year-end data kept by Aswath Damodaran of New York University ([index, earnings and dividends](https://pages.stern.nyu.edu/~adamodar/New_Home_Page/datafile/histimpl.html); [yearly returns](https://pages.stern.nyu.edu/~adamodar/New_Home_Page/datafile/histretSP.html)). The figures below were calculated for this primer from those tables. From 1961 to 2025 the index returned 10.65% a year. Dividends gave 2.82 points, earnings growth 7.12 and the multiple 0.46, as the P/E rose from 18.7 to 25.2.

Over one decade the multiple can dominate. In the 1970s earnings grew 9.08% a year, but the P/E fell from 15.1 to 7.4 and the return was only 5.92%. In the 1990s the P/E doubled from 14.5 to 28.4. The next ten years returned −0.95% a year.

The balance sheet is a list on one date. Assets are what the company owns. Liabilities are what it owes. The difference is equity (also called book value), the owners' share.

### The cash flow statement: profit against cash

Profit is an accounting figure. Cash is what arrived in the bank. They differ because some costs are not paid in cash that year (depreciation spreads the price of a building over its life), some pay is in shares, and the timing of payments moves cash without touching profit.

Costco reported net income of $9,226 million but its operations brought in $15,817 million of cash. It then spent $6,435 million on new warehouses and equipment, which is called capital spending. What is left is **free cash flow**: cash from operations minus capital spending, the money the owners could take out without shrinking the business. For Costco that was $9,382 million. Dividends took $2,458 million and share buybacks (the company buying its own shares) $848 million.

### The whole market

The same measures exist for the S&P 500. On 23 November 2026 its P/E was about 24.7, against an average of 16.2 since 1871 ([multpl](https://www.multpl.com/s-p-500-pe-ratio)). Its earnings yield was 4.05% and its dividend yield 1.12%, near the lowest on record.

## Intrinsic value and discounting

**Intrinsic value** is what a business is worth to an owner who keeps it: the cash it will pay out over its life, expressed in today's money. Buffett, following the economist John Burr Williams, defines it as the cash an asset will produce, discounted ([1992 letter](https://www.berkshirehathaway.com/letters/1992.html)).

**Discounting** is the step that needs explaining. A dollar next year is worth less than a dollar today, because today's dollar can be invested in the meantime. If money can earn 9% a year, $91.74 today becomes $100 in a year. So $100 due in a year is worth $91.74 today, and $100 due in ten years is worth $42.24. The 9% is the **discount rate**: the return the buyer demands.

A **discounted cash flow** (DCF) applies this to every future year. Take an imaginary business:

1. It will pay its owner $100 next year.
2. The payment grows 6% a year for ten years, reaching $168.90 in year 10.
3. After that it grows 3% a year for ever.
4. The buyer wants 9% a year.

Each of the first ten payments is discounted to today: $91.70, $89.20, $86.80 and so on, down to $71.40 for year 10. They sum to $812. All payments after year 10 are valued in one step: the year-11 payment ($174) divided by the gap between the discount rate and the growth rate (9% − 3% = 6%) gives $2,900 at year 10, which is $1,225 today. The business is worth $812 + $1,225 = $2,037.

Sixty percent of that answer comes from the years after year 10, and small changes in the two rates move it a long way.

## Margin of safety and "Mr. Market"

Both ideas come from Benjamin Graham's book *The Intelligent Investor*, first published in 1949 ([overview](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Intelligent_Investor)).

**Margin of safety** (chapter 20) is the gap between the price paid and the estimated value. It exists because the estimate will be wrong. In the example above, a buyer who insists on paying one third less than the middle estimate pays at most $1,358. That is below the lowest of the nine values. The forecast can fail in every direction shown and the buyer has still not overpaid. Buffett writes that a value only slightly above the price is not enough to justify buying ([1992 letter](https://www.berkshirehathaway.com/letters/1992.html)).

**Mr. Market** (chapter 8) is Graham's picture of the stock market as a business partner who offers every day to buy the investor's share or sell his own. His prices swing with his mood. The investor is free to ignore him, and a new offer comes tomorrow ([summary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mr._Market)). A recent example: the S&P 500 closed at 7,340.53 on 17 November 2026 and at 6,910.39 three sessions later, a fall of 5.9% ([FRED](https://fred.stlouisfed.org/series/SP500)). The cash that 500 businesses will produce over the next twenty years did not change by 5.9% in three days. The price of it did.

## How the style evolved

**Graham: net-nets and cigar butts (1930s–1950s).** Graham bought shares priced below the company's net current asset value: cash, stock and money owed by customers, minus every debt. Such a "net-net" was cheaper than its own liquid assets, with the factories free. A study of these shares for 1971–1983 found 33.7% a year against 12.1% for the benchmark ([Oppenheimer, cited here](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Net_current_asset_value)). Buffett later named the method the cigar butt approach: a discarded business with one profitable puff left ([1989 letter](https://www.berkshirehathaway.com/letters/1989.html)).

**Fisher: growth at quality (1958).** Philip Fisher looked for outstanding companies that could grow for decades, checked them by talking to customers, suppliers and competitors, and held on. He bought Motorola in 1955 and kept it until his death in 2004 ([profile](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philip_Arthur_Fisher)).

**Munger: a wonderful business at a fair price.** Buffett's 1989 letter sets out the change. A cheap price on a poor business turns out less cheap than it looks, because the business keeps earning poor returns. He credits his partner Charlie Munger with seeing this first, and concludes that it is better to buy "a wonderful company at a fair price" ([1989 letter](https://www.berkshirehathaway.com/letters/1989.html)).

**Lynch: growth at a reasonable price.** Lynch's *One Up on Wall Street* (1989) made the PEG ratio popular: the P/E divided by the yearly growth rate of earnings in percent. By his rule of thumb a fairly priced company has a P/E equal to its growth rate, a PEG of 1 ([PEG ratio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PEG_ratio)). Costco's P/E of 53.1 against 14% growth is a PEG of 3.8.

**Fama and French: value by formula (1992).** The economists Eugene Fama and Kenneth French sorted all US stocks by book value against price and found that the cheap group had earned more than the dear group. Their factor HML ("high minus low") measures that gap every month ([three-factor model](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fama%E2%80%93French_three-factor_model)).

## Moats

A moat is a durable advantage that stops competitors from taking a company's profits. The term is associated with Buffett's letters ([overview](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economic_moat)). The usual kinds, with one example each chosen for this primer:

| Kind | How it protects profit | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Brand | Customers pay more for the name | Coca-Cola |
| Switching costs | Leaving is expensive or disruptive | Microsoft's office software |
| Network effects | Each user makes the product more useful to the others | Visa's card network |
| Cost advantage | The company can sell for less than rivals and still profit | Costco |
| Scale | The market supports only one or two suppliers | A railroad such as Union Pacific |
| Licences and patents | The law keeps others out | A drug under patent; a regulated utility |

The evidence for a moat is a high return on invested capital held for many years. Without a moat, high returns attract competitors until they fall to the **cost of capital** (the return lenders and owners demand). In Damodaran's January 2026 data, US soft-drink companies earned 29.0% on capital against a cost of 6.3%, and software companies 29.3% against 9.3%. Airlines earned 7.9% against 6.7%, steel makers 6.7% against 7.8% and car makers 2.3% against 9.4% ([Damodaran](https://pages.stern.nyu.edu/~adamodar/New_Home_Page/datafile/EVA.html)).

## Compounding

Compounding is earning a return on past returns. At 10% a year, $10,000 becomes $11,000, and the next year's 10% is earned on $11,000.

The **rule of 72** gives the doubling time: 72 divided by the yearly rate. At 10% money doubles in about 7.2 years, at 15% in 4.8, at 20% in 3.6.

| $10,000 at | 10 years | 20 years | 30 years | 40 years |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 10% a year | $25,937 | $67,275 | $174,494 | $452,593 |
| 15% a year | $40,456 | $163,665 | $662,118 | $2,678,635 |
| 20% a year | $61,917 | $383,376 | $2,373,763 | $14,697,716 |

Two things follow. Small differences in rate become very large differences in money: 20% is twice 10%, and after 40 years it gives 32 times as much. And most of the money comes at the end: at 10%, 63% of the 40-year gain arrives in the last ten years. This is why the style prizes businesses that can reinvest at high returns for decades.

Fees compound too. A 1% yearly fee turns 10% into 9%. Over 40 years $10,000 then grows to $314,094 in place of $452,593. The fee has taken $138,498, or 31% of the final sum.

## Does value work? The evidence

### The academic factor

Kenneth French publishes the HML factor back to 1927. Averaged by decade, cheap stocks beat dear ones in every decade until the 2010s. Over 1927–2006 the gap averaged 5.9 points a year. Then it reversed: over 2007–2020 it averaged −6.7 points a year and was positive in 4 of 14 years. In 2020 alone it was −46.9 ([French data library](https://mba.tuck.dartmouth.edu/pages/faculty/ken.french/data_library.html); averages calculated here).

### Two meanings of "value"

An index needs a rule. FTSE Russell sorts stocks mainly by book value against price, together with two measures of growth ([FTSE Russell](https://www.lseg.com/content/dam/ftse-russell/en_us/documents/other/ftse-russell-your-index-matters-styles.pdf)). On 20 November 2026 the value fund's holdings traded at 3.3 times book and 23.1 times earnings, the growth fund's at 12.8 times book and 32.7 times earnings ([iShares value fund](https://www.ishares.com/us/products/239708/ishares-russell-1000-value-etf); [growth fund](https://www.ishares.com/us/products/239706/ishares-russell-1000-growth-etf)).

Buffett means something else. In his 1992 letter he calls the term "value investing" redundant, says growth is one of the inputs to value, and says a low P/E, a low price-to-book or a high dividend yield does not settle whether a purchase is good value ([1992 letter](https://www.berkshirehathaway.com/letters/1992.html)). By his definition a company at 13.6 times book can be cheap and one at 0.5 times book can be dear. The drought in the charts above is a drought of the index test.

### Value traps

A value trap is a share that looks cheap on the measures and keeps falling because the business is shrinking.

- **Eastman Kodak.** Revenue peaked near $16 billion in 1996. A Kodak engineer had built the first hand-held digital camera in 1975, and digital photography then removed the film business. Kodak filed for bankruptcy on 19 January 2012 ([history](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kodak)).
- **Sears Holdings.** Formed in 2005 from Sears and Kmart, it was held by some investors for its property. Revenue fell from about $53.0 billion in 2007 to $16.7 billion in 2018. It filed for bankruptcy on 15 October 2018 ([history](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sears_Holdings)).

In both cases the assets were real and the earnings on them were vanishing. A low multiple of a falling number is not a margin of safety.

## Can stock pickers beat an index? The base rates

A base rate is how often something has happened in the past. Three matter here.

**Most professional funds lag.** S&P's SPIVA scorecard compares actively managed funds (funds whose managers choose the stocks) with an index. Over the 20 years to December 2025, 92.9% of US large-company funds returned less than the S&P 500. Only 34.6% of the 760 funds that existed at the start still existed at the end ([SPIVA U.S. Year-End 2025](https://www.spglobal.com/spdji/en/spiva/article/spiva-us-year-end-2025/)). At mid-2026 the 15-year figure was 90.5% ([SPIVA data](https://www.spglobal.com/spdji/en/research-insights/spiva/)).

**Investors earn less than their funds.** Morningstar's "Mind the Gap" study compares a fund's return with what the average dollar in it earned, which depends on when investors bought and sold. Over the ten years to December 2025, funds returned 9.9% a year and their investors earned 8.7%. The gap of 1.2 points a year came from buying after gains and selling after losses. It was 0.4 points for broad US stock funds and 1.2 for funds that hold one sector ([WealthManagement.com, 6 Aug 2026](https://www.wealthmanagement.com/investing-strategies/investors-miss-out-on-12-of-fund-returns-over-decade)).

> [!warn] What the base rates do and do not say
> They do not say that skill is absent: the records in the two profiles are real. They say that the starting assumption for any stock picker is a result below the index.

### Insurance float

Berkshire Hathaway, the holding company Buffett ran, owns insurers. An insurer is paid first and pays claims later. The money it holds in between is called **float**.

A worked example: an insurer collects $1,000 of premiums on 1 January. Claims and costs of $970 are paid, on average, a year later. Meanwhile the $1,000 is invested at 5% and earns $50. The insurer makes $30 from insurance and $50 from investing other people's money. Even if claims and costs came to $1,020, a loss of $20 on insurance, it would have had the use of $1,000 for a year at a cost of 2%, less than a bank would charge.

Berkshire's float was $176 billion at the end of 2025, up from $88 billion at the end of 2015 ([2025 annual report](https://www.berkshirehathaway.com/2025ar/2025ar.pdf)).

### For a swing trader holding days to weeks

Fundamentals explain why a stock leads. The oldest evidence is the post-earnings-announcement drift: after a company reports a profit well above what was expected, its shares tend to keep rising for weeks. Ball and Brown documented it in 1968. Bernard and Thomas found in 1989 that stocks with the best earnings surprises beat those with the worst in 41 of 48 quarters from 1974 to 1985 ([summary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Post%E2%80%93earnings-announcement_drift)). Studies report 10% to 25% a year for buying the best and selling short the worst, though a 2018 paper found the pattern far weaker stock by stock than in the averages ([Katz, McCubbins and McMullin](https://jkatz.caltech.edu/documents/28622/peads.pdf)).

Valuation is a poor timing tool. The Shiller P/E began 2023 at 28.3, 2024 at 32.0 and 2025 at 37.1, far above its average each time ([multpl](https://www.multpl.com/shiller-pe/table/by-year)). The S&P 500 then returned 26.1%, 24.9% and 17.8% ([Damodaran](https://pages.stern.nyu.edu/~adamodar/New_Home_Page/datafile/histretSP.html)). A high multiple says little about the next few weeks.

> [!test] Two hypotheses to check
> - In a trade history, breakouts and gap entries in stocks whose latest quarterly earnings grew more than 25% gave a higher average result than the same setups in stocks with flat or falling earnings.
> - Earnings gaps that came with a raised company forecast held their gains over the next 20 sessions more often than gaps without one.

### For a day trader

Almost nothing transfers. A holding period of minutes is too short for earnings, margins or moats to matter. Two things remain. One is the earnings calendar: results arrive on known dates, mostly outside trading hours, and the session after is not a normal one. The other is the discipline of knowing what is held and why, so that a trade meant to last an hour is not kept for a month on the strength of a story found afterwards.

> [!test] A hypothesis to check
> Opening range breakouts in a stock on the session after its earnings report have a different win rate and average result in R (the fixed amount risked per trade) than breakouts in the same stock on other days.

### What does not transfer

- **Permanent capital.** A holding company cannot be forced to sell. An individual who may need the money can be.
- **Float.** Cheap, long-dated borrowed money requires owning an insurer.
- **Access.** Large investors speak to managements and are offered private deals.
- **Time.** Reading one 10-K properly takes hours, and a professional reads hundreds a year.
