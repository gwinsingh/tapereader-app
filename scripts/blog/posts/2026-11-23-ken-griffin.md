Ken Griffin founded Citadel in 1990 and still runs it. Citadel is a multi-strategy hedge fund (a private fund that splits its money among many specialist teams, bets on falls as well as rises, and uses borrowed money). He also founded and mostly owns Citadel Securities, a separate company that is the largest market maker in US stocks (a firm that buys from sellers and sells to buyers all day and earns the small gap between the two prices). His flagship fund, Wellington, is reported to have returned about 19% a year after costs for 35 years, with one very bad year: it lost about 55% in 2008. The one thing worth taking from him is that he does not rely on being right about markets. He built a system that takes many small hedged bets, cuts the losing ones by rule, and treats financing as the risk that can end everything. Almost none of the edge is available to an individual. The habits are.

The vocabulary used here is explained in the [quant primer](/blog/quant-primer). The profile of [Jim Simons](/blog/jim-simons) covers the other great firm in this group.

## How he got started

Griffin grew up in Boca Raton, Florida, and sold educational software by mail while at high school ([Fortune, 16 Apr 2007](https://web.archive.org/web/20100929065538/http://money.cnn.com/magazines/fortune/fortune_archive/2007/04/16/8404298/index.htm)). He entered Harvard in 1986 and finished an economics degree in three years.

He started trading as a student. The instrument was the convertible bond (a company bond that can be swapped for the company's shares). He priced these bonds against the shares with a computer program, bought the bonds that looked cheap and sold the shares short against them. Selling short means selling borrowed shares in order to buy them back later. The trade earns the mispricing between two linked securities and depends little on where the stock market goes. This is called convertible arbitrage.

His first partnership held $265,000, with his grandmother among the investors. He was short going into the crash of 19 October 1987 and made money. A second partnership then raised $750,000 ([Institutional Investor, Sept 2001](https://www.institutionalinvestor.com/article/2btfmc4i914x7pya9zwg0/home/boy-wonder)).

> [!warn] Where the satellite dish story comes from
> The best-known story about Griffin is that he fixed a satellite dish to his Harvard dormitory to receive live stock prices. The earliest account found is the Institutional Investor profile of September 2001. It quotes a former tutor at Cabot House, Julian Chang, who remembered the dish outside a third-floor window in autumn 1987, and Griffin himself: "I can't believe they let me put it up." The story has a named witness, which most trading legends lack. It was first printed 14 years after the event.

After graduation Frank Meyer, who ran Glenwood, a Chicago fund that placed money with outside managers, gave him $1m to trade (Fortune 2007, above). The firm that became Citadel began on 1 November 1990 with $4.6m ([Bloomberg Markets, 29 Apr 2005](https://jaginvest.blogspot.com/2005/05/americas-first-listed-hedgefund.html)). The 2001 profile says he raised $18m for a fund called Wellington Partners in 1991. The likely reading is $4.6m at the start and $18m a few months later.

The first lesson came in 1994. Bond markets fell, Wellington lost 4.3%, and investors pulled about a third of the capital. From 1 August 1998 investors had to accept long lock-ups (a period during which money cannot be withdrawn) or leave. Weeks later Russia defaulted and the hedge fund Long-Term Capital Management nearly failed. Other funds had to sell. Citadel, with its capital locked in, was a buyer. Wellington returned 30.5% that year (Institutional Investor 2001, above).

## Where the edge came from

Nearly all of the edge is structural. Griffin has said that even his best stock pickers are right about 54% of the time, and that the market maker wins on about 52% of its trades ([Stanford GSB, 16 July 2025](https://www.gsb.stanford.edu/insights/ken-griffin-investing-winning-why-hes-focused-future)). An advantage that small becomes a large return only when it is repeated across thousands of positions, hedged, and multiplied with borrowed money.

| Edge | Evidence | Open to an individual? |
|---|---|---|
| Structure: many hedged books on one balance sheet, with leverage | Gross assets about 8.8 times capital in 2026 | No |
| Structure: capital that cannot leave quickly | Lock-ups since 1998; charges of up to 16% for early withdrawal in 2008 | No |
| Structure: costs paid by investors | The funds repay all of the firm's costs, including all pay | No |
| Information: specialist research at scale | An energy team of 70 with four meteorologists by 2005; gas storage and transport today | No |
| Speed and technology | The market maker handles about one in four US stock trades | No |
| Behaviour: central control that cuts losers | A yearly risk budget for each manager; a unit cut in 2016, another closed in 2019 | Partly |
| Behaviour: spare capacity to buy from forced sellers | 1998, 2006, 2007, 2026 | Partly |
| Era | Convertible bonds in the 1990s, when few priced them by model | No |

Sources: Form ADV and brochure, linked above; Institutional Investor 2001; Bloomberg Markets 2005; [Risk.net, 26 Jan 2023](https://www.risk.net/awards/7955800/hedge-fund-of-the-year-citadel); [Fortune, 8 Dec 2008](https://web.archive.org/web/20100314111249/http://money.cnn.com/2008/12/08/news/companies/citadel_vickers.boyd.fortune/index.htm); [Fortune, 31 Mar 2026](https://fortune.com/2026/03/31/ken-griffin-citadel-securities-hedge-fund-miami-wall-street-trump-republican-politics/).

The contrast with [Stan Druckenmiller](/blog/stan-druckenmiller) is the sharpest in this series. Druckenmiller put most of his risk into a few large directional bets and trusted his own judgement. Griffin removed market direction from most of his books and spread the risk over hundreds of people. [Ray Dalio](/blog/ray-dalio) also built a system, but one run from a single set of written rules.

### Teams

The hedge fund has 3,284 employees, of whom 1,314 work in investment roles ([SEC Form ADV, 11 June 2026](https://reports.adviserinfo.sec.gov/reports/ADV/148826/PDF/148826.pdf)). They work in small teams, usually called pods (a portfolio manager plus a few analysts, running a separate book with its own profit and loss). A 2026 profile describes hundreds of teams of six to ten people across his two firms (Fortune 2026, above). A reliable count of the hedge fund's teams, or of the capital each manager runs, was not found.

In stocks, each team covers one industry in depth. It owns the companies it expects to do better and sells short the ones it expects to do worse, in roughly equal amounts. The book is then market neutral (built so that a general rise or fall in the market has little effect on it). What remains is the difference between the good and the bad companies.

### Central risk control

Risk is not left to the teams. A central unit, the Portfolio Construction and Risk Group, had more than 50 staff in 2023 and is led by the chief risk officer, Joanna Welsh. A committee she chairs gives every portfolio manager a yearly volatility allocation (a budget for how much the book's value may swing), down to single risk factors.

On top of the budget sit firm-wide rules (Risk.net 2023, above):

- Stock books must be beta neutral. Beta is the part of a stock's movement that comes from the market as a whole.
- A minimum share of each book's risk must come from the specific companies, not from broad themes.
- Limits apply to concentration, to funding, and to how fast a book could be sold. Stocks must trade above a minimum daily volume.
- In several businesses, the loss in a stress test may not exceed a set multiple of the book's normal swing.

The magazine that reported these rules called the firm's attachment to stress tests "near obsessional". A stress test asks what a book would lose if a past crisis, or an invented one, happened tomorrow. The results for every book are shown live on a wall of screens ([Risk.net, 27 Nov 2018](https://www.risk.net/awards/6161776/buy-side-risk-manager-of-the-year-citadel)). Managers are also steered away from the stocks most widely held by other hedge funds ([Risk.net, 26 Nov 2024](https://www.risk.net/awards/7960368/hedge-fund-of-the-year-citadel)).

> [!warn] What is not public about the loss limits
> Multi-strategy funds are known for automatic rules: a book that falls by a set percentage has its capital cut, and a book that falls further is closed. Risk.net confirms that Citadel has drawdown limits (a drawdown is a fall from a peak in value) but gives no numbers. Figures that circulate online, such as capital halved after a 5% fall, describe a rival firm in an unsourced post. No reliable source for Citadel's thresholds was found.

The effect is visible without the numbers. In February 2016, after early losses, more than a dozen people were cut from one stock unit and its head was replaced ([Fortune, 18 Feb 2016](https://fortune.com/2016/02/18/citadel-is-cutting-jobs/)). Another stock unit, Aptigon, was closed in 2019 according to Bloomberg reports (not verified in full).

### Capital and payouts

Citadel does not let the funds grow without limit. When profits exceed what the teams can put to work, the money goes back to investors: about $7bn after 2022, about $7bn after 2023 ([Fortune, 12 Dec 2023](https://fortune.com/2023/12/12/ken-griffins-citadel-return-7-billion-to-clients/)) and about $5bn after 2025 ([Tekedia citing CNBC, 23 Dec 2025](https://www.tekedia.com/citadel-to-return-5bn-in-2025-profits-to-investors-trimming-assets-to-67bn-amid-subdued-performance/)). The total since 2017 passed $32bn in January 2026 ([Institutional Investor, 19 Jan 2026](https://www.institutionalinvestor.com/article/chris-hohns-tci-tops-hedge-fund-gains-2025)). At the end of 2024 the payout was optional, and investors took out only about $300m ([Hedgeweek citing Bloomberg, 3 Jan 2025](https://www.hedgeweek.com/majority-of-citadel-investors-decline-offer-to-redeem-profits/)).

### Fees

Most hedge funds charge a yearly management fee and a share of profits. Citadel uses pass-through fees (the fund repays the manager's actual costs instead of a fixed percentage). The brochure states that the funds fully reimburse the firm's service companies, including the pay of all employees. The firm then takes a share of gains above the high-water mark (the previous peak value of an investor's holding) each 31 December (brochure, above).

The costs are large. They ran above 3% of assets a year in 2001 and reached 8.75% in 2005 (Institutional Investor 2001 and Fortune 2007, above). A bond document from February 2025 gave a fuller picture for recent years.

### People

Griffin's stated method is to hire very able graduates, pay more than banks do, and give responsibility early (Stanford GSB 2025, above). For the 2026 summer programme the two firms received more than 115,900 applications and took about 350 interns, an acceptance rate the firm put at 0.36% ([Fortune, 11 June 2026](https://fortune.com/2026/06/11/115k-young-people-applied-citadel-internships-350-made-cut-acceptance-rate-0-36/)).

Analysts must wait at least 12 months before joining a rival, and better-paid staff up to two years ([Hedgeweek citing Bloomberg, 14 Aug 2026](https://www.hedgeweek.com/citadel-tightens-staff-restrictions-with-non-competes-of-up-to-two-years/)). Staff and partners held 18% of the main funds at the start of 2025 (prospectus report, above).

### The other business: Citadel Securities

Griffin and his partners founded the market maker in 2002.

> [!define] Payment for order flow
> When a customer of a retail broker sends an order to buy 100 shares "at market", the broker usually does not send it to a stock exchange. It sends it to a wholesaler such as Citadel Securities, which fills the order itself at or slightly better than the best quoted price. The wholesaler pays the broker a small fee for the order. That fee is payment for order flow. Retail orders are worth paying for because they are small and, on average, carry no information about the next few seconds, so the wholesaler can earn the bid-ask spread (the gap between the best buying and selling prices) at low risk.

Forbes and Fortune both describe the firm as handling about one in four US stock trades. In 2021 its own website said it executed nearly half of US retail volume, as the chair of the SEC noted ([SEC, 9 June 2021](https://www.sec.gov/news/speech/gensler-global-exchange-fintech-2021-06-09)). It has since moved into government bonds, interest-rate swaps and corporate bonds.

## Risk and drawdowns

The stated rules are to size positions before the trade, to avoid concentration of any kind, and to plan for extreme events (Risk.net 2024, above). The record has two losing calendar years: a small one in 1994, and 2008, which nearly ended the firm.

**The ideas were not the main problem.** Convertible bonds accounted for about a quarter of the losses. Corporate bonds hedged with credit default swaps (contracts that pay out if a borrower defaults) lost money because the bonds and their hedges stopped moving together. The positions were financed with borrowed money, and after Lehman the lenders withdrew.

**Leverage figures differ by source.** A rumour in October 2008 put borrowing at 13 times capital. The firm said four times (Fortune 2008, above). A later profile said seven times (Chicago Magazine, 2011, as cited by [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Citadel_LLC); not verified). The numbers may count different things.

**Cash and lock-ups kept the firm alive.** On the 24 October call Griffin and his chief operating officer said 30% of capital was in cash and an $8bn credit line was unused. Margin calls (demands from lenders for more cash against positions) were met. Investors were tied in by two-year lock-ups and by charges of up to 16% for leaving early. In December the firm suspended withdrawals from both main funds for about ten months (Chicago Tribune, 13 Dec 2008, as cited by Wikipedia, above).

The often-repeated detail that television vans waited outside the office could not be traced to a source and is not used here. What Griffin has said on the record is enough. In May 2026 he called it "a near-death experience" and described a morning when he was not sure the firm would still exist that evening ([Stanford Leadership Forum, 5 May 2026](https://singjupost.com/?p=61476)).

### What changed afterwards

Griffin's own summary, given in 2026, is about funding. Before 2008 large trading firms borrowed in the same markets as banks and assumed that in a crisis credit would become dearer. It stopped. Banks could turn to the central bank and other firms could not. His conclusion was that a firm that is not a bank must not fund itself as if it were one (Stanford Leadership Forum, above).

In practice the firm closed side businesses, among them a $1bn fund that invested in other managers (Fortune 2008, above), and later built the central risk group described earlier. In the interview closest to the event he said: "With hindsight, of course, I would have been all in cash!"

Drawdowns since then have been small. The main fund was down 6.5% by mid-February 2016 and ended that year up about 5% (Fortune 2016, above; [Institutional Investor, 13 Jan 2017](https://www.institutionalinvestor.com/article/b18bk2145hpk69/citadel-posts-smallest-gain-in-years-in-tough-year-for-multistrats), figure not verified). It fell 1.7% in February 2025, its largest monthly fall since May 2021, when several multi-strategy funds cut the same positions at once ([Hedgeweek citing Bloomberg, 13 Mar 2025](https://www.hedgeweek.com/multi-strats-troubled-by-trump-trade-war/)).

### The losses and failures

**2008** is covered above.

**E*Trade, 2007.** On 29 November 2007 Citadel provided $2.55bn to the online broker. It bought the broker's mortgage-linked securities for about $800m, a fraction of face value, took notes paying 12.5% and received just under 20% of the shares. The share price then fell for years. Citadel sold its last shares in March 2013 ([Fox Business, Mar 2013](https://www.foxbusiness.com/markets/etrades-shares-fall-as-citadel-sells-all-27-4m-of-its-shares)). Press reports say the whole investment made money (not verified). It also tied up capital ten months before the firm needed every dollar.

**The investment bank, 2008 to 2011.** Griffin had spoken of building a rival to the large Wall Street banks and hired senior bankers in 2008. Two heads of the unit left within about a year each. In August 2011 about 25 bankers moved to Wells Fargo and the project ended ([Wells Fargo, 15 Aug 2011](https://www.businesswire.com/news/home/20110815005584/en/Wells-Fargo-Securities-Hires-Investment-Bankers-Citadel); not verified in full).

**Melvin Capital, 2021.** Melvin was a hedge fund that had sold GameStop shares short. In January 2021 small investors, many organising online, drove the price up, and Melvin lost 53% in the month ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Melvin_Capital)). On 25 January Citadel's funds and partners put in $2bn and Point72 put in $750m, for a share of Melvin's future revenue ([Melvin press release, 25 Jan 2021](https://prnewswire.com/news-releases/melvin-announces-2-75-billion-investment-from-citadel-and-point72--301214477.html)). This was the hedge fund acting, not the market maker. Citadel asked for about $500m back in August 2021 ([Bloomberg, 21 Aug 2021](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2021-08-21/citadel-to-redeem-about-500-million-from-melvin-capital-dj)). Melvin announced its closure on 18 May 2022. What Citadel finally recovered is not public.

### GameStop: what was established

On 28 January 2021 Robinhood and other brokers stopped customers from buying GameStop and several other shares. Citadel Securities was Robinhood's largest source of revenue, and Citadel had just financed Melvin. A claim spread that Griffin's firms had ordered the halt. A regulator and two courts have examined it.

The SEC staff reported on 14 October 2021 ([SEC staff report](https://www.sec.gov/files/staff-report-equity-options-market-struction-conditions-early-2021.pdf)). Its main findings:

- GameStop closed at a high of $347.51 on 27 January and touched $483.00 the next day. The number of accounts trading it rose from about 10,000 a day to nearly 900,000.
- Short sellers buying back shares were a small part of the buying. In the staff's words, "it was the positive sentiment, not the buying-to-cover, that sustained" the rise.
- On 27 January the clearing house (the body that stands between buyers and sellers until trades settle) demanded an extra $6.9bn from 36 member firms within the day. Brokers said this was why they restricted buying.
- Citadel Securities handled nearly half of the GameStop volume that wholesalers filled in January.

## Criticism and mistakes

### The conflict of interest

One person controls a hedge fund that bets on share prices and the firm that fills a large share of small investors' orders. The hedge fund's own filing lists this as a risk and says the two units keep information barriers and do not coordinate (brochure, above).

The best-argued criticism came from Gary Gensler, then chair of the SEC, on 9 June 2021. Seven wholesalers handled most retail orders, he said, and one of them claimed nearly half. Brokers paid for orders also have a reason to encourage more trading. "Market concentration can deter healthy competition and limit innovation" (SEC speech, above). In December 2022 the SEC proposed sending retail orders to open auctions. The proposal was withdrawn with 13 others on 12 June 2025 ([LexBlog, 16 June 2025](https://www.lexblog.com/2025/06/16/sec-formally-withdraws-various-proposed-rules/)). Payment for order flow remains legal in the United States.

Griffin's reply is that the practice is long-standing, disclosed and regulated, and is a main reason small investors can trade without commission (testimony, above).

Robinhood's annual reports show the size of the relationship. Citadel Securities supplied 34% of Robinhood's revenue in 2020, 22% in 2021, 16% in 2022, 12% in 2023 and 2024, and 13% in 2025. On Robinhood's revenue of $958m in 2020 and $4,473m in 2025 that is about $326m and about $581m (computed). Sources: Robinhood Form 10-K for [2022](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1783879/000178387923000045/hood-20221231.htm), [2023](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1783879/000178387924000054/hood-20231231.htm) and [2025](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1783879/000178387926000023/hood-20251231.htm).

### The cost to investors

About 43% of gross gains went to costs and fees in 2021 to 2024 (figure above), and investors bear the cost of pay even in a poor year. The counter-evidence is the net result, and that investors declined to take money out when offered. No direct reply from Griffin on fees was found.

### Risk to the system

Regulators worry that multi-strategy funds borrow heavily and hold similar positions. The International Monetary Fund wrote in October 2026 that hedge funds held about 9% of US government bonds and that margin calls can turn them from suppliers of liquidity into amplifiers of stress ([IMF, 6 Oct 2026](https://www.imf.org/en/blogs/articles/2026/10/06/hedge-funds-improve-market-functioning-but-can-also-amplify-stress)). The governor of the Bank of England made a similar point in March 2025 (Hedgeweek, above). In 2023 the SEC raised the basis trade (buying government bonds with borrowed money against a sale of bond futures). Griffin answered that the agency was "searching for a problem", that the trade lowers the government's borrowing cost, and that regulators should look at the banks that lend ([Fortune, 8 Nov 2023](https://fortune.com/2023/11/08/ken-griffin-gary-gensler-citadel-hedge-fund-treasury-basis-trade-sec-chairman/)).

His own 2008 is the strongest evidence for the critics. A hedged, diversified fund lost 55% because lenders withdrew.

### The gate and the culture

Investors could not withdraw for about ten months from December 2008. A gate (a suspension of withdrawals) protects the investors who stay and is allowed by the fund documents. It also shows that the lock-up is part of the product.

On culture, a rival, Dan Loeb, called the firm a "gulag" in a 2005 email, and Fortune reported in 2007 that at least 15 senior managers had left in five years. In 2026 the complaint is the non-compete terms that now reach junior analysts (Hedgeweek, 14 Aug 2026, above).

## Routine and mindset

Little is documented about Griffin's day. Before 2008 he was at his desk by 7 am (Fortune 2008, above). He says he looks at the firm's profit and loss every day (Stanford GSB 2025, above). Former colleagues described a man who reads management books and wants only dense, technical conversations (Fortune 2007, above).

His stated views:

- **On being wrong.** He says he has been wrong millions of times and calls losses the cost of learning. The aim is a small edge applied often, not a high hit rate (Stanford GSB 2025).
- **On conviction.** An investor should press an advantage and leave quickly when wrong. "We're here to win" (same interview).
- **On emotion.** He relies on partners who challenge him when he is attached to a position.
- **On 2008.** He had not imagined that the largest banks could all need rescue at once (Fortune 2008). The lesson he repeats is about funding, not about the trades.

In public life he is a large donor. He pledged $3bn to Carnegie Mellon University on 30 September 2026, most of it for a campus in Miami ([Carnegie Mellon, Sept 2026](https://www.cmu.edu/news/stories/archives/2026/september/griffins-landmark-gift-will-shape-the-future-of-higher-education)), after more than $2bn of earlier gifts. He has given almost $250m to political candidates in a decade, mostly Republicans, and has called tariffs a regressive tax (Fortune 2026, above). He moved both firms from Chicago to Miami in 2022, and owns well-known paintings and expensive homes ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kenneth_C._Griffin)).

## What carries over

The returns cannot be copied. Some of the rules can be studied.

### Investing (years)

- **Leverage and forced selling do the damage, not volatility.** Citadel's hedged funds lost 55% in 2008 while the S&P 500 lost 36.6%. An investor who adds to index funds on a schedule, without borrowing, cannot be forced to sell in a pullback.
- **Recovery arithmetic.** A 55% loss needed a 122% gain to repair. A 36.6% index loss needed 58%.

### Swing trading (days to weeks)

- **Separate the market's part from the stock's part.** Citadel's stock books must be market neutral, so a stock picker's result is the difference between longs and shorts. A swing trader who buys leading stocks on pullbacks and breakouts is deliberately taking market direction. The useful habit is to measure it.
- **Crowding.** Citadel steers its managers away from the most widely held hedge fund names, which fall hardest when funds cut together, as in early 2016 and early 2025. The IMF found the most crowded stocks ten percentage points more volatile. Leading stocks are often crowded stocks.
- **Stress the whole book, not one trade.** Every Citadel book has a limit on its loss in a bad scenario. The general counterpart is to ask what an account loses if every open position gaps against it at once, and to size margin borrowing so that a bad week cannot force a sale.
- **Keeping gains.** The firm pays profits out of the funds once they exceed what the teams can use. The nearest equivalent for a trading account is withdrawing part of the profit after a strong run, so that the next drawdown is taken on a smaller base.

### Day trading (minutes to hours)

- **Who is on the other side.** A retail market order in a US stock is usually filled by a wholesaler, not on an exchange. In January 2021 about 80% of off-exchange GameStop trades were filled this way (SEC staff report, page 37). The wholesaler's income is the spread. The trader's cost is the spread plus any slippage.
- **The open is the expensive time.** Spreads are generally widest and quotes thinnest in the first minutes of the session (the evidence is in the [quant primer](/blog/quant-primer)). Strategies that enter on a break of the 5-minute opening range trade exactly then, often with market or stop orders.
- **A win rate near 52% works only at very low cost.** The market maker has the cost base and the volume. An individual has neither, so the edge has to come from fewer trades with a larger payoff than loss, as in a fixed risk unit with a target of several units.
- **Daily limits are the individual's version of a pod's loss limit.** A daily trade limit, a maximum daily loss and smaller size after a losing streak do what the central risk group does for a portfolio manager.

### Seven questions

| Question | What the record shows |
|---|---|
| Sizing up when right, cutting when wrong | At the level of books, not single trades. Each manager gets a yearly risk budget; managers who lose have capital cut or leave. Adding on strength or on pullbacks: not found |
| After a string of losses | Loss limits exist for every book; the thresholds are not public. In September 2008 the instruction was to sell the weak positions and keep the best |
| Keeping gains | Books are hedged, and profits are paid out of the funds in most years |
| Market-wide filter | None for direction. The filters are liquidity and funding: stress tests, minimum trading volume, limits on crowded names |
| Separate books by horizon | Yes, by team and by company: quantitative, stock picking, macro, commodities, and a separate market maker. E*Trade, held from 2007 to 2013, is the nearest case of a rescue deal becoming a long holding |
| Record-keeping | A live risk centre showing every book's exposure and stress loss. A personal journal was not found |
| Preparation against execution | No ratio found. Only 1,314 of 3,284 staff are in investment roles |

> [!test] Hypotheses worth checking
> - In a day trader's log, market-order entries in the first five minutes after the open have worse fills, measured against the price one minute later, than entries after the first fifteen minutes. If so, part of an opening range strategy's result is cost, not signal.
> - For a swing trader, after subtracting the index's move over each holding period, the remaining return per trade is close to zero. If so, the results are mostly market direction.
> - Across a trade history, the worst drawdowns come on days when several open positions fall together, not from single large losers. If so, a limit on total open risk matters more than the stop on any one trade.

> [!take] What to take from it
> - A small edge, repeated and controlled, built this record. A high hit rate did not: his best stock pickers are right about 54% of the time.
> - Loss limits are set in advance and applied without debate.
> - The 2008 loss came from financing, not from the ideas. Borrowed money turns a temporary loss into a permanent one.
> - Cash in reserve is what allowed the best trades of his career.
> - The party filling a retail market order is a professional whose income is the spread.

## What cannot be copied

- **The balance sheet.** About 8.8 dollars of gross positions for each dollar of capital, financed by the largest banks.
- **The capital terms.** Investors who are locked in, who pay to leave early, and who can be gated.
- **The fee structure.** $17bn of costs charged to three funds in under four years. This pays for the next two items.
- **The people and data.** 1,314 investment staff, a risk group of more than 50, meteorologists, gas storage.
- **The phone call.** Distressed funds and their banks ring Citadel first, because few others can price and absorb a multi-billion-dollar book in a day.
- **The market maker.** Order flow bought from brokers and the systems to quote thousands of securities at once.

The 19% a year depended on all six. The habits listed above depend on none of them.

## What to learn first

- **Multi-strategy fund and pod:** one fund, many small teams, each with its own book and loss limit.
- **Market neutral:** a book built so the general market move cancels out.
- **Leverage and margin call:** borrowed positions, and the lender's demand for more cash.
- **Lock-up, gate and high-water mark:** limits on withdrawals, and the peak a fund must pass before it earns performance fees again.
- **Market maker and payment for order flow:** the firm that quotes both sides, and the fee it pays brokers for orders.

All of these are covered in the [quant primer](/blog/quant-primer).
