Peter Lynch ran the Fidelity Magellan Fund, a US mutual fund (a pooled fund that anyone can buy, priced once a day), from 31 May 1977 to 31 May 1990. The fund returned 29.2% a year over those 13 years, while the S&P 500 index of large US companies returned about 16%, and it grew from about $20m to about $14bn. His method was to sort every company into one of six types, to be able to say in two minutes why it should do well, to start small and add as the facts came in, and to sell by a rule that depended on the type. The one thing worth taking from him is that habit of naming the kind of position before buying it. The record is real, but most of its margin over the index was earned early, on very little money, and several popular claims about him do not survive a check.

The background for this profile (financial statements, valuation, the price-to-earnings ratio, compounding) is in the [value investing primer](/blog/value-investing-primer).

## How he got started

Lynch's father, a mathematics professor, died of cancer when Lynch was ten. From the age of 11 he worked as a golf caddie and heard members discuss stocks (Frontline, above; [Irish America, Sept 1994](https://www.irishamerica.com/1994/09/lynchs-law)). As a student he bought shares in an air-freight company, Flying Tiger. They rose about tenfold and paid for an MBA at Wharton.

He had caddied for Fidelity's president, interned there in 1966, joined as an analyst in 1969 and became director of research in 1974 ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peter_Lynch)).

His best-known early find came through his wife, Carolyn, who bought L'eggs tights in a supermarket and liked them. Lynch bought 48 pairs of the rival brand, had colleagues test them, and concluded that L'eggs was better. The maker, Hanes, became his largest position, a tenfold gain cut short by a takeover (Frontline, above). The story is the origin of "invest in what you know". It already contains the part that retellings drop: the product was only the lead, and the work came after.

He took over Magellan on 31 May 1977, aged 33.

## Where the edge came from

The returns came from analysis and effort, helped by access to information, a small fund at the start, and the era.

| Edge | Evidence | Open to an individual? |
|---|---|---|
| Analysis: each company judged by the rules of its type | Six categories with different expectations and sell rules | Yes |
| Behaviour: fully invested, no forecast, more money to what works | Interviews of 1987 and 1990 | Yes |
| Effort: looking at more companies than anyone else | 13-hour days, about 40 company visits a month, about 15 assistants | Partly |
| Information: direct talks with company managers | He could visit any company; the rule against selective briefings dates from 2000 | No |
| Structure: a tiny fund at first, then Fidelity's analysts | $20m and closed to the public until 1981 | No |
| Era: small US stocks from 1977, then the 1982–90 bull market | The index itself returned 16.5% a year in 1978–89 | No |

Sources: [AAII Journal, Jan 1997](https://www.aaii.com/journal/article/the-peter-lynch-approach-to-investing-in-understandable-stocks), a summary of his first two books; Time 1990 and Irish America 1994, above; index returns from [NYU Stern](https://pages.stern.nyu.edu/~adamodar/New_Home_Page/datafile/histretSP.html).

> [!define] Regulation FD
> A US rule, in force since 23 October 2000, that bars a company from giving important information to selected analysts or fund managers before the public ([SEC](https://www.sec.gov/rule-release/33-7881)). Before it, a manager who visited hundreds of companies could legally learn things the market did not yet know.

The contrast with [Warren Buffett](/blog/warren-buffett) is one of breadth: a few businesses held for decades, against more than a thousand stocks traded far more often.

### Finding ideas

He worked bottom-up (company first, economy last) and used no screen. Leads came from company visits, Fidelity's analysts and ordinary life. He compared it to turning over rocks: whoever looks at the most companies finds the most bargains (Frontline, above).

### Six categories

Every stock went into one of six groups before he bought it. The group told him how much to expect and what would make him sell.

| Category | What it is | What he expected | When he sold |
|---|---|---|---|
| Slow grower | Large, old company growing about as fast as the economy, paying dividends | A modest gain plus the dividend | After 30–50%, or when the business weakened |
| Stalwart | Large company growing profits 10–12% a year | At most about 50% in two years | After 30–50%, or when price ran far ahead of profits; then into another stalwart |
| Fast grower | Small company growing 20–25% a year, ideally in a dull industry | The "tenbaggers" (his word for a tenfold gain); also the riskiest | When growth was ending: new outlets disappointing, price very high against growth |
| Cyclical | Profits rise and fall with the economy: cars, airlines, steel | Large gains if bought early in a recovery | When unsold stock built up or prices fell |
| Turnaround | A battered company that may recover | A fast rise that depends little on the market | Once the recovery was complete and widely known |
| Asset play | Hidden assets worth more than the share price | The gap closing | When a buyer for the assets appeared |

Sources: AAII Journal, above, and [chapter notes on *One Up on Wall Street*](https://www.manassaloi.com/booksummaries/2016/03/04/one-up-on-wallstreet-lynch.html). A stock could change group, as Chrysler did (below).

### The two-minute story

Before buying, he wanted to be able to say aloud, in about two minutes, why the company was interesting, what had to happen for it to succeed and what could go wrong. The script differed by category: for a fast grower, where it could still expand; for a turnaround, whether the rescue plan was working (chapter notes, above). In 1993 he said a holding should be explainable to a ten-year-old in two minutes ([Charlie Rose, 4 Mar 1993, via Wikiquote](https://en.wikiquote.org/wiki/Peter_Lynch)).

### The numbers

The story had to be backed by figures (AAII Journal and chapter notes, above):

- **The profit record**, year by year, and whether growth fits the category.
- **Price against growth.** The price-to-earnings ratio, or P/E, is the share price divided by yearly profit per share. He compared it with the growth rate of profits.
- **Share of sales.** A hit product matters only if it is a large part of the company. L'eggs was large for Hanes; a successful plastic was under 7% of General Electric.
- **Cash and debt.** Ford in 1988 had $16.30 of net cash (cash minus long-term debt) behind a $38 share.
- **Inventories** (unsold goods) rising faster than sales: a warning, above all for cyclicals.
- **Managers buying shares**, and **few professional owners**: a stock that funds and analysts ignore is more likely to be cheap.

> [!define] The PEG rule
> PEG is the P/E divided by the yearly growth rate of profits. Lynch's rule of thumb: a P/E equal to the growth rate is fair, half the growth rate is attractive, twice is unattractive. For dividend payers he added the dividend yield (yearly dividend as a percent of the price) to growth first.

### Timing the entry

He used no charts and no forecasts. A fall in price with the story intact was a reason to buy more. He held that nobody can predict the market, interest rates or the economy: since 1900 the market had fallen 10% or more about once every two years and 25% or more about once every six, and he could recall nobody calling it right twice (Frontline, above).

His one gauge of market mood was a joke he did not trade on. At the bottom, party guests would rather talk to the dentist than to a fund manager. At the top they tell the fund manager what to buy ([excerpt of *One Up on Wall Street*](https://quartr.com/insights/investment-strategy/the-cocktail-party-theory)).

### Sizing: wide first, deep later

He bought small amounts of many companies and concentrated as conviction grew. In 1989 the fund held 1,400 stocks, but half the money was in 100 of them ([Business Day, 2019, quoting the 1989 book](https://www.businessday.co.za/bd/opinion/columnists/2019-03-26-street-dogs-a-few-good-stocks/)).

A US fund sold as "diversified" may not put more than 5% of its assets into one company when it buys. Summaries of his second book say Chrysler reached that limit in 1982 (not verified: the page could not be opened). He described his habit in 1990 as buying what he liked, watching it and adding to the best ones ([Deseret News, 7 Jan 1990](https://www.deseret.com/1990/1/7/18841992/magellan-fund-manager-hopes-to-keep-the-edge-in-90s)).

### Managing and exiting

He reviewed each story every few months. He used no stop orders (standing orders to sell if the price falls to a set level): a 10% stop, he argued, is triggered by ordinary swings (chapter notes, above). He sold when the story had played out, when it broke, or when a similar company offered a better one.

The mistake he warned against most was selling winners to keep losers, which he compared to pulling out the flowers and watering the weeds. Buffett phoned to ask permission to quote the line ([speech in Minneapolis, 26 May 1993](https://archive.mpr.org/stories/1993/05/26/peter-lynch-on-investing-strategies-and-misconceptions)).

### How the fund was actually run

- **Turnover** (the share of the portfolio replaced in a year) was about 300% in the early years by his own estimate, an average holding of about three months ([Huber, 11 Dec 2015, quoting *Beating the Street*](https://basehitinvesting.substack.com/p/the-misunderstanding-of-peter-lynchs-investment-style)). The filings show 82% to 101% for the fiscal years (the fund's accounting years, which end on 31 March) of 1987 to 1990 (SEC, above).
- **Growth stocks** were never more than half the fund, by his own account (Huber, above).
- **Hours.** At his desk by 6:45 am, 13-hour days, Saturdays in the office, with about 15 assistants (Time 1990, above). He travelled 13 or 14 days a month and saw about 40 companies a month in person (Irish America, above).
- **Sector tilts** followed the stories: carmakers from 1982 (Chrysler, Ford, Volvo), financial companies later (Fannie Mae, the student-loan company Sallie Mae, the insurer Kemper), and foreign and large steady companies as the fund grew. In January 1990 the ten largest holdings included Nestle and Unilever (Deseret News, above).

## Risk and drawdowns

His risk rules were few: stay fully invested, spread money over the categories, know why each stock is owned. There was no borrowed money and no hedge (a position that gains when the market falls). No rule for trading smaller after losses was found.

The price of being fully invested showed in October 1987. Lynch was in Ireland on his first holiday in eight years. The Dow Jones index fell 508 points on Monday 19 October and he flew home (Frontline, above). The fund lost more than $4bn of about $12bn. He told a reporter that the fund and the market had each lost about a third, and that all but about 2% of the fall in assets was price, not investors leaving ([Christian Science Monitor, 8 Dec 1987](https://proof.csmonitor.com/1987/1208/glynch.html)). He described the fund then as "fully invested, at the bottom and the top".

Magellan ended calendar 1987 up 1.0%, behind the index at 5.8%. The fiscal year to 31 March 1988 lost 9.64%, the one losing year among the four of his tenure that the filings cover. The same filings imply that roughly $0.5bn more left the fund than came in during that year and $0.7bn the year after (computed for this profile; approximate). A widely repeated detail, that the fund lost 18% or $2bn on the Monday alone, was not found in a source that could be opened.

In October 2025 he said that Magellan had fallen more than the market in every market decline of his tenure ([interview transcript, 6 Oct 2025](https://waterboystocks.substack.com/p/peter-lynch-interview)). He left before the fall of July to October 1990.

## The record

### The headline number

Magellan returned 29.2% a year from 31 May 1977 to 31 May 1990. $1,000 became about $28,000 (Frontline, above; [Yahoo Finance, 8 Jul 2023](https://finance.yahoo.com/news/fidelity-legend-peter-lynch-i-never-said-to-invest-in-the-stock-market-120755736.html)). A mutual fund is priced daily and files its results with the regulator, so this is among the best-documented records in the series.

The fund was closed to new investors from 1965 until July 1981, and in Lynch's first three years about a third of its shares were cashed in ([Fidelity](https://www.fidelity.com/myfidelity/InsideFidelity/NewsCenter/quickFacts/Magellan.html); Frontline, above). The public could own it for nine of his 13 years. Over those nine it returned about 22% a year against about 16.5% for the index, as William Bernstein calculated ([quoted by Carlson, 17 Jul 2016](https://awealthofcommonsense.com/2016/07/peter-lynchs-track-record-revisited/)).

The filings show the end precisely. Over his last three fiscal years, 1 April 1987 to 31 March 1990, the fund gained 32.9% and the S&P 500 29.2%. After the sales charge, $10,000 put into the fund became $12,894, against $12,919 in the index ([SEC prospectus, 1997](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/0000061397/000088019597000019/0000880195-97-000019.txt)). Against other funds it was still a strong finish: Magellan beat the average competing fund in each year from 1986 to 1990 (SEC 1996, above).

### Costs

Published returns are after yearly expenses of 1.0% to 1.1% of assets and before the sales charge, or load: 3% of each purchase. For a holder of all 13 years the load turns 29.2% a year into about 28.9%. For a one-year holder it turns a 20% year into 16.4%. The charge was dropped on 23 June 2003 ([SEC supplement, 26 Jun 2003](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/0000061397/000027530903000010/main.htm)). When it was introduced, and whether it began at 2% in 1981 as often stated, could not be confirmed.

### The claim that the average investor lost money

It is one of the most repeated stories in investing: Magellan made 29% a year, yet its average investor lost money, according to a Fidelity study. A newspaper column repeated it in April 2026, crediting Fidelity's "own internal analysis" ([Irish News, 27 Apr 2026](https://www.irishnews.com/life/personal-finance/one-up-on-wall-street-tells-ordinary-investors-something-they-rarely-hear-GTXHUILLPZCO3FOQNV6ECSG5P4/)).

No such study could be found, and the trail suggests there never was one.

- In 1996 a Canadian columnist, Duff Young, tried to source the claim, then worded as "half of Magellan's investors lost money". Fidelity told him it came neither from Lynch nor from anyone at the firm, and that it had never made such a study. Young, who had repeated the story himself, called it an urban myth ([Globe and Mail, 27 Jul 1996, archived](https://bylo.org/magellan.html)).
- A 2016 book by Spencer Jakab says Lynch calculated that his average investor made about 7% a year (quoted by Carlson, above). No statement by Lynch with that figure was found.
- The one estimate with a stated method is from 2010. Adjusting for when money arrived, the typical investor made 13.4% a year from 1981 to 1990, against 21.8% for the fund and 16.2% for the index ([CBS MoneyWatch, 16 Jul 2010](https://www.cbsnews.com/news/lessons-from-a-great-fund-managers-record)).

> [!warn] Legend against record
> The fund had no losing calendar year from 1978 to 1989. Money that arrived at the end of 1986, just before the worst stretch, was up 67% by the end of 1989 if left alone. An average loss would need most of the money to have been sold at a loss, and nothing supports that. What the evidence supports is smaller and still important: most of the money arrived after the best years, so the typical investor earned well under the fund's return and, on the one estimate available, less than the index.

Lynch's own remarks fit the weaker version: investors redeemed a third of the fund in his first three years, which were his best.

### Fannie Mae, 1977 and 1982–89

Fannie Mae buys home loans from banks. Lynch's diary shows a first purchase of 30,000 shares in 1977, sold too soon. He bought again in 1982, 1984, 1987 and 1988 as its profits improved. Magellan made about $500m in about five years, and Fidelity's funds together more than $1bn, which he thought might be a record for one firm in one stock ([Motley Fool, 13 Nov 2006, from *Beating the Street*](https://www.fool.com/investing/general/2006/11/13/do-you-have-what-it-takes-to-make-500-million.aspx)). It was the fund's largest holding in January 1990. The size of the position, usually given as the 5% maximum, could not be confirmed. A holding first sold early became his largest winner after several rounds of adding.

### Chrysler and Ford, 1982–88

In 1982 Chrysler was priced for bankruptcy. Lynch's case was that it had about $1bn of cash, had sold a division and held a government-backed loan, and so had years to recover (chapter notes, above). He recommended it in public in October 1982 (Frontline, above), held it as a turnaround, and sold in mid-1987 once it was an ordinary carmaker again. His book gives the rise as $2 to $48, adjusted for share splits. Ford was his second most profitable stock at $199m.

### La Quinta, Taco Bell and Dunkin' Donuts

These are the classic fast growers. La Quinta offered motel rooms about 30% cheaper than Holiday Inn by leaving out restaurants and meeting rooms. It was growing about 50% a year at a P/E of 10 and rose fifteenfold (chapter notes, above). Taco Bell he bought on his first day at Magellan; PepsiCo then bought the company, cutting the gain short (1993 speech, above). Dunkin' Donuts he noticed as a customer, studied, and held to about fifteen times his cost ([interactive investor](https://ii.co.uk/analysis-commentary/how-invest-best-peter-lynch-ii534756)). Dates and sizes for these were not found.

### Losses and errors

- **Bildner's, 1986–88.** A Boston chain of upmarket food shops, bought at its flotation (first sale of shares to the public) at $13 in September 1986. It expanded into new cities before the idea was proven, and the stock fell to 12.5 cents. He sold at losses of 50% to 95%. The rule he drew: wait until the concept works in a second place (chapter notes, above).
- **Long shots.** About 25 companies with an exciting story and no profits. He says he never broke even on one (1993 speech, above).
- **Selling too early.** Fannie Mae in 1977. Home Depot and Toys "R" Us are the names repeated in secondary sources; they could not be checked against the books. A loss on Texas Air, often cited, was not found either.
- **Missing.** More than 100 stocks rose tenfold during his tenure and he owned none of them (Frontline, above). Later he named Apple and Nvidia (CNBC 2023, above). He avoided makers of technology by choice. "I have zero AI stocks," he said in 2025 ([Benzinga via Yahoo, 16 Nov 2025](https://finance.yahoo.com/news/peter-lynch-ai-stocks-literally-203704664.html)).

## Why he stopped, and Magellan after him

He announced his retirement at the end of March 1990, aged 46. His father had died at 46, and he wanted time with his wife and three daughters. He said the fund's size was not the reason (Time 1990, above).

What happened next bears on whether the record was the man, the size or the era.

**Size and luck.** William Bernstein's case is that the public had nine years of Lynch, that the last four gave a thin margin, and that a dozen or so funds will beat the index by six points a year for a decade by chance (quoted by Carlson, above). The first two points match the filings.

**The edge has gone.** John Huber argues that the early results came from very fast trading on information gathered from managements, a route Regulation FD has closed (Huber, above). An individual never had it.

**"Invest in what you know."** Lynch made the criticism himself in 2015: he had never advised buying a stock because the shop is pleasant, and the slogan leaves out the study of the accounts ([Wall Street Journal, 7 Dec 2015, reprint](https://www.foxbusiness.com/markets/peter-lynch-25-years-later-its-not-just-what-you-know)). The evidence is against the short version. Norwegian investors hold 11% of their portfolios in their own industry and earn nothing extra on it, in some tests less ([Døskeland and Hvide, 2011](https://ideas.repec.org/a/bla/jfinan/v66y2011i3p1011-1041.html)).

**Do amateurs beat professionals?** In 1993 Lynch told an audience that 62% of investment clubs beat the market (1993 speech, above). Barber and Odean examined 166 clubs from 1991 to 1997: the average made 14.1% a year against 17.9%, and 60% lagged the market ([Financial Analysts Journal, 2000](https://ideas.repec.org/a/taf/ufajxx/v56y2000i1p17-25.html)). Their 66,465 households averaged 16.4% against 17.9%, and those who traded most 11.4% ([Journal of Finance, 2000](https://ideas.repec.org/a/bla/jfinan/v55y2000i2p773-806.html)).

**Index funds.** In December 2021 he said: "The move to passive is a mistake" ([Bloomberg, 7 Dec 2021, reprint](https://www.financial-planning.com/articles/peter-lynch-says-all-in-on-passive-investing-is-all-wrong)). Passive means index funds, which hold every stock in an index at low cost. His former fund is the awkward example: from 1990 to mid-2010 it returned 7.8% a year, 0.4 points behind the index (CBS MoneyWatch, above).

No regulatory or legal action against him was found.

## Routine and mindset

He treated stock picking as a count of attempts. A good picker is right about six times in ten, he says, and a few large winners pay for the rest (Frontline, above). That is why he was content to own hundreds of small positions and to be wrong often.

He kept a transaction diary, parts of which appear in his second book. After leaving the fund he stayed at Fidelity part-time to coach young analysts, wrote three books with John Rothchild, and gave much of his time to his foundation.

## What carries over

### Investing (years)

- **Staying invested through falls.** He was fully invested through 1987 and judged forecasts worthless. His 30-year example: investing each year at the market's high for the year earned 10.6% a year, at the low 11.7%, on the first day 11.0% (Frontline, above). For investors who add to index funds on a schedule, that is evidence on how little waiting for a pullback is worth.
- **The investor's return is not the fund's return.** The estimate of 13.4% against 21.8% measures what late buying cost in the best fund of its time.

### Swing trading (days to weeks)

Lynch is the nearest of the value names to a trader of leading growth stocks. Five points transfer.

- **Name the category before entry.** A fast grower, a cyclical recovery and a turnaround had different expected moves and exits. A swing trader's equivalents might be a breakout from a base, a pullback in a leader and an earnings gap, each with its own exit rule.
- **Know the story in two minutes.** A position without a stated reason is harder to hold through a normal shake-out.
- **Start small, add to what works.** Fannie Mae was built in several rounds as profits confirmed the case. He also added on price falls when the story held, unlike [Stan Druckenmiller](/blog/stan-druckenmiller), who added only after price agreed.
- **Sell rule by category.** He took 30–50% on steady companies and rotated, and held fast growers until growth itself slowed. His answer to giving back gains was a different rule for each kind of position.
- **No market filter.** He never reduced exposure on a market view, and the fund fell more than the market each time. For a trader who wants to keep gains when the market turns, his record shows the cost of that choice, not a solution.

### Day trading (minutes to hours)

Lynch did not day trade and used no stops and no charts. Nothing in his method fits a 5-minute opening range breakout strategy directly. Two principles carry over.

- **Hit rate and payoff.** He expected to be right six times in ten and relied on large winners. A fixed risk unit (1R, the amount risked per trade) with a target of several R rests on the same arithmetic.
- **The reason first.** Its intraday counterpart is a stock that is active for a known reason, written down before the open.

### Seven questions

| Question | What the record shows |
|---|---|
| Sizing up when right, cutting when wrong | Starts small; adds as the story is confirmed, and on falls with the story intact; sells when the story breaks. No price stop |
| After a string of losses | No rule found. He stayed fully invested |
| Keeping gains | A range of 30–50% for slow growers and stalwarts; otherwise held until the reason changed |
| Market-wide filter | None. A mood gauge (the party indicator) that he did not act on |
| Separate books by horizon | One fund; the six categories act as separate rule sets. Fannie Mae went from a position sold early in 1977 to the largest holding by 1990 |
| Record-keeping | A transaction diary; each story re-checked every few months |
| Preparation against execution | Almost all preparation: about 40 company visits a month |

> [!test] Hypotheses worth checking
> - In a trader's own history, trades labelled by type (base breakout, pullback in a leader, earnings gap), with the label assigned without looking at the result, differ in how far they run. If so, one exit rule for all types gives back gains on some.
> - Trades entered with a written one-sentence reason are exited less often on noise than trades without one.
> - For regular purchases of an index fund, the money-weighted return (which counts when each amount went in) is close to the fund's published return. A wide gap measures the cost of the buyer's own timing.

> [!take] What to take from it
> - The category came first. It fixed the expected gain and the reason to sell before any money went in.
> - Size was built in steps on evidence from the company, not from the price.
> - He never used a market forecast, and paid for it with deeper falls than the market.
> - The famous number was mostly earned small and early. On billions, the margin over the index was about two points a year.
> - The slogan was never the method. The product was the lead; the accounts were the decision.

## What cannot be copied

- **Access.** Hundreds of private conversations a year with company managers, legal until October 2000.
- **People and hours.** About 15 assistants, Fidelity's analysts, and 13-hour days for 13 years. He stopped at 46.
- **The start.** A $20m fund, closed to new money, buying small stocks.
- **The wrapper.** A fund pays no tax on its own trading and deals at professional cost. Turnover of 300% in a private account would be taxed and charged very differently.

The early years depended on most of these. The categories, the two-minute story and the sell rules did not.

## What to learn first

- **Earnings, P/E and PEG:** profit per share, the price paid for it, and that price against growth.
- **Balance sheet:** cash, debt and inventories.
- **Cyclical company:** one whose profits follow the economy.
- **Mutual fund, sales load, turnover:** a pooled fund, its entry charge, and how fast it trades.
- **Time-weighted and money-weighted return:** the fund's result, and the investor's after the timing of deposits.
- **Active share:** how much a fund differs from its index.
