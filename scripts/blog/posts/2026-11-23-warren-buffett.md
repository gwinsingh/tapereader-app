Warren Buffett is a value investor (someone who buys a business, or shares in one, for less than his estimate of what it is worth). He ran the Buffett Partnership from 1956 to 1969 and has controlled Berkshire Hathaway since 1965. He handed the chief executive's job to Greg Abel at the end of 2025 and the chairman's seat to his son Howard in September 2026, at 96. His method is to buy a few durable businesses at sensible prices, hold them for decades, and pay for them with money that costs almost nothing: retained profits and the premiums that insurance customers pay in advance. The record is the best documented of any investor: 19.7% a year for 61 years against 10.5% for the S&P 500 index. The part worth taking is behaviour: long stretches of doing nothing, holding through falls of half the share price, and admitting errors in writing. The structure cannot be taken, and the lead over the index was built before 1999.

The background for this profile (financial statements, valuation, margin of safety, compounding) is in the [value investing primer](/blog/value-investing-primer).

### Where he stands in November 2026

Buffett announced on 3 May 2025 that he would hand over, and the board appointed Greg Abel chief executive from 1 January 2026 ([Berkshire Hathaway, 5 May 2025](https://www.berkshirehathaway.com/news/may0525.pdf)). In November 2025 he wrote that he was "going quiet": no more annual letter and no long sessions at the annual meeting, only a yearly Thanksgiving message. He said he felt well, moved slowly and read with increasing difficulty ([Berkshire Hathaway, 10 Nov 2025](https://www.berkshirehathaway.com/news/nov1025.pdf)).

The 2025 letter, dated 28 February 2026, was Abel's first. It says capital allocation and the stock portfolio are now his responsibility. At the meeting on 2 May 2026 Buffett sat in the audience ([AP, 2 May 2026](https://wtop.com/national/2026/05/berkshire-hathaways-profits-double-as-shareholders-gather-for-the-annual-meeting-on-saturday/)). On 18 September 2026 he became chairman emeritus and stayed on the board; Howard Buffett, a director since 1993, became chairman. On 16 November 2026 he gave away another 6,150 Class A shares and now owns 182,140, all of which are to go to four family foundations by the end of 2034 ([Berkshire Hathaway, 16 Nov 2026](https://www.berkshirehathaway.com/news/nov1626.pdf); [14 Jul 2026](https://www.berkshirehathaway.com/news/jul1426.pdf)).

## How he got started

Buffett bought Benjamin Graham's *The Intelligent Investor* in 1949 and studied under him at Columbia in 1951. Graham taught him to buy shares priced well below the value of the company's assets, with a margin of safety (a gap between price and estimated value that leaves room for error). He worked at Graham's firm from 1954 to 1956, then went home to Omaha and formed three partnerships ([2013 letter](https://www.berkshirehathaway.com/letters/2013ltr.pdf); [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Warren_Buffett); [partnership letters](https://www.chudov.com/tmp/BuffettPartnershipLetters.pdf)).

He called the Graham method "cigar butts": a poor business so cheap that one last puff of profit was free. He says the 1950s, when he ran small sums this way, were the best decade of his life for returns ([2014 letter](https://www.berkshirehathaway.com/letters/2014ltr.pdf)). The partnership charged no fixed fee. Buffett took a quarter of any gain above 6% a year.

### Three books in the partnership

From 1962 he told partners that the money sat in three categories with different behaviour, and he later reported the results of each separately.

- **Generals:** undervalued stocks with no timetable. Usually 5% to 10% of assets in each of five or six, smaller amounts in ten to fifteen more. They moved with the market.
- **Workouts:** stocks whose result depended on a corporate event (a merger, a liquidation, a buyback) with a date. They earned steady returns whatever the market did, and he borrowed against them, up to a self-imposed limit of 25% of net worth.
- **Controls:** companies where the partnership owned enough to change policy.

| 1966 by category | Average invested | Gain | Return (computed) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Controls | $17.3m | $1.57m | 9% |
| Generals, private-owner value | $1.4m | $1.00m | 74% |
| Generals, relatively undervalued | $21.8m | $5.12m | 23% |
| Workouts | $7.7m | $1.71m | 22% |

Source: partnership letters of January 1962 and January 1967. The Dow Jones index fell 15.6% in 1966.

The formative mistake came out of this system. Berkshire Hathaway began as a general: a textile company bought from December 1962 at $7.50 a share, below its working capital of $10.25. In 1964 its chief executive agreed to buy the shares back at $11.50 and then offered $11.375. Buffett, annoyed over an eighth of a dollar, bought control instead. A short-term trade had become a permanent holding in a dying industry, with over a quarter of the partnership in it (2014 letter).

In May 1969 he told partners he would stop. Bargains found by numbers alone had almost gone, $100m was too much for the few that remained, and the market had turned speculative. "The only way to slow down is to stop," he wrote (partnership letter of 29 May 1969).

## Where the edge came from

The returns came from analysis and behaviour, multiplied by a structure that no individual has.

| Edge | Evidence | Open to an individual? |
|---|---|---|
| Analysis: judging business quality and price | See's, Coca-Cola, Apple. A study found his listed stocks did better than the companies he owned outright, so selection mattered more than management | Partly. The facts are public; the judgement is rare |
| Behaviour: patience and buying in panics | Purchases in 1974, 1988 and 2008; years with almost no trades | Yes |
| Structure: insurance float | $39m in 1970, $176bn at the end of 2025 | No |
| Structure: leverage of about 1.6 to 1 at low cost | "Buffett's Alpha" | No |
| Structure: permanent capital | One cash dividend in 60 years; no investor can withdraw | Partly (savings that are never needed) |
| Structure: tax deferral | $50.5bn of deferred tax at the end of 2018 | Partly (not selling) |
| Structure: private deals in crises | Goldman Sachs, General Electric, Bank of America | No |
| Era | 28.9% a year from 1965 to 1998 | No |

> [!define] Float
> An insurer collects premiums first and pays claims later. The money it holds in between is called float. It belongs to policyholders, but the insurer invests it and keeps the investment income. If premiums also cover claims and costs, the float is free.

### What the academic study found

In 2013 three researchers at the fund manager AQR examined the record from 1976 to 2011 ("Buffett's Alpha", linked above; the journal version appeared in the Financial Analysts Journal in 2018).

- Berkshire shares returned 19.0% a year above Treasury bills, against 6.1% for the stock market. The price swings were also larger: volatility of 24.9% a year.
- The Sharpe ratio (return above the safe rate divided by volatility) was 0.76. That was higher than any other US stock or mutual fund with 30 years of history, yet lower than many expect.
- Leverage averaged about 1.6 to 1. Insurance float was 36% of liabilities and cost 2.2% a year, three points below the Treasury-bill rate.
- Once the tilt to cheap, safe, high-quality stocks is accounted for, the unexplained part of the return (alpha) is no longer statistically significant.

The authors' reading is that he chose the right kind of stock decades before researchers named it, and applied cheap leverage to it without ever being forced to sell. The study also records the price: from June 1998 to February 2000 Berkshire lost 44% while the market gained 32%.

Charlie Munger, whom he met in 1959, moved him off cigar butts. Buffett's summary of the advice was to stop buying fair businesses at wonderful prices and buy "wonderful businesses at fair prices" (2014 letter, above). The test case was See's Candies in 1972. The sellers wanted $30m; Buffett would not go above $25m for a company with $8m of tangible assets, and nearly lost it. See's then earned less than $5m a year before tax. By 2014 it had earned $1.9bn in total and needed only about $40m of new investment ([2007 letter](https://www.berkshirehathaway.com/letters/2007ltr.pdf); 2014 letter).

Munger died on 28 November 2023. Buffett called him the architect of Berkshire and himself the general contractor ([2023 letter](https://www.berkshirehathaway.com/letters/2023ltr.pdf)).

## The process

### Finding ideas

He works bottom-up (company by company, not from a view of the economy). The input is reading: by his own account about five to six hours a day of newspapers and company reports (reported by [Farnam Street in TIME, 2015](https://time.com/3919319/warren-buffett-information-filter); the quotation could not be checked, not verified). Whole companies reach him by letter or phone. Berkshire's published criteria promise an answer on interest within about five minutes (2014 letter). In 2025 the owner-manager of Bell Laboratories, a maker of rodent-control products, wrote to him and Berkshire bought it (2025 letter).

The speed rests on preparation. He read IBM's annual report for 50 years before buying and watched Coca-Cola for 52 (2011 letter; [1989 letter](https://www.berkshirehathaway.com/letters/1989.html)).

### Forming a thesis

Four ideas recur, each with a written source.

- **Circle of competence:** an investor needs to judge only companies he understands; the size of the circle matters less than knowing its edge ([1996 letter](https://www.berkshirehathaway.com/letters/1996.html)).
- **Moat:** a durable advantage that protects high returns on capital, such as low cost or a strong brand. He rules out industries that change fast (2007 letter).
- **Owner earnings:** reported profit plus depreciation and similar non-cash charges, minus the spending needed to keep the business where it is ([1986 letter](https://www.berkshirehathaway.com/letters/1986.html)).
- **The punch card:** Munger reported in 1994 that Buffett told students they would do better with a ticket allowing only 20 investments in a lifetime ([Munger at USC, 1994](https://fs.blog/great-talks/a-lesson-on-worldly-wisdom/)).

### Timing the entry

He uses no charts and makes no forecasts. The 1986 letter says he and Munger never try to predict when fear or greed will arrive; they try to be "fearful when others are greedy" and the reverse. He repeated the line in a New York Times article on 17 October 2008, saying he was buying US stocks for his personal account ([New York Times](https://www.nytimes.com/2008/10/17/opinion/17buffett.html), not opened for this profile). The S&P 500 closed at 940 that day, fell another 20% within five weeks and bottomed in March 2009 ([CNBC, 17 Feb 2009](https://www.cnbc.com/2009/02/17/has-warren-buffett-been-buying-american-for-berkshire-hathaway-we-find-out-today.html)). Price relative to value was his only trigger, and it was early.

### Sizing

Positions are large from the start, and he adds when the price falls.

| When | Position | Share of capital |
|---|---|---|
| 1951 | GEICO | 65% of his own net worth |
| 1964–66 | American Express | About 40% of the partnership ($13m) |
| End of 1989 | Coca-Cola | 35% of Berkshire's listed holdings ($1.8bn of $5.2bn, computed) |
| End of 2023 | Apple | 49% of the stock portfolio ($174bn of $354bn) |

Sources: 2014 letter; [1994 letter](https://www.berkshirehathaway.com/letters/1994.html); 1989 letter; [2023 annual report](https://www.berkshirehathaway.com/2023ar/2023ar.pdf). From November 1965 the partnership's rules allowed up to 40% in one security when the facts were near certain.

He adds to losers, provided the business is intact. The Washington Post, bought for $10.6m in 1973, was worth $8m at the end of 1974 and he kept it ([1985 letter](https://www.berkshirehathaway.com/letters/1985.html)). He bought Wells Fargo in 1990 in a chaotic market for bank shares ([1990 letter](https://www.berkshirehathaway.com/letters/1990.html)). After buying IBM he wrote that he hoped the price would languish (2011 letter). This is the opposite of [Stan Druckenmiller](/blog/stan-druckenmiller), who adds only after the price confirms the idea.

### Managing and exiting

There are no stops and no hedges. For the best businesses "our favorite holding period is forever" ([1988 letter](https://www.berkshirehathaway.com/letters/1988.html)). The same letter borrows a phrase from [Peter Lynch](/blog/peter-lynch) for the opposite habit: selling winners and keeping losers is like cutting the flowers and watering the weeds.

The record of sales is longer than the saying suggests.

| Sold | What happened | His verdict or the outcome |
|---|---|---|
| McDonald's, 1998 | Sold in the year | "A very big mistake" |
| Freddie Mac and Fannie Mae, 2000 | Nearly all sold | Both were placed under US government control in September 2008 |
| PetroChina, 2007 | $488m became $4bn; sold when the market value reached his estimate | Sold on valuation |
| IBM, 2017–18 | $10.9bn position closed about flat before dividends (not verified) | Thesis wrong |
| Airlines, April 2020 | $7bn to $8bn invested; all sold at a large loss | "My mistake"; near the low |
| Wells Fargo, 2017–2022 | A stake of about 10%, held since 1989–90, sold in steps | Out after the fake-accounts scandal |
| Apple, 2024 | From 906m shares to 300m | Kept the rest; price rose further |

Sources: [1998 letter](https://www.berkshirehathaway.com/letters/1998htm.html), [2000 letter](https://www.berkshirehathaway.com/2000ar/2000letter.html), 2007 letter, [CNBC, 4 May 2018](https://www.cnbc.com/2018/05/04/warren-buffett-says-berkshire-hathaway-has-sold-completely-out-of-ibm.html), [CNBC, 2 May 2020](https://www.cnbc.com/2020/05/02/warren-buffett-says-berkshire-sold-its-entire-position-in-airlines-because-of-the-coronavirus.html), [InvestmentNews, 2022](https://www.investmentnews.com/wirehouses/buffett-exits-wells-fargo-stake-ending-decades-long-bet-on-bank/221579), Form 13F filings ([SEC EDGAR](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1067983/000095012325002701/xslForm13F_X02/39042.xml)).

He sells slowly, and counts that as a fault. Munger's word for delay in correcting a mistake was "thumb-sucking" (2024 letter). Tesco is the example: he soured on the management in 2013, sold only part, and took an after-tax loss of $444m in 2014 (2014 letter).

## Risk and drawdowns

His risk rules concern survival, not price. Berkshire keeps far more cash than it needs, will not buy back shares if cash would fall below $30bn, and depends on no lender (2008 letter; third-quarter report 2026). The share price is left to fall. A drawdown is the fall from a peak.

What he did in each case is consistent. In 1973–75 he bought: the Washington Post in 1973, and his three largest realised losses in 50 years came from selling cheap stocks then to buy cheaper ones (2014 letter). After October 1987 he bought Coca-Cola. From 1998 to 2000 he refused technology shares and explained why in public. In 2008 he spent $14.5bn on preferred shares, let cash fall from $44bn to $31bn, and still wrote that he should have done more: "reach for a bucket, not a thimble" ([2009 letter](https://www.berkshirehathaway.com/letters/2009ltr.pdf)). In 2020 he did the least: he sold the airlines and bought back $24.7bn of Berkshire's own shares over the year ([SEC company data](https://data.sec.gov/api/xbrl/companyfacts/CIK0001067983.json)). ## Cash and the market-wide filter

Buffett says he does not time the market. His cash position has still tracked the supply of bargains.

- **1969:** he closed the partnership because he could not find enough to buy.
- **1999:** in a speech at Sun Valley in July, turned into a Fortune article, he argued that stocks could return about 6% a year for the next 17 years, not the 13% to 22% investors expected ([Fortune, 22 Nov 1999](https://fortune.com/1999/11/22/warren-buffett-on-stock-market/)).
- **2001:** he described the ratio of the total value of US stocks to the size of the economy as "probably the best single measure" of valuation. At 70% to 80%, buying was likely to work well; near 200%, as in 1999, it was dangerous. It stood at 133% ([Fortune, 10 Dec 2001](https://fortune.com/2001/12/10/warren-buffett-stock-market/)).
- **2008:** he bought.
- **2024–26:** Berkshire sold far more stock than it bought.

The ratio, now called the Buffett indicator, was reported at about 225% in the third quarter of 2026 after a record near 232% in May ([Statista](https://www.statista.com/chart/21972/market-capitalization-to-gdp-ratio/); not verified against the underlying data). He has not commented on it in the 2026 documents.

He does not tell individuals to copy him. His will instructs the trustee for his wife to put 90% in a very low-cost S&P 500 index fund (a fund that simply holds all the stocks in the index) and 10% in short-term government bonds (2013 letter).

## Criticism and mistakes

**Lagging the index.** The numbers are above: 12.9% a year against 14.8% since 2009. David Rolfe of Wedgewood Partners, a long-time holder, sold in 2019 and blamed the cash pile, Kraft Heinz, IBM and the failure to buy companies such as Visa and Microsoft ([Fox Business, 2019](https://www.foxbusiness.com/money/berkshire-hathaway-investor-bails-buffett)).

**Missing technology.** At the 2017 meeting he said of Google "I blew it", although GEICO was a large advertising customer, and that he had been too slow to understand Amazon ([CNBC, 6 May 2017](https://www.cnbc.com/amp/2017/05/06/warren-buffett-admits-he-made-a-mistake-on-google.html)). Apple, bought from 2016 as a consumer franchise, repaired much of the damage.

**The Sokol affair.** In January 2011 David Sokol, a senior manager seen as a possible successor, bought 96,060 shares of Lubrizol and then proposed that Berkshire acquire it. Buffett's release of 30 March said neither man thought the purchases unlawful ([Berkshire Hathaway, 30 Mar 2011](https://www.berkshirehathaway.com/news/mar3011.pdf)). A month later the audit committee found that Sokol had broken company policy. Critics, among them the analyst Janet Tavakoli, faulted the mildness of the first statement ([Fortune, 27 Apr 2011](https://fortune.com/2011/04/27/berkshire-finally-scolds-sokol/)).

**Wells Fargo and Kraft Heinz.** He kept a stake of about 10% through the fake-accounts scandal of 2016 and sold only over the following years. On Kraft Heinz he said in 2019 that Berkshire had overpaid ([Reuters, 25 Feb 2019](https://business-standard.com/article/reuters/warren-buffett-says-berkshire-overpaid-for-kraft-heinz-119022500786_1.html)). When the company announced a split in September 2025 he said he was disappointed ([Fortune, 3 Sep 2025](https://fortune.com/2025/09/03/warren-buffet-kraft-heinz-57-billion-faceplant/)). Abel's first letter calls the return "well short of adequate". In January 2026 the stake was registered for possible sale; the split was later halted and Abel said no sale was planned ([Transport Topics, 2026](https://www.ttnews.com/articles/berkshire-kraft-heinz-stake)).

**Image against practice.** The journalist David Dayen argued in 2018 that the moats Buffett seeks are market power, hidden by a folksy image ([The Nation, 15 Feb 2018](https://www.thenation.com/article/archive/special-investigation-the-dirty-secret-behind-warren-buffetts-billions/)). ProPublica, using leaked tax data, calculated in 2021 that his personal income tax was 0.1% of the growth in his wealth from 2014 to 2018, a comparison tax experts dispute. In 2023 it reported that he had at least three times sold stocks in his personal account in or just before quarters when Berkshire traded them ([ProPublica, 2021](https://www.propublica.org/article/the-secret-irs-files-trove-of-never-before-seen-records-reveal-how-the-wealthiest-avoid-income-tax); [ProPublica, 9 Nov 2023](https://www.propublica.org/article/warren-buffett-privately-traded-stocks-berkshire-hathaway-ethics-irs)). Berkshire itself paid $26.8bn of US income tax in 2024 (2024 letter).

**Succession.** In 2025, the year of the hand-over, Berkshire gained 10.9% against 17.9% for the index. No regulatory action against Buffett was found.

## Routine and mindset

He has lived in the same house since 1958 and worked in the same building for 64 years (November 2025 letter). Most of the day is reading. He trades rarely: the 1990 letter calls the style "lethargy bordering on sloth".

His review habit is the annual letter, where he names his errors and their cost. He counted the words "mistake" or "error" 16 times in his letters for 2019 to 2023 (2024 letter), and in 1989 he listed the mistakes of his first 25 years. No private journal is documented. Munger was for 60 years the partner who corrected him.

Compared with [Ray Dalio](/blog/ray-dalio), who wrote his rules into computer programs and spread risk across many unrelated bets, Buffett kept the rules few and the bets concentrated.

## What carries over

### Investing (years)

- **The base rate favours the index fund.** Evidence: the ten-year bet, his instruction for his wife's money, and his own 20 years level with the index. For investors who add to broad index funds on a schedule, his record is evidence in favour of the schedule.
- **Falls of half are part of the best record there is.** Berkshire fell 37% to 59% four times and compounded at 19.7%. No borrowed money and no need to sell made the falls survivable.
- **Buying in a panic was early, not exact.** The October 2008 article preceded the low by almost five months, and the index fell another 20% in the first five weeks.
- **Inactivity is a decision.** In 1990 he changed one of six major holdings.

### Swing trading (days to weeks)

Little transfers. He uses no charts, no stops and no time frame under several years. Three things do.

- **Separate categories, reported separately.** In the partnership he knew which book made the money in each year. A swing trader who buys pullbacks, breakouts and earnings gaps could keep the same split.
- **A trade that changed category.** Berkshire began as a quick trade and became a 20-year burden when he kept it out of irritation. He names it as his costliest decision.
- **The amount invested followed the supply of good ideas.** He held more cash when he found little to buy. This bears on the common problem of giving back gains by staying fully invested after the good setups stop appearing.

One habit does not transfer: he adds to positions that fall. That rests on an estimate of value made independently of price. In momentum strategies price is the evidence.

### Day trading (minutes to hours)

Nothing in the record applies to intraday execution. Two general points remain.

- **The punch card.** Fewer, better decisions. A daily trade limit in a 5-minute opening range breakout strategy is the same idea on a small scale.
- **Written post-mortems.** He names each error and its cost in a public document.

### Seven questions

| Question | What the record shows |
|---|---|
| Sizing up when right, cutting when wrong | Large at the start when certain: 40% and more in one name. Adds on falling prices, never on strength. Cuts on a changed view of the business, slowly |
| After a string of losses | No rule. Size follows the opportunity, not recent results. He bought most in his worst drawdowns |
| Keeping gains | No targets or trailing exits. Sells on valuation (PetroChina), a broken thesis (IBM, airlines) or tax and price (Apple) |
| Market-wide filter | Denies timing. In practice cash rises when bargains are scarce: 1969, 1999, 2024–26. The measure is valuation, not trend |
| Separate books by horizon | Yes in the partnership: generals, workouts, controls. A personal account apart from Berkshire. A short-term trade that became permanent: Berkshire itself |
| Record-keeping | A public letter every year since 1957, with mistakes named |
| Preparation against execution | Nearly all preparation: hours of reading a day, a handful of decisions a year |

> [!test] Hypotheses worth checking
> - In a trader's own history, weeks with fewer trades have a higher average result per trade than busy weeks.
> - Trades that were kept after their original reason had failed (a day trade held overnight, a swing trade turned into a "long-term" holding) lose more on average than trades closed on plan.
> - When results are tagged by setup type and reported separately, one category carries most of the profit, and the mix of categories explains good and bad months better than the market does.
> - For index investors: on historical S&P 500 data, money held back to wait for a 20% fall ends lower over most 10-year windows than money invested on schedule.

> [!take] What to take from it
> - The best-documented stock picker of the century tells individuals to buy the index, and his last 20 years support the advice.
> - His returns were highest with small sums and a simple method, and fell as the money grew.
> - He held through three falls of about half because nothing was borrowed against the shares.
> - His costliest errors came from irritation (Berkshire), from paying in shares (Dexter) and from large purchases at full prices late on, not from market falls.

## What cannot be copied

- **Float.** $178.5bn of other people's money at a cost below zero.
- **Permanent capital.** No investor can withdraw, so a 50% fall forces nothing.
- **Private terms.** Preferred shares at 10% with warrants were offered to Berkshire because it could wire $5bn in a day.
- **The corporate tax position.** Profits of owned companies move to the parent untaxed, and unrealised gains defer tax for decades ([2018 letter](https://www.berkshirehathaway.com/letters/2018ltr.pdf)).
- **The early opportunity.** He says the cigar-butt method works only with small sums.

The 19.7% depended on these. The habits listed above did not.

## What to learn first

- **Intrinsic value:** an estimate of what a business is worth from the cash it will produce.
- **Margin of safety:** the gap between that estimate and the price paid.
- **Moat:** a lasting advantage that keeps competitors from taking the profits.
- **Owner earnings:** profit that could be taken out without weakening the business.
- **Float:** insurance premiums held before claims are paid.
- **Compounding:** gains earning further gains.
- **Preferred shares and warrants:** fixed-dividend shares, and rights to buy ordinary shares at a set price.
- **Index fund:** a fund that holds every stock in an index at low cost.
- **Drawdown:** the fall from a peak in value.
- **Form 13F:** the quarterly US filing of large managers' listed stock holdings.
