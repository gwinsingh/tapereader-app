Rallies inside declines are common, large, and usually fail

The study counts 91 rallies of 5% or more that failed inside drops since 1950, against 36 that turned out to be the low. Banks that have counted them reach the same conclusion: a sharp rally in a bear market is the norm. Size and speed in the first days did not tell the two kinds apart.

What the published counts say

  • Goldman Sachs counted about 19 bear-market rallies in world stocks since the early 1980s, lasting 44 days and gaining 10–15% on average. Goldman Sachs not verified (read through summaries; the page could not be opened).
  • Bank of America counted 43 bear rallies of more than 10% since 1929, averaging 17.2% over 39 days. not verified
  • The study’s own count: the 91 failed rallies gained a median 7.4% over 11 sessions, and the largest gained 21% (from 21 September 2001). The low was undercut a median 32 sessions after the rally began.
  • In 2022 six rallies of 5–17% failed before the seventh held. In 2000–02 and 2007–09 there were at least ten each.

The practitioner evidence points the same way. In Rob Hanna’s tests of rally signals from 1971, the signal worked more often after a shallow decline, when breadth on the day was in the top 5% of the past year, and when the index was above its 200-day average. Quantifiable Edges No rally inside the 2000–02 or 2007–09 bear markets produced a breadth thrust on the NYSE by Walter Deemer’s rule. Deemer

What to take from it
  • The first week of a failed rally and of a real turn looked alike. Breadth and the 200-day average separated them, weeks later.
  • A rally of 15–20% inside a bear market has many precedents.

How often a 10% drop went on, and how often the low was retested

About a third of the declines that reached −10% went on to −20%. A clean V-shaped low with no failed rally before it and no retest after it was the exception.

In the study’s table, the position against the 200-day average on the day of the first −10% close mattered. Six drops crossed −10% with the index still above that average; none reached −20%. Of the 29 that crossed below it, 11 did. One year after the first −10% close the index was higher in 26 of 35 cases.

Retests

Two rules give two answers, and both are worth knowing.

  • The study’s rule (lowest close 5 to 60 sessions after the low): the index came back within 3.5% of its low in 22 of 35 drops. In 9 of those the visit came 15 or more sessions after the low, a true later retest (1998, 2010 and February 2018 among them). In 4 it never came within 7% (1962, 2002, 2009, 2020).
  • The research file’s rule (only after a rally of 7% or more had happened, up to a year later, on 19 larger declines): back within 3.5% in 8 of 19, within 4–6.5% in 5, never within 7% in 6. This rule finds a retest in March 2003 that the 60-session window misses.

Before the final low, the first sharp rally had usually already failed. On the research file’s 19 declines, the first rally of 7% or more started from the final low in only 6. In the other 13 an earlier low was undercut.

The follow-through day: nearly every low had one, and about half of them failed

The follow-through day is the best-known signal that a rally attempt is real. Its promoter claimed a success rate of 70–80%. Independent tests find about 50%, and in the large bear markets almost every one failed.

Definition

William O’Neil’s rule, as used by Investor’s Business Daily: day 1 is the first higher close after a low. On day 4 or later, a major index gains strongly on volume higher than the previous session’s. The required gain was 1% originally, 1.7% by the mid-2000s and about 1.25% in recent descriptions. The attempt is void if the low is broken. The study’s figure is lower because its test is stricter: a signal counts only if the low it followed was never broken. By the looser test a bear rally that doubles and then fails still counts as a success.

Hanna also found that with a 1% threshold every rally since 1971 began with such a day, so the claim that no major rally starts without one held. Quantifiable Edges The study agrees in part: 28 of 36 turns had one within 15 sessions.

In the large declines

By the research file’s recoding, the S&P 500 gave 7 signals between March 2000 and October 2002 and 7 between October 2007 and March 2009. All 14 failed. In 2022 it gave 3 before the low, and all failed. On the Dow in 1929–32 Hanna counted 13 failures before the one that worked. The real lows of 1990, 2002, 2009, 2011, 2019, 2020, 2022 and 2025 each had a signal 4 to 11 sessions later.

What improved the odds, in Hanna’s tests

  • A shallower prior decline: 63.5% worked after declines of 5% or more, 44.4% after 12% or more.
  • Index above its 200-day average: 14 of 23 worked, against 23 of 48 below it.
  • Breadth on the signal day in the top 5% of the past year: 22 of 35 worked.
  • A close above the signal day’s close three sessions later: 27 of 46 worked, against 12 of 34.
  • The number of stocks at new highs on the day made no difference.
What to take from it
  • The signal was close to necessary and far from sufficient. Its absence was informative; its presence much less so.
  • It failed most where it was wanted most: deep declines below a falling 200-day average.
