2000: a top in two stages, with the Fed still raising rates

The S&P 500 fell 49.1% in 637 sessions, from 24 March 2000 to 9 October 2002. The Nasdaq Composite fell 77.9%. The first decline in spring 2000 was repaired by September. The decline that did not recover began only after a second peak on 1 September, when large technology companies started to warn about sales.

Three peaks in ten weeks

The Dow peaked first, on 14 January 2000. The Nasdaq Composite peaked on 10 March at 5,048.62; that day the Dow was already 15.3% below its own high. The S&P 500 peaked last, on 24 March at 1,527.46. AP market report, 10 Mar 2000 Money was leaving the old leaders for the Dow stocks and back again within days: on 15 and 16 March the Dow gained 819 points while the Nasdaq corrected.

The Federal Reserve was tightening throughout. It raised its policy rate on 2 February and 21 March 2000, and by 0.50 point to 6.50% on 16 May, eight weeks after the S&P peak. FOMC statement, 16 May 2000 The yield on 10-year Treasuries had been below the 2-year yield since 2 February (an inverted yield curve, which has often come before recessions).

The April 2000 crash

  • 3 April. Settlement talks in the Microsoft antitrust case collapsed. The Nasdaq fell 349 points (7.6%) while the Dow rose 300. AP, 3 Apr 2000
  • 4 April. The Nasdaq was down 13.6% during the day and closed down 1.8%.
  • 14 April. A strong consumer-price report raised fear of more rate rises, and forced selling by investors who had borrowed to buy shares (margin calls) added to it. The Nasdaq lost 9.7% on the day and about 25% on the week. The S&P 500 lost 5.8% and closed 11.1% below its peak, its first close 10% down. AP, 14 Apr 2000
  • The next two sessions brought the Nasdaq’s two largest point gains to that date (+6.6% and +7.2%).

Two well-known hedge funds were casualties from opposite sides. Tiger Management, which had bet against the bubble, closed to outside investors on 30 March. The managers of Soros’s Quantum fund, which had owned technology shares, left on 28 April. not verified beyond Wikipedia’s citations of the press.

The summer rally and the 1 September retest

The Nasdaq made its spring low on 23 May 2000 at 3,164.55, down 37.3%. Then it rose 35.1% in eight weeks, to 4,274.67 on 17 July. A soft jobs report on 2 June had raised hope that the Fed was finished. On 1 September the S&P 500 closed at 1,520.77, within 0.4% of its record. The Nasdaq that day was 16% below its own.

To this point company earnings had given no warning. The Fed’s July report said forecasts were still being revised up. Fed Monetary Policy Report, Jul 2000

Autumn 2000: the warnings

The second decline was led by news from the companies themselves. Intel warned on 21 September and fell 22% the next day on record volume. Apple, Dell, Home Depot and IBM followed within four weeks, and Nortel fell 29% on 25 October after a shortfall in sales of optical equipment. AP, 22 Sep 2000 AP, 25 Oct 2000 The disputed presidential election ran from 7 November to 12 December; the court ruling that ended it brought no relief rally.

The Fed held its rate at 6.50% and kept warning about inflation until 19 December, when it switched to warning about weakness without cutting. The Nasdaq fell 4.3% that day and 7.1% the next. It ended 2000 down 39%, its worst year.

What to take from it
  • The first 11% fall in the S&P 500 was fully repaired within five months. The top was a process, not a day.
  • The leading index broke first and made lower highs in July and September while the broad index returned to its record.
  • The lasting decline began with company warnings, six months after the price peak.

2001: eleven rate cuts, three large rallies, lower lows

The Fed cut its rate from 6.50% to 1.75% during 2001. Each burst of cuts produced a rally of about 20% in the S&P 500 and 40% in the Nasdaq. Each rally failed, because profits and business investment were still falling.

The surprise cut of 3 January

A factory survey on 2 January 2001 came in at its lowest since 1991 and the Nasdaq fell 7.2%. The next day, between scheduled meetings, the Fed cut by 0.50 point. The Nasdaq rose 14.2%, its largest one-day gain, and the S&P 500 rose 5.0% on record volume. FOMC statement, 3 Jan 2001 AP, 3 Jan 2001

The rally lasted three weeks. The Nasdaq peaked on 24 January, 24.8% above its 2 January close, and a second cut on 31 January was sold. Then the news that had started the decline resumed. Cisco missed its earnings forecast on 6 February for the first time in over six years. Nortel, Dell and Hewlett-Packard warned on 15 February. By 4 April the Nasdaq had fallen 42.7% from the January high and the S&P 500 19.1%.

The reason the cut did not hold was stated by the Fed itself in April: a persistent fall in current and expected company profits, and investment plans being postponed or cancelled. FOMC statement, 18 Apr 2001 Profits of non-financial companies were falling at an annual rate of about 45% in the last quarter of 2000. Fed Monetary Policy Report, Feb 2002

Spring rally, summer slide

Cisco warned again on 16 April and cut 8,500 jobs. Two days later the Fed made a second surprise cut. The S&P 500 rose 19.0% from 4 April to 21 May and the Nasdaq 41.2% to 22 May. Over the summer the rally faded as the expected recovery kept being pushed back. JDS Uniphase reported the largest write-down of acquired assets to that date on 26 July.

11 September

The attacks closed US markets for four trading days. They reopened on 17 September with a 0.50-point cut before the bell. The Dow lost 7.1% that day and 14.3% on the week, its largest weekly loss since 1940. The low came on 21 September: S&P 500 965.80. The VIX (an index of expected S&P 500 volatility taken from option prices) reached its highest intraday level of the whole bear market that day, 49.35.

From there the S&P 500 rose 21.4% to 4 January 2002 and the Nasdaq 44.7%. In the study’s count this is the largest failed rally inside any S&P 500 decline since 1950. The recession had started in March 2001 and ended in November 2001, though that was announced only later. NBER, 26 Nov 2001

What to take from it
  • Rate cuts produced three rallies of about 20% in the S&P 500 inside twelve months. All three ended at lower highs.
  • The rallies began on policy news. The declines resumed on company news.
  • The Fed’s cuts worked on housing and consumers; the recession lasted eight months. They did not restore technology spending or profits.

2002–03: a last leg with no recession, a low on earnings news, and a retest

The S&P 500 fell another 33.8% from 4 January to 9 October 2002 with the economy growing and the Fed’s rate unchanged. The cause was a collapse of trust in reported profits, at valuations that were still high. The low held on a run of earnings reports, and was tested once more in March 2003 before the advance began.

The accounting scandals

  • Enron filed for bankruptcy on 2 December 2001, the largest US filing to that date. Global Crossing followed in January 2002.
  • Tyco’s chief executive resigned on 3 June 2002; the stock fell 27% and the S&P 500 2.5%.
  • WorldCom disclosed $3.8bn of disguised expenses on 25 June 2002 and filed for bankruptcy on 21 July. Wikipedia, WorldCom scandal

July 2002: the panic

On 23 July 2002 the S&P 500 closed at 797.70; the Dow had fallen on eleven of the last twelve sessions. The next day brought arrests at Adelphia and agreement in Congress on an accounting-reform law; the S&P 500 rose 5.7% on record New York Stock Exchange volume. It rose another 5.4% on 29 July. The Sarbanes-Oxley Act was signed on 30 July. The rally reached 962.70 on 22 August, a gain of 20.7%, and then failed. AP, 24 Jul 2002

Volume and fear peaked in this stretch, not at the final low. The VIX made its highest close of the bear market, 45.08, on 5 August 2002.

9 October 2002: the low

The S&P 500 closed at 776.76 and the Nasdaq at 1,114.11. The VIX closed at 42.13, lower than in August. The turn came on earnings: Yahoo on 10 October, General Electric on 11 October, then Citigroup, General Motors and Johnson & Johnson on 15 October. The Dow gained 13.3% in four sessions. AP, 15 Oct 2002 By the study’s rule the follow-through day (a strong gain on higher volume four or more sessions after a low) came on the fourth session, and the index was back above its 50-day average with a gain of 13.4%.

The Fed cut by 0.50 point to 1.25% on 6 November, four weeks after the low. The first advance ended on 27 November at 938.87, up 20.9%.

March 2003: the retest and the turn

Through the winter the approach of war in Iraq weighed on prices. The S&P 500 closed at 800.73 on 11 March 2003, 3.1% above the October low. The Nasdaq was 14% above its October low and the semiconductor index 30% above. The index rose 3.5% on 13 March and 3.5% on 17 March, hours before the ultimatum to Iraq; the invasion began on 20 March. The S&P 500 closed above its 200-day average on 21 March 2003, 112 sessions after the low. The Fed’s last cut, to 1.00%, came on 25 June 2003.

What to take from it
  • The final leg had no macro cause: the recession had ended eleven months before the low.
  • Fear and volume peaked in July and August 2002. The October low came on less of both.
  • The low turned on company earnings, not on the Fed. On the way down the index had closed back above its 20-day average 37 times and above its 50-day average 19 times without the decline ending.
