The 200-day rule cut the worst losses and paid for it at every low

Holding stocks only above a long moving average has matched buy-and-hold on return with about half the worst drawdowns over a century. The price is a late re-entry: a median 10% above the low across all drops, about 18% in the large ones, and over 35% in 2009 and 2020. The first reclaim usually failed at least once.

What the long-run tests found

  • Faber’s ten-month rule (hold the S&P 500 when the month-end price is above its ten-month average, otherwise cash), 1901–2012: compound return 10.18% a year against 9.32% for buy-and-hold. The average annual returns were the same, 11.22% against 11.26%; the gain came from lower volatility. The rule was invested about 70% of the time, traded less than once a year, and trailed the index in about half of all years. Faber, 2013 update
  • Its worst drawdowns: 42% against 84% in 1929–32, 17% against 45% in 2000–02. It left the market on 31 December 2007 and missed the 51% fall.
  • On the Dow from 1897 to 1986, returns after moving-average buy signals were higher and steadier than after sell signals. Brock, Lakonishok & LeBaron, 1992 A later paper re-tested those rules with a correction for having searched many rules. Sullivan, Timmermann & White, 1999 Its finding that the best rules did not hold up in the following decade is not verified.
  • Between 60% and 80% of both the best and the worst days happened below the 200-day average. Faber, via CXO Advisory Of the 50 largest S&P 500 moves since 1997, 46 were below it. Batnick, 2019

  • The slowest reclaims of the 200-day: 126 sessions after the December 1987 low, 123 after 1957, 112 after October 2002.
  • The costliest: +39.4% off the low in 2009 (session 58) and +35.7% in 2020 (session 45).
  • By Faber’s month-end rule the re-entries were 12.6% to 36.1% above the low at the eleven major lows from 1987 to 2025, median about 18% (research file).
  • In 9 of those 11 the first close above the 200-day was lost again within 60 sessions.

Whipsaws on the way down

The index closed back above its 50-day average 19 times during the 2000–02 decline, 15 times in 2007–09, 11 times in 2015–16 and 6 times in 2022. It did so 0 times in 2020. Among the study’s 91 failed rallies, 61 closed above the 50-day average before failing and 37 closed above the 200-day. Among the 36 turns, 35 reclaimed both. Reclaiming the 200-day was necessary for a turn and still left roughly even odds: 35 turns against 37 failures.

Death cross and golden cross

A death cross is the 50-day average falling below the 200-day; a golden cross is the reverse. On the S&P 500 from 1950 to 2026 the research file counts 38 death crosses.

  • In 16 of the 38 the index fell less than 5% further before the next golden cross.
  • The cross came on the exact day of the low in July 2010, 11 sessions before it in December 2018, and after it in March 2020 and April 2025.
  • Three of the 18 since 1987 came before large further falls: 30 October 2000 (−44.5% more), 21 December 2007 (−54.4%) and 14 March 2022 (−14.3%). Those three were crosses that arrived 5–13% below the high after a slow roll-over. The harmless ones mostly arrived after a fast fall of 15% or more.

Shorter averages

The first close above the 20-day average came a median 4 sessions after the low in the study’s table. On the way down it happened up to 37 times in one decline. No tested evidence was found on a close above the 21-day average after an undercut as a signal. not verified

What to take from it
  • Below the 200-day average both tails were fat: the worst days, the best days and most failed rallies happened there.
  • The rule’s benefit came from the two or three long bear markets per generation. In V-shaped lows it was pure cost.
  • A death cross after a fast fall has been a late signal as often as an early one.
Limits The death-cross split into “slow” and “fast” rests on three bad cases. Siegel’s 200-day tests were not read.
