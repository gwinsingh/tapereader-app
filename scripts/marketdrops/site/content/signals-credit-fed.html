Credit spreads peaked with the stock market low more often than before it

A credit spread is the extra yield a corporate bond pays over a Treasury. Spreads widening means lenders are more afraid. In the declines studied, spreads usually peaked on or near the day stocks bottomed. They confirmed the low; they rarely announced it.

A Fed pivot marked the low when there was no recession, and did not when one was starting

A pivot is the Fed’s change from raising rates, or holding them, to cutting or pausing. In 1998 and 2018 the low came within seven sessions of it. In 2001 and 2007 stocks fell another 42% and 55% after the first cut.

Why the difference

  • 1998 and 2018. The problem was liquidity and positioning. Profits were flat to rising and no recession followed. Easier policy removed the cause.
  • 2001 and 2007. The cut answered a collapse in profits and credit that had only begun. Profits fell for two years. The cut did not remove the cause; it was a sign of it.
  • 2020. Rate cuts did nothing for three weeks. The low came when the Fed backed corporate credit directly.
  • 1994, 2004, 2006, 2022. Stocks bottomed while increases were still under way, before any pivot.

This reading is an inference from nine cases. A statement that can be tested: a pivot was followed by a low within weeks when earnings estimates were not falling and credit spreads had stopped widening.

Lows against the business cycle

Six of seven recession lows came before the recession ended: May 1970 (six months before), October 1974 (five), August 1982 (three), October 1990 (five), March 2009 (three), March 2020 (one). The exception was October 2002, eleven months after the recession ended. Earnings troughed after the price low in 1990–91, 2009, 2020 and 2022–23. News on jobs and profits was still getting worse when stocks turned.

What to take from it
  • The first cut was a reliable marker only in declines without a recession.
  • In recessions the low came about three to six months before the economy turned, with spreads at or just past their peak.
  • Spreads that were still widening argued against a low holding.
