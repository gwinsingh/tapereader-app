Earnings are strong and still being revised up; the fall so far is all valuation

S&P 500 profits grew about 33% in the third quarter of 2026 and analysts are still raising their estimates. Since 30 September the index price has fallen 6.2% while the earnings estimate for the next twelve months has risen 4.4%. What changed is the price investors pay for those earnings.

Working from FactSet’s figures, the forward P/E was about 19.4 at the 6 October record close and about 16.5 at the 20 November close. That is a fall of roughly 15% in the multiple in 33 sessions with no fall in estimates.

Three details behind the headline

  • The growth is concentrated. Energy earnings rose 151% because oil averaged $85.68 in the quarter against $64.97 a year earlier. Technology earnings rose 69%, and 133% in semiconductors. Without semiconductors, technology grew 30%. Financials grew 6.4%.
  • The largest companies now grow more slowly than the rest. The “Magnificent 7” grew earnings 27.0% in the quarter against about 33–35% for the other 493 companies, the first such gap since Q4 2022. FactSet expects the same in Q4 (22.6% against 30.8%). Meta booked a $9.9bn legal charge and Alphabet an $8.7bn loss on investments.
  • The estimates flatten in mid-2027. Expected growth drops to 3.6% in the second quarter of 2027, when this year’s energy and chip profits become the comparison.

A slower-moving measure tells a less comfortable story. The CAPE (price divided by the ten-year average of inflation-adjusted earnings) was 40.6 at the October peak and is 37.9 now, against 32.6 at the September 2018 peak and 43.2 at the 2000 peak, by the study’s macro sheet. The Fed’s November report says equity valuations “remained elevated” and that the extra return investors receive for holding stocks over bonds is near the low end of its historical range Fed Financial Stability Report, 13 Nov 2026.

What to take from it
  • Nothing in reported earnings or in analyst estimates had turned down by 20 November.
  • On next-year earnings the index is cheaper than its ten-year average. On ten-year earnings it was, at the October peak, more expensive than at any past peak in the study except 1999 and 2000.

AI spending now exceeds the cash the spenders generate

The largest technology companies are spending more on data centres than their businesses throw off, and are raising debt and equity to fill the gap. Smaller data-centre operators depend on credit that became scarcer and dearer as rates rose. The October–November earnings reports made this visible, and the stocks were sold on it.

What the Fed counted

The Fed’s November Financial Stability Report has a box on leverage in the data-centre build-out Fed, 13 Nov 2026:

  • Debt issued to finance AI computing passed $680bn in 2026 by October, about five times the $130bn of all of 2023.
  • It was about 31% of all investment-grade company borrowing in 2026, up from under 6% in 2023.
  • More than $1.1tn of such public debt is outstanding. Private loans, leases and guarantees come on top and are hard to measure.
  • Measured against the size of the economy, spending is tracking the telecom build-out that began in 1995 and may soon exceed it.

The minutes of the October meeting add that most officials saw this debt growing through complex and opaque financing, and that many saw lower AI share prices as a form of market discipline Fed minutes, 18 Nov 2026.

The weakest link: rented computing

“Neoclouds” are companies that borrow to buy AI chips and rent out the computing power. Their shares fell most. From 6 October to 20 November: IREN −71%, Nebius −68%, CoreWeave −57%, Applied Digital −55%.

  • The cost of insuring CoreWeave’s debt against default hit a record on 5 November, and its bonds yielded more than 16% Reuters, 5 Nov 2026.
  • Its interest bill was about four times its operating profit. Nebius plans to spend more than three times its expected revenue in 2027 Capex Cat, 13 Nov 2026.
  • Lenders cut the share of a chip’s value they will lend against from about 80% to 50–60% Strategitz, 19 Nov 2026.

New shares to absorb

US share sales by newly listed companies raised $230.3bn in 2026 to 19 November, more than six times the year before, on 36% fewer deals CNBC, 19 Nov 2026. SpaceX listed in June. Anthropic raised $81.3bn on 19 November, the largest sale on record, and its filing lists computing commitments of about $269bn to three cloud providers Capex Cat, 27 Oct 2026. The June chip rout came a week before the first sale; the 19 November reversal began at the first trade of the second. A fund of recent listings (IPO) fell 24.8% from 6 October to 20 November.

What to take from it
  • Demand for AI chips had not weakened in any reported number. The questions raised were who pays, with what money, and at what interest rate.
  • The stocks that fell most were those furthest from self-funding.
  • Two record share sales in one year each coincided with a break in the leading group.

November 2026 against autumn 2018: the same shape, a different Fed problem

By the study’s macro measure, September 2018 is the closest past peak to October 2026, followed by January 2018 and 2000. The chart shapes are alike. The main difference is inflation: about 2% and falling then, 3.7% and rising now, which makes a Fed retreat much harder.

Main similarities

  1. A Fed that is raising rates, a chair who avoids guiding markets, and a president attacking the Fed in public.
  2. A record high in early autumn, a fall led by technology, and a midterm election in the middle of it.
  3. Long-term yields at multi-year highs within days of the top.
  4. Strong earnings and GDP growth of 3.4% while prices fell: a fall in valuation, not in profits.
  5. A December Fed meeting with a rise expected, and a possible government shutdown in December.

Main differences

  1. Inflation. In 2018 it was near target and falling with oil. In 2026 it is 3.7% and rising, and core inflation is above 3%. Almost all officials expect further rises; two voted to raise in October. On 20 November, the day of the 3.70% fall, a governor called financial conditions “too accommodative”.
  2. Stage of the cycle. Late in 2018; the first rise in 2026.
  3. Oil. Falling throughout the 2018 drop. War-driven in 2026, though down about 20% in three weeks.
  4. The Fed’s balance sheet. Shrinking in 2018, not in 2026.
  5. A financed spending boom. 2018 had none. 2026 has the AI build-out described above, which is the feature shared with 2000.
  6. Leverage. Margin debt is 2.3% of GDP against 1.4%, and the Fed reports record hedge-fund leverage.
  7. Starting valuation. Higher in 2026 on every measure in the table.
  8. Estimates. Rising now; they were being cut in late 2018 not verified.
What to take from it
  • The 2018 fall ended when the Fed changed course. The conditions that allowed that change, low inflation and falling oil, are only partly present in 2026.
  • On the items where 2026 differs from 2018, it more often resembles 2000: valuation, leverage and a debt-funded investment boom.
