Off a low the hardest-hit stocks rose most; the lasting leaders showed themselves later

Two views conflict. Growth-stock practice says the first stocks to reach new highs after a low are the new leaders. Academic work on momentum says the opposite happens in the first weeks: the stocks that fell most rebound hardest. The study’s stock data side with the second view for the first month and give the first view only mixed support.

The practitioner view

William O’Neil’s rule is that the first stocks to break out of sound price bases to new highs in the weeks after a follow-through day (a strong gain on higher volume, four or more sessions after a low) tend to lead the next advance, and that their relative-strength lines (the stock’s price divided by the index) often reach new highs before the price does. The source is his book How to Make Money in Stocks; no independent test was found. not verified One related test was negative: the share of NYSE stocks at 52-week highs on the signal day was the same before rallies that worked (2.1%) and rallies that failed (2.0%). Quantifiable Edges, 7 Feb 2008

The momentum-crash evidence

A momentum strategy buys the past year’s winners and sells its losers. Daniel and Moskowitz showed that its worst losses come after market declines, when volatility is high, as the market rebounds. Daniel & Moskowitz, 2014

+163%past losers, March–May 2009
+8%past winners, March–May 2009
+232%past losers, July–August 1932
+32%past winners, July–August 1932

The market itself rose 26% in the 2009 window and 82% in the 1932 one. In March 2009 the loser group was down 84% from its peak on average and held banks and carmakers; the winner group held defensive companies. The authors’ description: after a bear market, beaten-down and indebted stocks behave like options on a recovery.

  • The hardest-hit fifth led over the first 21 sessions at seven of the eight lows, by a wide margin. The exception was 2022, the slow bear market without a panic.
  • Stocks that made a new high within 30 sessions of the low did better than the rest over the next year at three of eight lows (February 2018, December 2018, 2022) and worse at five (2002, 2011, 2016, 2020, 2025).
  • Stocks that had been strongest in the year before the drop beat the weakest over the next year at five of nine lows. Prior leaders were neither reliably finished nor reliably back.
  • In the multi-leg declines 38–51% of stocks made their own low ten or more sessions before the index (2002, 2009, 2011, 2016, 2022). In the fast shocks almost none did: 0.2% in 2020, 6.5% in 2025.

First weeks against first months

  • First weeks. The rebound was led by what had fallen most: energy and miners in 2016, energy and homebuilders in 2020, banks in 2009. A screen for relative strength run in the first month mostly found defensive stocks.
  • First months. Groups making new highs two to three months after the low were often the durable leaders: banks and semiconductors in 2016, semiconductors and large technology in 2023. This is O’Neil’s observation on a slower clock.
  • After a short shock inside a rising market (1998, 2025) the earlier leaders simply resumed: internet stocks in 1998, semiconductors in 2025.
What to take from it
  • “Strongest stocks” and “most beaten-down stocks” were different lists at a low, and the second list won the first month in seven of eight cases.
  • The first-new-highs rule was not confirmed as a general rule in this data; it may hold for the narrower set of growth stocks it was written for.
  • Whether old leaders returned depended on the decline: yes after outside shocks, no for energy after 2022.
Limits The study’s stock list is today’s index members plus past leaders that still trade. Companies that failed are missing, which flatters the rebound of the hardest-hit group. Medians hide wide ranges. Eight or nine lows.
