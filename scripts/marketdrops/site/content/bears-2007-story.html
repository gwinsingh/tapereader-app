2007: credit broke in the summer, the index peaked in October

The S&P 500 fell 56.8% in 355 sessions, from 9 October 2007 to 9 March 2009. The trouble started in mortgage credit eight months before the index peak. Two ordinary-looking corrections came and went in 2007, and the Federal Reserve was already cutting rates when the index made its high.

The tremors: February and the summer

  • February 2007. HSBC warned on 7–8 February of losses on US subprime mortgages (loans to weaker borrowers); the market ignored it. On 27 February the S&P 500 fell 3.5% after a plunge in Shanghai. It lost 5.9% in all and was at a new high by mid-April.
  • March–May. The Fed chairman told Congress on 28 March that the subprime problem seemed likely to be contained. Bernanke testimony, 28 Mar 2007
  • June–July. Two Bear Stearns hedge funds holding mortgage securities failed: redemptions frozen on 7 June, a letter on 17 July saying almost nothing was left, bankruptcy on 31 July. Lenders had demanded more collateral and nobody would buy the assets. Financial Crisis Inquiry Commission report, p. 240
  • 19 July. The Dow closed above 14,000 for the first time. The Russell 2000 index of small companies had peaked on 13 July and the Dow Transports peaked that day; neither made a new high in October.

August 2007: the first credit seizure

On 9 August the French bank BNP Paribas froze three funds because their mortgage securities could not be priced. The European Central Bank lent about €95bn overnight. In the same week, funds that held shares long and short by computer models (quant funds) suffered sudden losses as they all sold the same positions: a simulated strategy of this kind lost 6.85% in three days before leverage. Khandani and Lo, 2007 The S&P 500 fell 9.4% from 19 July to 15 August on closing prices.

The Fed cut its discount rate (the rate on its direct loans to banks) on 17 August and its main policy rate by 0.50 point on 18 September. The index rose 2.9% that day and went on to a record close of 1,565.15 on 9 October 2007.

October–December 2007: the peak and the first 10%

The peak came with the Fed easing and with credit unrepaired. Merrill Lynch raised its third-quarter loss to $8.4bn on 24 October; the chief executives of Merrill and Citigroup left within a week of each other. The S&P 500 was 10.1% below its peak on 26 November. Speeches by two Fed officials on 28 and 29 November promised flexibility, and the index rose 7.7% in two weeks. A rate cut of only 0.25 point on 11 December disappointed: −2.5% that day. FOMC minutes, 11 Dec 2007

Stress between banks was worse in late November than in August. The TED spread (the gap between the rate banks charge each other and the Treasury-bill rate) was 1.29 points at the October peak and 1.99 on 26 November. Short-term company IOUs backed by assets (asset-backed commercial paper) shrank from $1,226bn on 8 August to $775bn at year-end.

What to take from it
  • Two corrections in 2007 (−5.9% and −9.4%) recovered to new highs in seven to eight weeks. Depth did not mark the one that mattered.
  • Financials, homebuilders, small companies and transports peaked months before the index.
  • The index peaked three weeks after the first rate cut.

2008: rescues, rallies, then the autumn crash

The Fed cut its rate from 4.25% to 2.00% between January and April 2008 and rescued Bear Stearns in March. The index rallied 12% and then fell through the summer as oil reached $147. After Lehman Brothers failed on 15 September, the S&P 500 lost 32% in six weeks (12 September to 27 October).

January to May: emergency cut, Bear Stearns, the spring rally

  • 22 January. After a worldwide sell-off on a US holiday, made worse by a French bank unwinding a rogue trader’s positions, the Fed cut by 0.75 point between meetings. The index made an intraday low on 23 January, 18.9% below the peak, and reversed. FOMC minutes, 30 Jan 2008
  • 11 March. The Fed offered to lend $200bn of Treasuries against mortgage bonds; S&P 500 +3.7%.
  • 14–17 March. Bear Stearns ran out of cash in four days as lenders and hedge-fund clients withdrew. JPMorgan agreed to buy it with Fed support, first at $2 a share, later $10. The Fed cut by another 0.75 point on 18 March; S&P 500 +4.2%.
  • 19 May. The rally peaked at 1,426.63, up 12.0% from the 10 March close and 8.9% below the record. It ended just under a falling 200-day average.

Summer: oil, the mortgage agencies, a first 20% loss

Oil peaked at $147.27 on 11 July 2008. Energy and materials shares had led the market until 20 May and then fell hard. The S&P 500 first closed 20% below its peak on 9 July. Worry moved to Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac, the two government-sponsored mortgage companies; the government took control of both on Sunday 7 September. St. Louis Fed crisis timeline

September–October: Lehman and the crash

  • 15 September. Lehman Brothers filed for bankruptcy; Merrill Lynch was sold to Bank of America. S&P 500 −4.7%.
  • 16–17 September. The Fed lent $85bn to the insurer AIG. A large money-market fund fell below $1 a share, and prime money-market funds lost $349bn in a week. Hedge funds asked to withdraw $32bn from Morgan Stanley in one day.
  • 18–19 September. A ban on short selling of financial shares and the first outline of a bank rescue plan: +4.3% and +4.0%. SEC, 19 Sep 2008
  • 29 September. The House of Representatives rejected the rescue plan (TARP). S&P 500 −8.8%, Dow −777.68.
  • 3 October. TARP passed; the index fell 1.4% that day.
  • 6–10 October. The index lost 18.2% in five sessions. The intraday low on 10 October was 839.80, on record New York Stock Exchange volume. The TED spread reached a record 4.58.
  • 13 October. Governments announced they would put capital directly into banks: +11.6%, the largest one-day gain since at least 1950. Two days later −9.0%.

November: the fear peak

After the election rally (+18.5% from 27 October to 4 November) the index fell 25.2% in twelve sessions. On 20 November 2008 it closed at 752.44 and the VIX closed at 80.86, its highest close until March 2020. Citigroup shares had fallen 23% and 26% on the last two days. Over the weekend the government guaranteed $306bn of Citigroup’s assets, and on 25 November the Fed announced it would buy up to $600bn of mortgage-agency debt and mortgage bonds, the start of what became known as quantitative easing. Fed, 25 Nov 2008 The rate on 30-year mortgages fell from 6.04% to 5.10% by year-end: the first time in the crisis that policy reached borrowers.

What to take from it
  • Rescues and rate cuts set off rallies of 7% to 19%; each failed while bank losses kept growing.
  • Approval of a rescue was sold more than once (3 October 2008; 10 and 17 February 2009).
  • Fear, volume and bank-to-bank stress peaked in October and November 2008, months before the final low.

2009: a last leg in banks, and a low with no retest

The index rose 24.2% from 20 November 2008 to 6 January 2009 and then fell 27.6% to 9 March. The last leg was concentrated in banks, industrial companies and the defensive groups that had held up before. Technology, emerging markets, commodities and company bonds did not make new lows.

January and February: the rally fails

The Fed cut its rate to a range of 0–0.25% on 16 December 2008. The rally that followed never closed above the 200-day average. Bank of America needed a second rescue on 16 January. The index fell 5.3% on 20 January, inauguration day, 4.9% on 10 February when the new Treasury Secretary presented a bank plan without detail, and 4.6% on 17 February when the fiscal stimulus was signed. AIG reported a loss of $99.3bn for 2008 on 2 March.

6–9 March 2009: the low

The intraday low was 666.79 on 6 March and the closing low 676.53 on 9 March. The VIX closed at 49.68, far below November’s 80.86. A survey of individual investors on 5 March showed 70.3% expecting lower prices, the highest reading in its history. AAII sentiment survey The Nasdaq 100 closed on 9 March slightly above its 20 November close.

What changed in the next two weeks

  • 10 March. Citigroup’s chief executive told staff the bank had been profitable in January and February. S&P 500 +6.4%. The Fed chairman spoke the same day about reviewing accounting rules that forced banks to mark assets to distressed prices. Bernanke, 10 Mar 2009
  • 12 March. Bank of America said the same about its profits. +4.1%.
  • 18 March. The Fed enlarged its purchases: $750bn more mortgage bonds and $300bn of Treasuries. +2.1%. Fed, 18 Mar 2009
  • 23 March. A Treasury programme to buy troubled assets with private partners. +7.1%.
  • 2 April. Accounting rules for hard-to-price assets were eased.
  • 7 May. Results of the bank stress tests: ten of nineteen banks had to raise $74.6bn in total. Fed, 7 May 2009

The index was 23.1% above its low after thirteen sessions and never came back. By the study’s rule the follow-through day came on the seventh session; the 50-day average was regained on the tenth with a gain of 21.6%, and the 200-day average after 58 sessions, on 1 June 2009, with a gain of 39.4%. The recession ended in June 2009. NBER, 20 Sep 2010

What to take from it
  • The final low came on lower fear and lower volume than October and November 2008, with technology and emerging markets holding above their earlier lows.
  • What ended the decline was bank capital, guarantees, asset purchases and accounting relief, plus two banks saying they were making money. Rate cuts had run out three months earlier.
  • On the way down the index had closed back above its 20-day average 31 times and above its 50-day average 15 times.
