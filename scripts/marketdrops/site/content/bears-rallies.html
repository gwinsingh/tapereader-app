Inside the two bears: frequent, violent rallies that began on policy and ended on earnings

The study counts 15 failed rallies of 5% or more in the S&P 500 in each bear, and 40 in the Nasdaq Composite in 2000–02. The largest gained 21% in the S&P 500 and 45% in the Nasdaq. Most began on a Federal Reserve move or a rescue. Each failed while company profits were still falling or bank losses still growing.

What they looked like

  • Sharper than anything in a rising market. Ten of the Nasdaq Composite’s twenty largest one-day gains since 1971 fall inside the 2000–02 bear, including +14.2% on 3 January 2001. Seven of the S&P 500’s twenty largest since 1950 fall inside the 2007–09 bear, including +11.6% on 13 October 2008.
  • Big up days were a mark of the bear itself. The S&P 500 gained 3% or more on 17 days in 2000–02 and on 23 days in 2007–09. In the whole advance from March 2003 to October 2007 it did so twice.
  • Short in a crisis. The ten S&P 500 rallies of 7% or more in 2007–09 lasted a median of six sessions. One lasted a single day.
  • Led by what had fallen most. This was true of the failures and also of the real low: in the three months after 9 March 2009 the financial sector fund doubled while consumer staples gained 23%.

The 200-day average as a ceiling

Once the 200-day average had turned down, every failed rally ended below it or no more than 2% above it. In May 2001, August 2002, May 2008, August 2008 and January 2009 the index never closed above it at all. But the first rally off the true low of October 2002 also stayed below it throughout. Both real advances were confirmed late by this measure: after 112 sessions in 2003 and 58 in 2009.

What published research adds

Goldman Sachs counted 19 bear-market rallies in world shares since the 1980s, lasting 44 days and gaining 10–15% on average, and explained them by thin positioning. CoinDesk report of the Goldman note, 10 Apr 2025

What to take from it
  • A rally of 20% did not end either bear. Four rallies of 19% to 24% failed in the S&P 500 between 2001 and 2009.
  • The triggers were policy actions. The real lows turned on earnings (October 2002) and on banks reporting profits (March 2009).
  • Size, speed and leadership by beaten-down shares were the same in the failures and in the real turn.
