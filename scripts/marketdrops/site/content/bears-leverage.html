Borrowed money peaked with or before the index, and forced selling marked the worst weeks

Margin debt (money investors borrow from brokers against shares) rose 54% in the five months to March 2000 and peaked in the month of the index peak. In 2007 it peaked in July, three months before the index. The 2007–09 bear added leverage of a different order: investment banks with up to 40 dollars of assets per dollar of capital, funded overnight.

2007: leverage in the lenders themselves

  • The five large investment banks ran assets of up to 40 times capital in 2007. Bear Stearns had $11.8bn of equity against $383.6bn of liabilities and borrowed up to $70bn overnight. Goldman Sachs went from 17:1 in 2000 to 32:1 in 2007. The two mortgage agencies, Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac, stood at 75:1. Financial Crisis Inquiry Commission report
  • The funding was short-term. Overnight loans against securities (repo) cleared through two banks grew from $800bn in 2002 to $2.8trn in early 2008.

On the day the index was back at its old high (28 March 2013) the 2× fund was still 20% below its starting value: a fund that resets its leverage daily loses ground in a volatile decline.

What to take from it
  • Margin debt rose fastest just before each peak and turned down with or before the index.
  • In 2007–09 the decisive leverage was inside banks and funds, and it was unwound by lenders, not by choice.
  • A 2× fund lost 85% in a 55% bear. Its 49% gain in the largest rally was gone within two months.
  • In 2026 the Fed’s margin-loan series stood at 2.28% of GDP in the second quarter, up 47% in a year; the comparable reading was 2.64% in early 2000.
Limits Margin statistics are published weeks late and also fell in corrections (−14% from July to October 1998). No three-times fund existed for the first thirteen months of the 2007–09 bear, so its full path cannot be observed.
