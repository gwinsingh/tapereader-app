The VIX usually peaked before price did, and its level did not say what kind of decline it was

The VIX is the index of expected S&P 500 volatility over the next 30 days, taken from option prices. In multi-leg declines its highest close came one to seven months before the final price low. In single-shock declines the two coincided. The level reached did not separate corrections from bear markets.

Peak before the low

Of the 15 completed drops of 10% or more that began in 1990 or later in the study’s table, the VIX made its highest close 30 or more sessions before the final price low in seven: 1990 (34 sessions), 2000–02 (46), 2007–09 (72), 2010 (30), 2011 (39), 2015–16 (118) and 2022 (about 155). In each the final low came at a lower VIX: 49.7 on 9 March 2009 against 80.9 in November 2008, and 28.1 on 11 February 2016 against 40.7 in August 2015.

In seven others the peak fell within five sessions of the low: 1997, 1999, February 2018, December 2018, 2020, 2023 and 2025. In 1998 the highest reading came at the retest, after the closing low.

The usual reading of a lower VIX at a lower price is that forced selling is fading. The study’s rally table adds a caution. Among lows that undercut an earlier low, the VIX peak was lower than at the earlier low in 6 of 12 turns and also in 12 of 29 failed rallies. It described the type of decline. It did not identify the last low.

A 10% correction in 2018 and a 25% bear market in 2022 reached the same level. The 49% fall of 2000–02 peaked lower than the 19% fall of 2011. The VIX measures the speed of a decline more than its size. The 2022 bear market never closed above 36.5; a trader waiting for 45 as a sign of capitulation did not get it.

The curve: VIX above three-month VIX

Normally the three-month measure is higher than the 30-day one. When the 30-day VIX closes above it, the curve is inverted: more fear is priced now than later.

  • Inversion began before the low: 5 inverted sessions before it in February 2018, 15 to 27 before the lows of 2010, 2015–16, 2018, 2020 and 2025, 42 in 2011 and 153 in 2007–09.
  • The ratio’s highest reading came before the price low in most cases: 1.43 on 24 October 2008, 1.26 on 8 August 2011, 1.34 on 28 February 2020.
  • After the low the curve returned to normal within 0 to 9 sessions in seven of nine cases. It took 12 sessions in 2025 and 23 in 2020.
  • In 2022 the ratio never exceeded 1.02. In the 10.3% fall of 2023 it never inverted.

A separate calculation in the research file, on Cboe closes from September 2009 to November 2026: the curve was inverted on 7.6% of sessions. The S&P 500’s average gain over the following 21 sessions was 3.0% (72% of cases positive), against 1.1% (67%) for all sessions. Over 63 sessions it was 7.3% against 3.1%. Those inverted days come from about eight episodes, so the effective sample is small.

What published work says

  • From 1989 to 1999, high readings of the VIX, the put/call ratio and the Arms index were followed by above-average S&P futures returns over 10 to 30 days. Simon & Wiggins, 2001
  • Expected returns do not rise in proportion to volatility, so portfolios that take less risk when volatility is high earned better risk-adjusted returns. Moreira & Muir, 2017
  • The slope of the VIX curve predicts returns on volatility products themselves. Johnson, 2017 That is not evidence about the date of a stock-market low.

Rules of the form “VIX spikes, then closes back below a level”, and the volatility of the VIX itself, could not be checked against a tested study. not verified

What to take from it
  • In a decline with several legs, the highest fear came early. The final low was quieter.
  • An inverted curve marked the zone in which lows formed, and in which the largest down days also happened. It appeared weeks before the low.
  • Slow, policy-driven bear markets (2022) gave no volatility signal at all.
Limits Fifteen declines since 1990; the three-month series starts in 2007. The forward-return figures use overlapping days.
